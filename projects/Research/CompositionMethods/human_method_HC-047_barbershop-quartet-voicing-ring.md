## Description

Barbershop harmony is a uniquely American a cappella vocal craft in which four singers (Tenor, Lead, Baritone, Bass) perform a melody with close-position, predominantly homophonic four-part harmony. The defining craft goal is the **"ring"** — an acoustic overtone phenomenon (a "phantom 5th voice") produced when all four voices lock into just-intonation tuning on dominant seventh chords and other expanded-sound sonorities. The method governs how an arranger or woodshedding singer distributes chord tones across four voices to maximize overtone reinforcement, maintain voice-leading economy, and drive harmonic momentum through the circle of fifths.

## Craft Procedure (how the human does it, step by step)

### Phase 1: Song selection & skeleton
1. **Pick a song with strong harmonic structure** — dominant 7ths, tritones, circle-of-fifths progression, a memorable hook. The song is ~80% of the arrangement.
2. **Extract the melody** (the "tune") — barbershop places melody in the **Lead** (second-highest voice, comfortable mid-range), not the top voice.
3. **Notate the melodic rhythm and phrasing** — barbershop is rhythmically straightforward; rhythmic interest comes from syncopated harmonic rhythm and the tag.

### Phase 2: Harmonic grid & chord assignment
4. **Write the harmonic grid** — label each beat's chord (dom7 + circle-of-fifths emphasis). The **barbershop seventh** (root–M3–P5–m7) should constitute ≈30% of chords for contest-appropriate repertoire.
5. **Assign chord tones to four voices** using these voicing rules:
   - **Bass** gets the **root or 5th** of each chord almost always (occasionally 3rd in a "scissor" move). Avoid 1st/3rd inversion as default. Never leave the bass an octave or more separated from the next voice ("divorced bass") — that kills the ring.
   - **Tenor** (falsetto, highest voice) sings **above the Lead**, typically chord 5th, major 3rd, or 7th. Tenor lines are the simplest — stepwise, conjunct, minimal rhythmic interest.
   - **Baritone** fills the **tricky middle** — moving above and below the Lead, completing the chord wherever the Tenor and Bass leave a gap. Baritone must **never double the Lead** (most common beginner mistake).
   - **Lead** carries the melody. Chord tones below Lead are supplied by Bass/Baritone; above Lead by Tenor.
6. **Check completeness** — every chord must be a full 4-note voicing with **no octave doubling** of any pitch class (except the tonic in final cadence). If two voices share a note, you've orphaned a chord tone.

### Phase 3: Voice-leading & motion economy
7. **Apply the woodshedding rule**: "Don't move unless you have to (or if the melody takes your note), and if you must move, move the smallest distance you need to move to complete the chord." This means:
   - Common-tone retention wherever possible.
   - Stepwise motion where change is forced.
   - Only Bass regularly makes leaps (roots & fifths).
8. **Prioritize circle-of-fifths root movement** — the "Does it circle?" test. The strongest barbershop progressions move by descending 5th (V→I, ii→V, vi→ii). The tag (final cadence flourish) almost always chains a circle progression into the final held dom7→I.
9. **Engineer the "scissor" move** — Bass and Tenor move in contrary motion: Bass ascends while Tenor descends (or vice versa), creating a visual "scissor" that audibly expands/contracts the chord. This is the single most characteristic barbershop gesture.

### Phase 4: The tag (signature craft element)
10. **Compose the tag** — a 4–16 bar coda appended after the last chorus. The tag is where barbershop craft concentrates:
    - Circle-of-fifths chain (ii→V→I, often extended vi→ii→V→I or iii→vi→ii→V→I)
    - Rising register in all parts
    - Stretch the final dominant 7th with a fermata (the "ring" target)
    - Bass holds the root; Tenor climbs to the 7th or 5th; Lead holds the 3rd; Baritone fills the 5th or 7th
    - Resolve to tonic, often with a final "chord of the barbershop seventh" voicing

### Phase 5: Tuning & ring optimization
11. **Tune chords to just intonation** — especially the dominant seventh:
    - Root: reference pitch
    - Major 3rd: lowered ~14 cents from equal temperament (5:4 ratio)
    - Perfect 5th: pure (3:2 ratio)
    - Minor 7th: lowered ~31 cents (16:9 or 7:4 ratio, historically the "barbershop 7th" favors the 7:4 harmonic seventh)
    - The **10th spacing rule**: optimum ring occurs when Tenor and Lead are a 10th apart (octave + 3rd), and Lead and Bass are also about a 10th apart. This distributes partials evenly across the harmonic series, maximizing overtone reinforcement at partials 2–6.
12. **Test by singing** — an arranger must be able to sing all four parts. If the arrangement can't be sung by a human, it's not barbershop.

### Phase 6: Form
13. **Set the form** — barbershop is strophic (verse-chorus or AABA). The arrangement arc:
    - Pickup (first 4 bars, establish key)
    - Chorus 1 (plain, establish melody)
    - Chorus 2 (slightly more ornamented, Baritone may vary)
    - Chorus 3 (key change or "release" section, often subdominant)
    - Tag (coda, the craft peak)
14. **No instruments** — pure a cappella. Vocal percussion is not traditional (though modern groups occasionally use it).

## Practitioner Examples

- **The Mills Brothers** (1930s–40s): The most commercially successful barbershop-adjacent quartet. Mastered the close-harmony lock without microphones, using one voice per syllable. "Paper Doll" (1943) defined the sound for a generation.
- **The Dapper Dans** (Disneyland's house quartet, 1960s–present): Continuous live performance tradition; their repertoire spans traditional barbershop through contemporary "barberpop."
- **Vocal Majority** (Dallas, 1972–present): 150-voice chorus, multiple international gold medals. Their arrangements (e.g. "The Sound of Silence," "Come Fly With Me") demonstrate choral-scale barbershop voicing.
- **Gas House Gang** (St. Louis, 1986 gold medal quartet): Known for theatrical comedy and tight ring. Arrangements by Jim Clancy and Joe Connelly.
- **Freddie King's Four Freshmen** (not technically barbershop but the same lock-and-ring voicing approach applied to jazz harmony).
- **The Delta Four** (earliest recordings of barbershop form, 1890s–1910s): African-American originators, woodshedding ear-singers.
- **Dr. David Wright** (historian of barbershop, author of "The African-American Origins of Barbershop Music"): Definitive scholarship on the craft's roots.
- **Adam Scott, Don Gray, Walter Latzko, Dave Stephens**: Notable 20th/21st c. arrangers who codified the harmonic/voicing rules (Stephens' "What Are We Trying to Preserve?" is a masterclass).

### Rules (encoding the barbershop voicing grammar)

1. **Voicing completeness**: Every chord = 4 distinct pitch classes. Zero octave doubling between voices (except final tonic chord).
2. **Bass-root default**: Bass on root or 5th, ≥85% of beats. Inversions allowed only for scissor moves or approach to cadence.
3. **No baritone-lead unison**: Baritone pitch ≠ Lead pitch at any point. (Baritone may cross above Lead momentarily but never sing the same note.)
4. **No divorced bass**: Max vertical gap from Bass to next voice (Baritone) ≤ 12th. If >12th, redistribute chord tones.
5. **10th spacing target**: Tenor to Lead interval = 10th (octave + 3rd). Lead to Bass similarly ≈10th. This distributes the harmonic series optimally for ring.
6. **Just intonation tuning**: M3 = 5:4 (−14¢), P5 = 3:2 (0¢), m7 = 7:4 or 16:9 (−31¢ or −5¢). Equal-temperament tuning kills the ring — the "phantom 5th" vanishes.
7. **Circle-of-fifths dominance**: ≥70% of chord roots move by descending 5th (ii→V→I chain). Predominant chords (ii, vi) lead to V; V resolves to I.
8. **Barbershop-seventh density**: ≥30% of chords must be dominant 7th (root–M3–P5–m7) for contest-eligible repertoire.
9. **Common-tone retention**: Each voice holds its pitch for as many chords as possible; only moves when the chord changes force it.
10. **Tag escalation**: Tag section = monotonic register rise across all voices, accelerating harmonic rhythm (2-beat chords → 1-beat → final held), culminating in fermata on dom7 → resolution.
11. **Stepwise tenor**: Tenor moves by step (diatonic, ≤whole tone). Leaps in Tenor are exceptional and produce a special color.
12. **A cappella only**: No instrumental doubling or accompaniment. Pure vocal source.

## Quirks & Pitfalls

- **The barbershop "ring" is fragile**: If any voice is even slightly out of just intonation, the phantom 5th overtone collapses. This is why barbershop quartets spend more time tuning single chords in rehearsal than running the song.
- **Novice arrangers double Lead with Baritone** constantly — the ranges are similar and it's the easiest mistake. It weakens the chord and kills the ring.
- **"Divorced bass"** is the second most common error: leaving the bass isolated an octave+ below everyone else. The upper 3 voices may ring beautifully among themselves, but the composite sound thins out.
- **The scissor is overused by beginners** — it's characteristic but predictable if used every cadence.
- **Pitch drift in a cappella**: Without instrumental reference, groups tend to drift flat, especially in the tag's held dominant 7th. Professional quartets internally calibrate to just intonation so the final V→I stays on pitch.
- **Repertoire limitation**: The style works best on songs with strong circle-of-fifths harmony. Modal, free-jazz, or atonal material cannot be "barbershop-ified" without losing the identity of the form.
- **The "phantom 5th" expectation**: Some listeners expect to hear it every time. When it doesn't ring (e.g. on passing chords, diminished 7ths), the arrangement sounds weaker — so arrangers must strategically place ring-target chords on structural beats only.
