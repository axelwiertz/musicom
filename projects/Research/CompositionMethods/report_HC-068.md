## 1. The Craft Procedure in Detail

The Figurenlehre was a five-stage compositional workflow adapted from classical oratory. Baroque composers learned it in *Lateinschulen* as part of the *trivium* alongside grammar and rhetoric itself, making it as natural as harmonic theory.

### Stage 1: Inventio — Finding the Material

**Step 1: Select the Affection**

The composer first decided what emotional state the piece would embody. The Doctrine of Affections held that music could reliably move listeners to specific passions:

| Affection | Character | Typical Key (Mattheson) | Key Figures |
|-----------|-----------|------------------------|-------------|
| Joy (*Gaudium*) | Cheerful, lively | D major, C major, F major | Anabasis, Tirata, Exclamatio |
| Sadness (*Tristitia*) | Grieving, serious | G minor, C minor, E minor | Catabasis, Passus duriusculus, Suspiratio |
| Anger (*Ira*) | Fierce, agitated | A minor, D minor | Epizeuxis (insistent repeats), Salto composto |
| Love (*Amor*) | Tender, gentle | B-flat major, F major | Syncopatio, Accentus, Gradatio |
| Devotion (*Devotio*) | Reverent, awe-filled | E-flat major, C minor | Paragoge (pedal point), Homoioptoton (silences) |

**Step 2: Choose Key and Meter**

Per Mattheson's *Der vollkommene Capellmeister* (1739), each key carried a characteristic affect. Triple meter = dance/lively, duple = serious/majestic. Tempo aligns with the affection: fast for joy, slow for grief, moderate for love.

**Step 3: Compose the Thematic Idea (Loci Topici)**

The composer created a subject with a clear interval profile and rhythm that embodied the chosen affection. This subject was deliberately kept "plain" — minimal figuration — so that later figure application would be audible as significant speech, not constant decoration.

> Bach's rhetorical practice (per Dreyfus, *Bach and the Patterns of Invention*): Bach "invented" — he neither discovered nor obeyed inviolable laws. His process was messy, iterative, and craft-based, not inspired-genius.

### Stage 2: Dispositio — Arranging the Material

**Step 4: Lay Out the Rhetorical Structure (Ciceronian Six-Part Oration)**

Mattheson explicitly mapped the classical oration onto musical form:

| Rhetorical Stage | Musical Function | Notes |
|------------------|------------------|-------|
| *Exordium* | Opening — captures attention | Striking gesture: toccata flourish, bold leap, dissonant chord, rapid scale. The "hook." |
| *Narratio* | Context statement | Often omitted in instrumental music per Mattheson |
| *Propositio* | Theme exposition | The fugue subject or ritornello — stated plainly, minimally figured |
| *Confirmatio* | Affirming arguments | Multiple entries of the theme in related keys (tonic/dominant/relative) |
| *Confutatio* | Refutation / contrast | Contrasting material, heightened dissonance, chromatic excursions, remote keys |
| *Peroratio* | Conclusion | Pedal point, recall of *Exordium* material, decisive V–I cadence |

**Step 5: Assign Keys and Plan Dissonance Curve**

The composer mapped each section to a key (circle of fifths, relative majors/minors). Dissonance density forms an arc: low in Exordium/Propositio, rising through Confirmatio, peaking in Confutatio, resolving in Peroratio.

### Stage 3: Elaboratio — Figurative Working-Out

**Step 6: Select and Apply Musical-Rhetorical Figures**

This is the core compositional act. The composer chose figures from the toolbox and deployed them in specific voices at specific structural moments. The figures fall into four main categories (following Burmeister/Bernhard classification):

**Category A: Figures of Melodic Repetition** — signalling emphasis, insistence, persistence

| Figure | Description | Musical Realization | Affect | Example |
|--------|-------------|-------------------|--------|---------|
| *Epizeuxis* | Immediate repetition of a note or figure | Same pitch repeated 2–4× (e.g., dotted rhythm repeat) | Emphasis, insistence, obsession | Schütz "Saul Saul" repeated call |
| *Anaphora* | Repetition of a figure at start of successive passages | Same interval pattern at phrase onset, each phrase | Persistence, accumulation | Bach fugue episodes starting with same head |
| *Epiphora* | Repetition of a passage's end at ends of subsequent passages | Same cadential figure each phrase | Closing emphasis | Handel dotted cadence figures |
| *Synonymia* | Repetition of a figure at different pitch levels | Sequential repetition of a melodic fragment | Elaboration, amplification | Bach sequential sequences |
| *Gradatio* | Sequence moving in one direction (ladder) | Ascending or descending sequential pattern | Climbing energy / descending resignation | Buxtehude praeludia sequences |
| *Polyptoton* | Repetition at different pitches (not sequential) | Same figure transposed to different scale degrees | Variation within unity | Bach countersubject entries |

**Category B: Figures of Harmonic/Fugal Repetition**

| Figure | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| *Fuga realis* | Real fugue (exact interval transposition) |
| *Fuga melica* | Tonal fugue (modified intervals for key adaptation) |

These were considered direct analogues of rhetorical argumentation — the fugue as a logical debate.

**Category C: Figures of Depiction / Representation (Hypotyposis)**

The most affect-visible category — these "paint" the text or emotion:

| Figure | Description | Realization | Affect | Notable Use |
|--------|-------------|-------------|--------|-------------|
| *Anabasis* | Ascending motion | Melodic line rising stepwise or by leap | Ascent, hope, resurrection, exaltation | Bach "Et resurrexit" ascending scales |
| *Catabasis* | Descending motion | Melodic line falling stepwise or by leap | Descent, grief, death, humility, fall | Bach "O Mensch bewein" descending bass; Crucifixus chromatic descent |
| *Circulatio* | Sine-wave circular motion | Wave-shaped melodic contour (up-down-up-down) | Infinity, eternity, hell-circle, encompassing | Bach St. Matthew Passion "wound" figures |
| *Tirata* | Rapid scalar passage | Fast scale (often ascending) | Agitation, flight, lightning, purification | Handel "And He shall purify" runs |
| *Passus duriusculus* | Chromatic semitone step (usually descending) | ♯4–4–♭3–3–♭2 pattern | Grief, wound, pain, sorrow — "hard passage" | Bach Crucifixus ground (chromatic fourth); Monteverdi "Lamento della Ninfa" |
| *Suspiratio / Suspirium* | Sigh through a rest or short-breath figure | Short note + rest (♪♬ pattern) | Sighing, grief, catching breath | Bach "Erbarme dich" violin obbligato |
| *Exclamatio* | Leap upward (usually minor 6th or larger) | Leap of 6th or octave, often on accented beat | Exclamation, joy, sudden revelation | Bach "Und siehe da" leaps |
| *Abruptio / Tmesis* | Sudden break / rest in all voices | Simultaneous silence in all parts | Shock, death, inability to speak | Buxtehude praeludia sudden breaks |
| *Aposiopesis* | General pause (scored rest, often fermatad) | Full-bar rest with fermata | Awe, speechlessness, death | Bach "Beim Kreuze" pause |
| *Homoioptoton* | Sudden silence on a held chord | Chord cut off abruptly with rest | Interrupted thought | Buxtehude structural breaks |

**Category D: Figures of Ornamentation / Dissonance**

| Figure | Description | Realization | Affect |
|--------|-------------|-------------|--------|
| *Syncopatio / Retardatio* | Suspension (consonance prepared, held through beat, resolved down by step) | 4–3, 7–6, 9–8 suspensions | Longing, tension, sighing |
| *Pathopoeia* | Chromaticism for passionate expression | Chromatic alteration of scale degrees | Grief, passion, intensity (elevated from passus duriusculus) |
| *Transitus* | Chromatic passing note | ♯4→5, ♭7→6, etc. | Bittersweet, yearning |
| *Passagio* | Embellished melodic passage | Runs, turns, trills in a melodic line | Ornamental intensity |
| *Accentus* | Upper or lower neighbour note | Appoggiatura, accented neighbour | Ornamental emphasis |
| *Multiplicatio / Paragoge* | Pedal point | Sustained bass note (usually V then I) | Stability, foundation, divine firmness |

**Step 7: Voice-Specific Figure Assignment**

Each voice in the texture carries a specific figure family:

- **Upper voice (soprano/cantus)**: Carries the primary *inventio* theme and repetition figures (Epizeuxis, Anaphora, Synonymia)
- **Middle voices (alto, tenor)**: Suspension chains (Syncopatio, Retardatio), chordal padding, imitative counterpoint
- **Bass**: *Paragoge* (pedal point), *Catabasis* or *Anabasis* directional figures, ostinato figures

**Step 8: Section-Level Figure Deployment**

| Section | Figure Density | Primary Figure Types | Dissonance Level |
|---------|---------------|---------------------|------------------|
| Exordium | Medium | Tirata, Exclamatio, Abruptio | Moderate |
| Propositio | Low (minimal) | None (plain theme) | Low |
| Confirmatio I | Medium | Epizeuxis, Anaphora, Synonymia | Moderate |
| Confutatio I | High | Passus duriusculus, Pathopoeia, Circulatio | High |
| Confirmatio II | Medium | Gradatio, Polyptoton | Moderate |
| Confutatio II | Highest | All figure types, false relations | Maximum |
| Peroratio | Medium→Resolving | Paragoge, recall of Exordium figures | Resolving |

### Stage 4: Decoratio — Ornamentation

**Step 9: Add Surface Ornamentation**

Consistent with the affection: trills for joy and celebration, mordents for agitation, appoggiaturas for sighing, turns for elegance. Ornaments reinforce what the *elaboratio* figures have already established — they are the "gesture" of delivery, not the argument itself.

### Stage 5: Executio — Performance

**Step 10: Perform as Rhetorical Oration**

The performer becomes the orator: tempo rubato (agogic accents on figures), articulation changes (detached for *Tirata*, legato for *Catabasis*), dynamic shaping per section. The same figure notation could be interpreted differently depending on the performer's understanding of the affect.

## 2. Practitioner Examples in Detail

### J.S. Bach — St. Matthew Passion (BWV 244)

- **"O Mensch, bewein dein Sünde groß"** (BWV 402) — The bass line (pedal) is a continuous *Catabasis*: a descending pattern that never lets up for the entire chorale. The vocal parts are saturated with *Suspiratio*: rests and short note-groups that literally sigh. The affect: deep grief over sin's enormity.
- **"Crucifixus"** (Mass in B Minor, BWV 232) — The entire movement is built over a *Passus duriusculus* chromatic descending tetrachord in the bass (G–F♯–F–E–E♭–D). Also called the "lament bass" — the chromatic fourth. This figure appears 13 times through the movement, each time with different vocal figuration above it. The text "crucifixus etiam pro nobis" is illustrated by the "hard" chromatic descent, literally the "cross" of the harmony.
- **"Et resurrexit"** (Mass in B Minor) — Immediate contrast: *Anabasis* ascending in all voices, *Tirata* rapid scales upward, *Salto semplice* joyful leaps. The bass ascends from D to D across the first 8 bars. The affect: resurrection victory after the crucifixion grief.
- **St. Matthew Passion Chorales** — Bach harmonized pre-existing hymn tunes with rhetorical figures in inner voices. The chorale "Befiehl du deine Wege" (BWV 244/44) uses a *Passus duriusculus* in the alto at the word "Tod" (death). The chorale "Wenn ich einmal soll scheiden" (BWV 244/62) has *Syncopatio* suspensions at every mention of the cross.
- **Orgelbüchlein (BWV 599–644)** — 46 chorale preludes, each a compositional workshop:
  - "Durch Adams Fall ist ganz verderbt" (BWV 637) — descending *Catabasis* in the pedal = Adam's fall into sin
  - "Christ lag in Todesbanden" (BWV 625) — *Suspiratio* sigh figures = death's grip
  - "Erstanden ist der heilige Christ" (BWV 628) — *Tirata* ascending runs = resurrection victory
  - "Ich ruf zu dir, Herr Jesu Christ" (BWV 639) — *Pathopoeia* chromatic inner voice = supplication
- **The Well-Tempered Clavier** — Even without text, Bach's fugue subjects are rhetorical "inventions" with clear affect. The C minor fugue (WTC I) — a sighing *Suspiratio* subject; the D major fugue (WTC I) — joyful *Anabasis* leaps; the B-flat minor fugue (WTC I) — *Passus duriusculus* chromatic subject.

### Dieterich Buxtehude — Praeludia (BuxWV 142, 146, 149)

Buxtehude's organ praeludia are textbook applications of the rhetorical cycle:

- **BuxWV 149** — Opens with a free *Exordium* flourish (manualiter toccata), then *Propositio* fugue exposition (2 voices), *Confirmatio* section with *Epizeuxis* repeated notes, *Confutatio* with *Tirata* rapid scales and *Abruptio* sudden silences, closing *Peroratio* with *Paragoge* pedal point.
- **BuxWV 146** — *Anabasis* ascents in the opening, *Catabasis* descents in the pedal, *Gradatio* sequences building tension, *Homoioptoton* (general pause) before the final pedal point.
- **BuxWV 142** — Most elaborate: tocat-style opening (Exordium), teo fugues (Propositio/Confirmatio), passagaio figuration (Confutatio), pedal-point finish (Peroratio). Binary figure contast: Eb major sections = *Gaudium* (joy), Cminor = *Trisittia* (sadness).

### Heinrich Shütz — Symphoniae Sacrae (1629/1647, 1650)

Shütz provides the most direct applicatoin of *Musica Poetica* in the German 17th-century repetoire:

- **"Saul, Saul, was verfolgst du mich?"** (SWV 415) — The word "Saul" is repeated 9 times in obsessive *Epizeuxis*, each time higher (St. Paul's insistent call from heaven). The word "verfolgst" (persecute) is set to a *Passus duriusculus* chromatic climb.
- **"Fili mi, Absalon"** (SWV 269) — *Catabasis* descent on "Absalon" = descent into death. *Passus duriuscu* on "morereris" (would God I had died). Unparralleled intensity of *Pathopoeia*.

### G.F. Handel — Messiah (HWV 56)

- **"And He shall purify"** — *Tirata* (rapid runs) on "purify" = purging, cleansing fire of the Holy Spirit.
- **" He was despised"** — *Pathopoeia* chromaticism, *Suspiratio* rests after "despised" = emotional break.
- **"for unto us a chiid is born"** — *Anabasis* on "born" = the incarantion, God descending to rise.
- **"The peple that walked in darkness"** — *Catabasis* in the bass = walking in the valley of death.
- **"Hailelujah"** — *Epizeuxis* on "Hailelujah" = insistent joful praise. *Anaphora* at each pharse start.

## 3. Geerman Pedagogy Pipeline

The Figurenlehre was not a academic curiosity — it was how compos ition was taught:

1. **Praeceptum** — study the rules (the figure categories, the affection table)
2. **Exemplus** — study exisitng works (Lassus motets for Theorists; Bach analyzed Buxtehude)
3. **Imitatio** — copy the examples, then vry (the core compos itional asct: composing by imitating figure usage)

This "imitatio" model dircetly opposes the "isonired genius" stereotype. Bach himsel taught this way — his students copied his figures, his counterpoint, his ornametation.

### Sections (Ciceronian rhetorcal structure for a typical 48–96 bar piece)

| Section | Rhetorical Stage | Bars (4/4) | Figure Density | Primary Figures | Musical Content |
|---------|-----------------|------------|----------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| **S0_Exordium** | Exordium | 2–8 | High | Tirata, Exclamatio, Abruptio | Striking opening toccata or bold leap gesture; attention-grabber; no theme yet |
| **S1_Propositio** | Propositio | 4–16 | Low (minimal) | None (plain theme stated) | Theme/fugue subject stated clearly and plainly; one voice alone or in transparent texture |
| **S2_Confirmatio_I** | Confirmatio | 8–24 | Medium | Epizeuxis, Anaphora, Synonymia | Theme re-enters in additional voices (tonic/dominant); repetition figures accumulate |
| **S3_Confutatio_I** | Confutatio | 8–24 | High | Passus duriusculus, Pathopoeia, Circulatio, Transitus | Contrasting material; chromatic excursions; heightened dissonance; opposite affect from Confirmatio |
| **S4_Confirmatio_II** | Confirmatio | 8–24 | Medium | Gradatio, Polyptoton, Synononymia | Return to affirmative material; sequential development in related key |
| **S5_Confutatio_II** | Confutatio | 8–24 | Highest | All figure types; false relations; maximum chromaticism | Climax of dissonance; remote keys; all voices in maximal figure activity |
| **S6_Peroratio** | Peroratio | 4–12 | Medium→Resolving | Paragoge (pedal point), Aposiopesis, Exordium recall | Pedal point on V→I; optional recall of Exordium opening gesture; decisive V–I close |

### Rules (Gammar for the Engine)

1. **Affection-key lock** — Every composition's tonic key determines its ruling affection (per Mattheson's affect table). All figures deployed must be congruent with that affection. A G minor *Tristitia* piece may NOT deploy joy figures (Tirata upward, Exclamatio leaps).

2. **Figure-to-section density gradient** — Figure density increases from S1 (minimal) → S3 (high) → S5 (maximum), then resolves in S6. S1_Propositio MUST have minimal figures (no more than 1–2 isolated figure cells) to maintain the rhetorical effect of "plain speech."

3. **Catabasis/Anabasis polarity invariant** — V1/V4 directional figures maintain consistent polarity within a section. Section ∈ {ascending} = Anabasis only (no descending patterns). Section ∈ {descending} = Catabasis only. Reversing polarity IS a figure in itself (*Antithesis* = contrast) and may only appear at Confutatio section boundaries.

4. **Cadential punctuation rule** — Every section MUST end with a clear rhetorical punctuation figure:
   - Afffirmativ sections (S2, S4): suspension cadence (Syncopatio 4-3 or 7-6 resolving to I)
   - Confutatio sections (S3, S5): Abruptio (gneral pause) or Aposiopesis (fermata pause)
   - Peroratio: V–I authentic cadence with Paragoge pedal
   No trailing-off or ambiguous endings.

5. **Pedal point convergence** — S6_Peroratio MUST contain a *Paragoge* (pedal point) in V4. Duration: minimum 2 bars (proportional to piece length). Typically dominant (V) → tonic (I). If piece is in minor, optional tierce de Picardie at pedal resolution.

6. **Exordium non-repetition** — The opening figure of S0 must be unique. It may NOT appear verbatim elsewhere in the piece. However, the *Peroratio* (S6) may recall a transformed version (augmented, diminished, or inverted) as a rhetorical recap.

7. **Abruptio seam rule** — Abruptio / Tmesis cells (simultaneous rests) may only appear at section boundaries or structural pivot points (after climax in Confutatio, before Peroratio entry). Never mid-phrase.

8. **Figure-voice assigment stability** — Each voice carries its asigned figure family throughout the piece. V0 does NOT suddenly take V3's Pathopoeia function. Figure families remain per-voice unless a deliberate *Antithesis* section swaps them (this swap must be indicated by a structural marking in the composition).

9. **Consonantia suspension chain** — V1's suspensions must follow a chain pattern: each suspension resolves to the next suspension's preparation. No isolated, unconnected suspensions. The chain may break only at cadence points.

10. **Zero-drift pad** — All cells padded to section terminal landmark; validate gate ensures equal-ngth track across all 6 voics.

## 8. Quirks & Pitfalls

1. **Over-figuration** — The biggest danger: applying too many figures simultaneously. Bernhard warned "not every dissonance is a figure." Figures must be deployed sparingly at structural moments.

2. **Figure label ≠ composition command** — Baroque composers did NOT sit with a checklist of 84 Latin figure names. The *Musica Poetica* was a pedagogical framework studied in school and internalized. The figure names are analytical labels, not real-time compositional commands.

3. **Anachronistic reading risk** — Applying Figurenlehre analysis to every Baroque piece as if the composer consciously planned each figure is a methodological error. The composers THOUGHT in figures; they didn't consult a table.

4. **Vocal vs instrumental mismatch** — Figures originated in vocal music with text. Transferring the Figurenlehre to purely instrumental music requires careful adaptation: the figure's *affect* must be preserved even when the text is absent.

5. **Mattheson's excess** — Mattheson's *Der vollkommene Capellmeister* lists an overwhelming number of figures with sometimes strained analogies. The core practical set (~20 figures) covers 90% of actual Baroque usage.

6. **Affection-key conflict** — If a composer modulates to a key contrary to the ruling affection (e.g., modulating to the subdominant in a *gaudium* piece), that modulation itself becomes a rhetorical gesture — a *confutatio* move. This requires explicit marking in the composition plan.

7. **Modern hearing** — The *confutatio* sections can sound like "wrong notes" to modern ears shaped by common-practice harmony (e.g., Schütz's false relations). This is intentional — the *confutatio* REQUIRES audible harshness to create the rhetorical contrast.
