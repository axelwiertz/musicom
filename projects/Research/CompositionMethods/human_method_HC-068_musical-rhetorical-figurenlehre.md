# HC-068: Musical-Rhetorical Figures Composition (Figurenlehre / Musica Poetica)

**Tradition:** Western Classical Baroque — German Lutheran *Musica Poetica* tradition (Rostock, Hamburg, Leipzig, Lübeck, c. 1599–1750); theorists Joachim Burmeister, Christoph Bernhard, Johann Mattheson; composers Heinrich Schütz, Dieterich Buxtehude, J.S. Bach, Georg Philipp Telemann, George Frideric Handel

**Layer:** human (→ concrete) — a rhetorical figure vocabulary that Baroque composers consciously deployed to move listeners to predictable affections; the figures are musical "gestures" that map directly onto concrete pitch-rhythm cells

**Related DB entries:** HC-055 (fugue) — fugue was considered part of the *dispositio* stage; HC-038 (sonata form) — successor form; HC-062 (twelve-tone) — different era; HC-023 (spectral listening) — different tradition

## Description

The Figurenlehre (German: "doctrine of figures") was a pedagogical and compositional system codifying musical-rhetorical figures — specific melodic, harmonic, and rhythmic gestures derived from classical oratory — that Baroque composers used to move listeners to specific emotional states (affections). Unlike later "word painting" (a surface-level text depiction), the Figurenlehre was a **generative compositional method**: the composer chose an affection (grief, joy, anger, devotion, awe), selected a repertory of figures associated with that affection, and built an entire movement around them, using the Ciceronian rhetorical stages (*inventio, dispositio, elaboratio, decoratio, executio*) as an architectural plan.

The method was taught in German *Lateinschulen* alongside rhetoric itself (both were part of the *trivium*), and Lutheran cantors like Bach, Buxtehude, and Schütz received it as part of their daily training. It is not a dry, academic exercise — it was the practical craft toolkit for communicating the Gospel through music.

## The Craft Procedure (Step by Step)

### Stage 1: Inventio — Finding the Material

1. **Select the affection** — What emotional state will this piece embody? Joy (*gaudium*), sadness (*tristitia*), anger (*ira*), love (*amor*), hope (*spes*), fear (*timor*)? The affection governs every subsequent choice.
2. **Choose the key** — Each key had a characteristic affect (Mattheson: D major = strident/cheerful, G minor = serious/graceful, E minor = pensive/sad, C major = crude/impudent, B-flat major = magnificent/refined).
3. **Select or compose a theme** (*loci topici*) — A melodic subject with a distinctive interval profile and rhythm that embodies the chosen affection. For vocal music, the text determines the affection directly; for instrumental music, the composer selects a topic (dance type, ceremonial gesture, pastoral mood).
4. **Choose the meter and tempo** — Triple meter = dance/lively, duple = serious/majestic; faster tempo = joyous, slower = solemn.

### Stage 2: Dispositio — Arranging the Material

5. **Lay out the rhetorical structure** (Ciceronian six-part oration):
   - *Exordium* — opening that captures attention (toccata flourish, striking gesture, poignant chord)
   - *Narratio* — statement of context (can be brief or omitted in music per Mattheson)
   - *Propositio* — expostion of the main theme (the fugue subject or ritornello)
   - *Confirmatio* — arguments in favour of the propositio (affirmative entrances)
   - *Confutatio* — refutatio / counter-rgument (dissonat sections, contrasting character)
   - *Peroratio* — concusive section (pedal point, recall of opening, closing gesture)

6. **Assign keys for each section** — Moate through related keys (circle of fifths, relative majors/minors) following the affectionat arc.

7. **Pace the dsonance** — Plan the "rhetoricat tension": exposiory sections have moderate dsonance, confutatio sections maxime dsonance (chromaticism, false relations, Passus duriusculus), peroratio resolves.

### Stage 3: Elaboratio — Figurative Working-Out

8. **Select musical-rhetorical figures** from the toolbox. These fall into four main categories (after Bernhard/Burmeister):

   **A. Figures of Melodic Repetition:**
   - *Epizeuxis* — immediate repetition of a note or figure (emphatic insistence)
   - *Anaphora* — repitition of a figure at the beginning of successive passages
   - *Epiphora* — repitition of a passage's end at the end of subsequent passages
   - *Synonymia* — repitition of a figure at different pitch levels (seqiential)
   - *Gradatio* — seqience moving in one direction (ascending or descening "ladder")
   - *Polyptoton* — repitition of a melodic passage at different pitches

   **B. Figures of Harmonic (Fugal) Repetition:**
   - *Fuga realis* — real fugue
   - *Fuga metslis* — tonal fugue (modified intervals for key)
   - With all fugel figures considered direct analogues of rhetorical argumentation

   **C. Figures of Depiction / Repesentation (*Hypotyposis*):**
   - *Abasis* — ascending motion (ascent, hope, resurrection)
   - *Catabasis* — descending motion (descent, grief, death, humility)
   - *Ciculatio* — sine-wave circular motion (infinity, cycle, hell circle)
   - *Tiraa* — rapid scalar passage (agitation, flight, lightning)
   - *Passu duriusclus* — chromatic semitone step, especially descending (grief, wound, sorrow — "hard passage")
   - *Suspiraio / Susprium* — sigh through a rest or short-breath figure (sighing)
   - *Exclamatio* — leap upward (exclamation)
   - *Abrupio / Tmsis* — suden break / rest in all voices (silence = death, shock)
   - *Apiosiopesis* — general pause (inability to speak, awe)
   - *Homoioptoton* — suden silence with fermata

   **D. Figues of Oamntation / Dsonance:**
   - *Synopatio / Reardatio* — suspensions (tension, longing)
   - *Penthe / Traisitus* — chromatic passing notes
   - *Passaio* — embellished melodic passage
   - *Accentus* — upper / lower neighour note
   - *Multa / Paragoge* — pedal point (persistence, foundation)

9. **Apply figures to each structural section**:
   - *Exordium*: striking opening figure (typtoica: a bold leap, a dsonant chord, a tiraa)
   - Theme ann ouncemet (Propositio): usually simple, figura-free — the "plain speech" of the theme
   - Developmet passes: increasingly figurative — sequenes, suspensions, cromatism
   - Cadential poins: clear punctuation figures (cadential suspensio, teirce de Picardie in minor)

10. **Layer multiple voices** — In contrapuntal textures, diffrent voices may carry difffrent figures simultanously. Bach's Art of Fugue and St. Matthew Passion frequently superimpose two or three figures in diffrent voices, each reflecting a facet of the affect.

### Stage 4: Decoratio — Ornamentation

11. **Add surface ornamentation** consistent with the affection: trills for joy, mordents for agitation, appoggiaturas for sighing. The decoratio reinforces what the elaboratio established.

### Stage 5: Executio — Performance

12. **Perform with rhetorical delivery** — tempo rubato, agogic accents, articulation changes — the performer becomes the orator delivering the figured speech.

## Practitioner Examples

### J.S. Bach — St. Matthew Passion (BWV 244)
- **"O Mensch, bewein dein Sünde groß"** — *Catabasis* (descending bass line) throughout, literally depicting the fall of humanity; *Suspiratio* sigh figures in the vocal line.
- **"Crucifixus"** (Mass in B Minor) — *Passus duriusculus* chromatic descending tetrachord as the bass ground (the "lament bass"), *Catabasis* throughout, *Pathopoeia* (chromaticism for grief).
- **"Et resurrexit"** — *Anabasis* ascending figures in all voices, *Tirata* rapid scales upward, *Salto semplice* joyful leaps.
- **St. Matthew Passion chorales** — Bach harmonized pre-existing hymn tunes with figures: the *Passus duriusculus* in the inner voice at "denn es gehet dem Menschen wie dem Vieh" (text about death), *Suspiratio* rests at "erwäge" (contemplate).
- **The Well-Tempered Clavier** — Fugue subjects themselves are "invented" as rhetorical ideas; individual episodes develop figures through sequence (*Gradatio*), interruption (*Tmesis*), and cadential climax.

### Dieterich Buxtehude — Praeludia (BuxWV 142, 146, 149)
- Opened with *Exordium* flourishes (free toccata figures), followed by *Propositio* fugue exposition, alternating *Confirmatio* and *Confutatio* sections with contrasting figures, ending with *Peroratio* on a pedal point.
- BuxWV 146: *Epizeuxis* (insistent repeated notes), *Abruptio* (sudden silences), *Gradatio* (sequential ascents), *Anabasis/Catabasis* contrasting.

### Heinrich Schütz — Symphoniae Sacrae (1629/1647, 1650)
- The most direct application of *Musica Poetica* in the German 17th century. Schütz used figures to illustrate every text detail. "Saul, Saul, was verfolgst du mich?" — obsessive *Epizeuxis* on "Saul" = the insistent call, *Passus duriusculus* on "verfolgst" (persecute).

### G.F. Handel — Messiah
- "And He shall purify" — *Tirata* (rapid runs) on "purify" = purging fire.
- "He was despised" — *Pathopoeia* chromaticism, *Suspiratio* rests.
- "For unto us a child is born" — *Anabasis* on "born" = incarnation.

### Johann Sebastian Bach — Orgelbüchlein (BWV 599–644)
- 46 chorale preludes, each a compositional workshop in Figurenlehre. E.g., "Durch Adams Fall ist ganz verderbt" (BWV 637) — descending *Catabasis* in the pedal = Adam's fall into sin; "Christ lag in Todesbanden" (BWV 625) — *Suspiratio* sigh figures = death's grip; "Erstanden ist der heilige Christ" (BWV 628) — *Tirata* ascending runs = resurrection victory.

| S6_Peroratio | Peroratio | 4–12 | Pedal point, recall of Exordium, V–I close | High → resolving — *Paragoge*, *Aposiopesis* → cadence |

### Rules (grammar encoded in the engine)

1. **Affection-key lock** — Each composition's key determines a ruling affection (via Mattheson's table). All figures selected must be congruent with that affection. No joy figures in a G minor *tristitia* piece.
2. **Figure-to-section density gradient** — Figures increase through *Confirmatio* and peak in *Confutatio* sections, then resolve in *Peroratio*. S1_Propositio must have minimal figures (the "plain speech" of the theme).
3. **Catabasis/Anabasis polarity invariant** — V2's directional figures must maintain consistent polarity per section: descending sections = grief/humility, ascending = ascent/hope. Reversing polarity is itself a figure (*Antithesis* = contrast).
4. **Cadential punctuation** — Every section must end with a clear rhetorical punctuation figure: suspension cadence (*Syncopatio* → resolution) or *Abruptio* (general pause) if the affect demands it. No trailing-off endings.
5. **Pedal point convergence** — S6_Peroratio must contain a *Paragoge* (pedal point) in V4, typically on the dominant then tonic. Duration proportional to section length (min 2 bars).
6. **Exordium non-repetition** — The opening figure of S0 must be unique; it may not appear verbatim elsewhere (though *Peroratio* may recall its gesture transformed).
7. **Consonantia suspension chain** — V1's suspensions must follow a chain pattern: each suspension resolves to the next suspension's preparation. No isolated, unconnected suspensions.
8. **Abruptio seam rule** — *Abruptio* / *Tmesis* cells (sudden rests) may only appear at section boundaries or structural pivot points (after climax in Confutatio, before Peroratio entry). Never mid-phrase.
9. **Figure-voice assignment stability** — Each voice carries its assigned figure family throughout the piece. V0 does not suddenly take V3's *Pathopoeia* function; figure families remain per-voice unless a deliberate *Antithesis* section swaps them.
10. **Zero-drift pad** — All cells padded to section terminal landmark; validate gate ensures equal-length tracks.

## Quirks & Pitfalls

- **Over-figuration** — The biggest pitfall: applying too many figures simultaneously. Bernhard warned: "not every dissonance is a figure." Figures must be deployed sparingly at structural moments, not everywhere.
- **Copy-paste fallacy** — Figures are not static labels you can stamp on. A *Passus duriusculus* is not just "any four chromatic notes" — it requires deliberate *duriusculus* (harshness) in its interval and rhythmic placement.
- **Anachronistic reading** — Baroque composers didn't sit with a checklist of 84 Latin figures. The *Musica Poetica* was a pedagogical framework studied in school, internalized, and applied instinctively in later life. The figure names are analytical labels, not real-time compositional commands.
- **Conflict with modern harmonic analysis** — A figure may sound like a "wrong note" to modern ears (e.g., the false relations in Schütz). This is intentional — the *confutatio* stage requires audibly harsh dissonance.
- **Vocal vs instrumental mismatch** — Figures originated in vocal music with text. Transferring the Figurenlehre to purely instrumental music (as Bach did in the WTC and Buxtehude in the praeludia) requires careful adaptation: the figure's *affect* must be preserved even when the text is absent.
- **Latin name overload** — The 84+ figure names are easy to over-enumerate. The core set (~20) covers 90% of actual Baroque usage. The remaining 60+ are hair-splitting distinctions by later theorists.
