## 1. Method name & tradition

**Jingju aria composition** — the craft of building a sung 唱段 (*chàngduàn*, aria) for Chinese Peking opera out of three interlocking human systems: (1) the two **melodic modes** 西皮 *xipi* / 二黄 *erhuang* (plus their transposed 反调 *fǎn diào* forms), (2) the **rhythmic-metric patterns** 板式 *bǎnshì* (the fixed tempo/meter/board-beat templates that chain into an aria's dramatic arc), and (3) the **percussion scores** 锣鼓经 *luógujīng* (oral syllable patterns that cue action, entrance, and aria alike), all bound together by the **heterophonic jinghu** accompaniment (托腔保调 *tuō qiāng bǎo diào* — "support the aria, preserve the mode").

- **Tradition / Culture:** Jingju (京戲/京剧, "capital theatre"), the national opera of China. Born 1790 when the Four Great Anhui Troupes (四大徽班: Sanqing, Sixi, Hechun, Chuntai) entered Beijing for the Qianlong Emperor's 80th birthday singing *erhuang*; 1828 Hubei Han-opera troupes brought *xipi*, fusing into 皮黄 *píhuáng* theatre; Beijing dialect colouring → named 京剧 in the press (1876); formally regarded as fully formed by 1845. Matured in the Qing court (esp. under Empress Dowager Cixi), flourished 1920s–30s (the "four great dan" 四大名旦 era), standardized after 1949, transformed in the 1960s–70s *yàngbǎnxì* model works. **UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, Representative List (2010).** Scholarship: Elizabeth Wichmann (*Listening to Theatre: The Aural Dimension of Peking Opera*, 1991), Dolores Menstell Hsu (1964), Huang Jinpei (*Xipi and Erhuang of Beijing and Guangdong styles*, Asian Music 1989), Nancy Guy (1990, 1995), Joshua Goldstein (*Drama Kings*, 2007).
- **Key figures:** 谭鑫培 Tan Xinpei (laosheng "king", 1847–1917), 余叔岩 Yu Shuyan (naohouyin 脑后音 school), 四大名旦: 梅兰芳 Mei Lanfang, 程砚秋 Cheng Yanqiu, 荀慧生 Xun Huisheng, 尚小云 Shang Xiaoyun; 杨宝森 Yang Baosen, 马连良 Ma Lianliang, 周信芳 Zhou Xinfang; qínshī (fiddler-composers): 梅雨田 Mei Yutian (Tan Xinpei's accompanist), 徐兰沅 Xu Lanyuan & 王少卿 Wang Shaoqing (Mei Lanfang; Wang added the erhu to dan accompaniment in 1923), 李慕良 Li Muliang (Ma Lianliang), 杨宝忠 Yang Baozhong (Yang Baosen); modern exponents 于魁智, 李胜素, 王珮瑜.

## 3. The craft procedure in detail (how the human does it)

### Stage 0 — apprenticeship (oral/aural transmission, 口传心授 "mouth-to-mouth, heart-to-heart")
1. A young performer or *qínshī* (fiddler) learns by imitation: whole *zhézi xì* (highlight scenes) are memorized by ear; *luógujīng* are learned as **spoken syllables** (仓 cang, 台 tai, 才/七 cai/qi, 倾 qing, 匡 kuang, 空 kong, 大/达 da, 八/崩 ba/beng, 嘟 du, 隆咚 longdong…) before ever touching a gong. Notation (historically 工尺谱 gōngchěpǔ, today jianpu numbered score) is a **memory aid, not the composition**.
2. The *gǔlǎo* (鼓佬, drum master) learns to conduct the whole ensemble with the 板鼓 *bǎngǔ* (single-skin high drum) and 檀板 *tánbǎn* (clapper) — the drum-and-clapper player IS the conductor, controlling tempo and cueing every 锣鼓点 with hand gestures.

### Stage 1 — choose the mode (声腔) for the dramatic emotion
3. Pick **xipi** (la–mi open strings; "bright, lively, excited"; English scholarship: "joyous stories", derived from the high loud Qinqiang of the northwest) for argument, excitement, haste, joy.
4. Pick **erhuang** (sol–re open strings; "low, soft, despondent folk tunes of south-central Hubei"; "lyrical stories") for reflection, grief, interiority, dignity.
5. For deeper sorrow use the transposed 反调 forms — 反二黄 *fǎn erhuang* (do–sol) and 反西皮 *fǎn xipi* (same la–mi but transposed down): darker, more lament-like. The two modes share a standard 2/4-feel grid and the same six common 板式, differing in pitch inventory, cadence notes (落音), and ornament.

### Stage 2 — design the 板式 chain (the aria's rhythmic architecture)
6. Chain the *bǎnshì* patterns to trace the character's emotional arc; each pattern is a fixed meter + tempo + phrase structure:
   - 导板 *dǎobǎn* (free-rhythm lead-in, one line, launches the aria),
   - 回龙 *huílóng* ("returning dragon", links after 导板),
   - 慢板 *mànbǎn* / 三眼 variants (*màn sānyǎn*, *zhōng sānyǎn*, *kuài sānyǎn*) — 一板三眼 (4/4, "one board, three eyes"), slow, the emotional centre,
   - 原板 *yuánbǎn* — 一板一眼 (2/4), the medium "original" pattern, most common,
   - 二六 *èrliù* (xipi only, 2/4, syllabic, argumentative narration),
   - 流水 *liúshuǐ* (xipi only, 有板无眼 "board without eye" 1/4, flowing),
   - 快板 *kuàibǎn* (xipi only, very fast 1/4, climax),
   - 摇板 *yáobǎn* ("shaking board": free-rhythm voice over the orchestra's strict 1/4 pulse),
   - 散板 *sǎnbǎn* (无板无眼, fully free — entrance and exit),
   - 滚板 *gǔnbǎn* (erhuang only, fast 1/4, extremity of grief).
   A typical full aria: 导板 → 回龙 → 慢板 → 原板 → 快板/流水 → 散板 — an accelerating, densifying arc exactly like a Western scena but governed by the 板眼 board-beat grid.

### Stage 3 — set the text (填词/依字行腔: melody born from the characters)
7. Lyrics are **couplets** (*lián* of two *jù* lines): two 7-character or two 10-character lines, each subdivided into three *dǒu* (pauses) in a 2-2-3 or 3-3-4 pattern; extra "padding" characters allowed for clarity.
8. Prosody rules: 13 rhyme categories (十三辙); Mandarin tones 1–2 are *píng* (level), 3–4 are *zè* (oblique); **the closing line of every couplet ends on a level tone**; *zè* characters take lower/shorter melodic treatment, *píng* characters higher/longer/ornamented.
9. Compose the vocal line so the contour *follows the speech tones* — 依字行腔 ("make melody according to the characters"); the same lyrics can be re-set differently by different singers/schools (improvisation within the frame). Sing at the top of the range (high pitch is a positive aesthetic value; a majority of arias span "an octave and a fifth"); ideal timbre a "controlled nasal tone", vibrato slower and wider than Western; the aesthetic slogan: 字正腔圆 *zì zhèng qiāng yuán* ("characters accurate, melody round").
10. Ornament with 润腔 *rùnqiāng* (vocal embellishment: appoggiaturas, shakes, portamento, 哭腔 "crying-tone" for grief — Cheng Yanqiu's specialty), chosen by **role type** 行当: 生 *shēng* (male: lǎoshēng old male in natural voice with 脑后音 back-of-head resonance; xiǎoshēng young male in falsetto), 旦 *dàn* (female: qīngyī/huādàn/lǎodàn in 小嗓 falsetto), 净 *jìng* (painted face, powerful chest voice, wide vibrato), 丑 *chòu* (clown: mostly spoken 数板 *shùbǎn*, licensed to improvise, orchestra follows even an unscripted folk song).

### Stage 4 — co-compose the jinghu accompaniment (托腔保调)
11. The *qínshī* writes/realizes the fiddle part as **heterophony**: follows the vocal contour but diverges in pitch and density — typically **more notes per measure than the singer, an octave lower**; techniques: 托 *tuō* (support the contour), 裹 *guǒ* (wrap it in denser passing figuration), 垫 *diàn* (pad the gaps), 补 *bǔ* (mend phrase ends).
12. Insert 过门 *guòmén* — instrumental interludes between vocal phrases (and 垫头 *diàntóu* fill-ins at phrase ends); these are fixed per mode+bǎnshì family but personalized: "the jinghu player adopts their own signature version of the song's melody" and **must adapt live to the singer's spontaneous ornaments** (Wichmann 1991: an "instinctive ability to change their performance without warning").
13. The whole accompaniment ensemble is small: jinghu (lead melodic), yueqin (plucked lute, fills), plus percussion — daluo (big gong), xiaoluo (small gong), naobo (cymbals) — conducted by the drum-and-clapper player.

### Stage 5 — lay the percussion score (锣鼓经)
14. Set the 锣鼓点 *luógǔdiǎn* patterns for each dramatic beat; the mnemonic syllables map to instruments: 仓 cang = big gong crash (often + cymbals), 台 tai = small gong, 才/七 cai/qi = cymbals, 倾 qing = light gong, 匡 kuang = muffled gong, 空 kong = choked, 大/达 da = drum single stroke, 八/崩 ba/beng = drum double stroke, 嘟 du = drum roll, 扎/衣 yi = clapper strike, 隆咚 longdong = drum heavy-weak.
15. Standard patterns include 四击头 *sìjítóu* (four-stroke, fixed-action punctuation), 急急风 *jíjífēng* ("hurried wind" — battle/martial rush), 冲头 *chōngtóu*, 长锤 *chángchuí*, 夺头 *duótóu* (the classic pattern that leads INTO an aria), 乱锤 *luànchuí*, 阴锣 *yīnluó*, 水底鱼 *shuǐdǐyú*. **Up to 48 entrance patterns identify the entering character's rank and personality** (Guy 1990). Percussion cues the 板式 and tempo, punctuates 身段 (body movement), imitates nature (thunder, waves), and accompanies both 唱腔 and 念白 (recitation).

### Stage 6 — assemble the scene & fix the work
16. Interleave aria sections with 曲牌 *qǔpái* (fixed instrumental tunes) for atmosphere: 水龙吟 "Water Dragon Tune" (arrival of an important person), 急三枪 "Triple Thrust" (feast/banquet), 小开门 (entrance/movement), 哭皇天 (mourning), 八岔, 柳摇金, 傍妆台, and the famous jinghu showpiece 夜深沉 "Deep Night" (used in *Farewell My Concubine*'s sword dance).
17. Because jingju is actor-centred, the "composer" is a **team**: singer proposes/ornaments, *qínshī* co-writes the fiddle line, *gǔshī* sets the percussion, the 导演/编剧 (director/playwright) sets the dramatic frame; in the *yàngbǎnxì* era (1960s–70s) dedicated 唱腔设计 composers (e.g., the teams behind *Taking Tiger Mountain by Strategy*, whose famous opening 导板 "穿林海跨雪原气冲霄汉" is a model-work landmark) formalized this into a written craft. The result is fixed by performance tradition, not by a single autograph score.

## 4. Practitioner examples

- **Tan Xinpei (谭鑫培, 1847–1917)** — "king of laosheng"; consolidated xipi/erhuang laosheng singing into the canonical repertoire; his accompanist 梅雨田 Mei Yutian co-created the qínshī craft (one of the "four great fiddlers").
- **Mei Lanfang (梅兰芳, 1894–1961)** — greatest qīngyī/dan; with qínshī 徐兰沅 Xu Lanyuan and 王少卿 Wang Shaoqing (who introduced the *erhu* into dan accompaniment in 1923) he reshaped dan arias and staged *The Drunken Concubine* (贵妃醉酒, a 四平调 aria — a 吹腔-derived tune type outside the strict xipi/erhuang pair) and *Farewell My Concubine* (霸王别姬, whose sword-dance is set to the jinghu qǔpái 夜深沉).
- **Cheng Yanqiu (程砚秋)** — 程派, famous for 哭腔 (crying-tone ornament) and dark, revisioned erhuang melodies; each school's aria settings differ — the same lyrics get different 依字行腔 by school.
- **Yu Shuyan (余叔岩)** — laosheng school built on 脑后音 (back-of-head resonance), "空灵, 韵味绵长"; students include 孟小冬 Meng Xiaodong (female laosheng).
- **Yang Baosen (杨宝森) / Ma Lianliang (马连良)** — laosheng schools with their house qínshī 杨宝忠 and 李慕良 respectively; 李慕良 Li Muliang is also a composer of new arias (新编唱腔) — the qínshī-as-composer model.
- **Modern model works (样板戏)** — *Taking Tiger Mountain by Strategy* (智取威虎山) 1960s: the opening 导板 "穿林海跨雪原气冲霄汉" is a textbook 二黄导板 → orchestral expansion of the old frame; *Shajiabang* etc. show the same mode/bǎnshì/luógujīng machinery inside a modern orchestra.
- **Contemporary exponents** — 于魁智, 李胜素 (China National Peking Opera Company), 王珮瑜 (Yu-school laosheng, "the little old man").
- **Scholarship** — Elizabeth Wichmann (*Listening to Theatre*, 1991: text-setting, timbre, heterophonic accompaniment), Huang Jinpei (1989: xipi/erhuang comparative structure), Dolores Menstell Hsu (1964: musical elements incl. the drum-conductor), Nancy Guy (1990: qǔpái and the 48 entrance patterns).

## 5. UnitMatrix mapping

### Voices
- **V0 = Vocal lead (aria, 唱腔)** — the composed melody: role-typed register (natural/falsetto), 依字行腔 contour, ornament vocabulary per 行当; carries the text with 板眼-aligned phrase ends.
- **V1 = Jinghu (fiddle, 托腔保调)** — heterophonic co-line: contour-following with denser figuration (2–4× V0 note density), an octave lower; 过门 interludes between vocal phrases; must land each section's 落音 together with V0.
- **V2 = Yueqin (plucked lute)** — chordal/pizzicato fills, rhythmic reinforcement of the 板 (downbeat) grid.
- **V3 = Bangu + Ban (drum master / conductor)** — the 板眼 timekeeper: clapper (ban) on 板 (strong beats), drum on 眼 (weak beats); tempo authority per section (this voice embodies the conductor function, like the Gnawa *maâlem*'s steering role in HC-081).
- **V4 = Luogu (gong/cymbal percussion)** — the 锣鼓经 score: pattern dictionary (四击头, 急急风, 冲头, 长锤, 夺头, 乱锤, 阴锣, 水底鱼…), each pattern a syllable string (仓才仓才…) mapped to daluo/xiaoluo/naobo hits; entrance patterns role-typed; 夺头 cues aria entries.
- **V5 = Metadata / dramaturgy layer** — per-section tags: mode (xipi / erhuang / fan-erhuang / fan-xipi), bǎnshì name, meter (4/4, 2/4, 1/4, free), role type, 落音 cadence degrees, luógujīng syllable transcription, qǔpái label, emotion/dramatic function.

### Sections
Columns = the **bǎnshì chain**, the aria's rhythmic architecture (one column per pattern), each subdivided into vocal-phrase / 过门 / percussion cells:
S0 导板 (free lead-in, one line) → S1 回龙 (link) → S2 慢板 (4/4, emotional centre) → S3 原板 (2/4) → S4 流水/快板 (1/4, climax) → S5 散板 (free close); martial-scene variants swap interior columns for 急急风-driven action blocks; aria preceded by a 夺头 percussion column and 曲牌 columns (e.g. 水龙吟 for a VIP entrance) as needed. Every voice's units are padded to the full column length — zero-drift invariant.

### Rules to encode
1. **Mode constraint (V0/V1/V5):** xipi pitch inventory with la–mi (A–E) jinghu frame, bright mi/do emphasis; erhuang with sol–re (D–A), dark sol/re emphasis; fan forms = transposed (fan erhuang do–sol G–D). Pentatonic core; no functional harmony — vertical sonorities incidental (HC-003-like rule). Encode as allowed pitch-class sets + 落音 (cadence degree) table per mode+bǎnshì.
2. **Bǎnshì grid (V3/V5):** meter map — 慢板/三眼 = 4/4, 原板/二六 = 2/4, 流水/快板/滚板 = 1/4, 导板/散板/摇板 = free voice over (yaoban: strict 1/4 orchestra grid); 板 on strong beats, 眼 on weak. Tempo ladder per column (accelerating chain).
3. **Couplet prosody (V0):** 7- or 10-char lines, dǒu pauses 2-2-3 / 3-3-4; píng tones (1,2) → higher/longer/ornamented, zè (3,4) → lower/shorter; couplet closing line ends on a level tone; 13-辙 rhyme enforced; contour follows speech tones (依字行腔).
4. **落音 skeleton (V0/V1):** each mode+bǎnshì has conventional phrase-cadence degrees — engine must end vocal and jinghu phrases on allowed 落音, converging at column boundaries.
5. **Jinghu heterophony (V1):** derived from V0 by contour-follow + densification (2–4× density, octave lower) + 过门 fills at phrase ends; V1 cadences with V0; personalize per seed ("signature version").
6. **Luógujīng (V4):** pattern dictionary as syllable→instrument maps; entrance pattern chosen by role rank/personality tag; 夺头 inserted before aria columns; percussion cells padded to section length; jitter low (percussion is the time authority alongside V3).
7. **Role-typed ornament (V0/V5):** ornament vocabulary by 行当 (dan falsetto vibrato, laosheng naohouyin timbre flag, jing wide vibrato, chou spoken shuban cells with improvisation license).
8. **Zero-drift gate:** `validate()` must pass — free-rhythm columns (导板/散板) quantized on a fine grid or rubato-padded to full column length (pad pattern from `_unit_from_events`).
9. **Emotion→mode mapping (V5):** xipi = joyous/excited/argumentative; erhuang = lyrical/grief/interior; fan-erhuang = deepest lament — the dramaturgy layer steers the chain.

## 6. Quirks & pitfalls

- **Oral/aural authority:** the aria is co-composed by singer + qínshī in rehearsal and fixed by school tradition, not by one autograph — model the DB entry as the *machinery*, not a fixed score. 依字行腔 means the same text legitimately produces different melodies by school (Yu vs Ma vs Yang laosheng settings).
- **Absolute tuning is flexible:** jinghu open strings are conventionally la–mi (xipi), sol–re (erhuang), do–sol (fan erhuang) — but "key has value only as a technical tool"; singers pitch at the top of their range and accompanists retune between performers. English Wikipedia's "A–D / C–G" for the two modes conflicts with the Chinese 6-3/5-2 convention; use relative degrees.
- **Free-rhythm bǎnshì vs the zero-drift engine:** 导板/散板/摇板 are rubato — grid the orchestra, keep the voice flexible (humanize), and pad everything to column length or `validate()` fails.
- **四平调 (siping diao) and 南梆子 are neither xipi nor erhuang:** famous arias (贵妃醉酒) use the 吹腔-derived 四平调 — don't force every aria into the two-mode frame.
- **The 板眼 grid is the skeleton:** bǎnshì names ARE meters; a "bǎnshì chain" is a tempo/meter plan, not a chord progression — encode as grid + cadence skeleton, never as functional harmony.
- **Percussion leads, not follows:** the luógujīng (esp. 夺头 and entrance patterns) cues the aria and identifies character — in the UnitMatrix it must sit *before* the vocal columns it introduces.
- **Role register is compositional data:** assigning the wrong 行当 register (dan falsetto vs laosheng natural) breaks the idiom more than any single pitch.
