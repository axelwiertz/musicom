## 1. What It Is

Fado is Lisbon's urban song tradition, born in the early 19th century in the working-class neighbourhoods of Mouraria, Alfama, and Bairro Alto. Its most distinctive architectural feature — unique among world song traditions — is the **split-author principle**: the melody is a fixed "mother melody" from a shared repertoire of ~200 known tunes, while the poem (lyric) is freely chosen by the fadista (singer) each performance. The same melody can carry a hundred different poems over a career. This separation of fixed music × free text is the tradition's defining craft.

Instrumentally, fado is anchored by the **guitarra portuguesa** (Portuguese guitar) — a pear-shaped 12-string (6-course) cittern — and the **viola** (classical/Spanish guitar). The guitarra plays a recurring melodic-harmonic motif (the accompaniment pattern IS the composition), while the viola provides steady harmonic rhythm and bass reinforcement. No percussion is used in traditional fado castiço.

## 3. Craft Procedure (Step-by-Step)

### Phase 1: Fix the Fado Form (Castiço)

1. **Choose the melodic fado.** Select from ~200 known traditional melody types, each with a recognized name: Fado Menor, Fado Corrido, Fado Mouraria, Fado Bailado, Fado Cravo, Fado Pajem, Fado Vitória, Fado Lopes, Fado Alberto, Fado Modesto, etc.
2. **Determine the tonal-harmonic axis:**
   - **Fado Menor** — minor key (typically Dm), slow/moderate tempo (♩≈80–100), sad/melancholic character, I–V harmonic base (Dm–A7 or root movement).
   - **Fado Corrido** — major key, fast tempo (♩≈120–140), cheerful/lively, I–V scheme.
   - **Fado Mouraria** — major key, moderate tempo (♩≈100–120), serene longing, I–V scheme with Allegro marking.
3. **Fix metre and duration.** All traditional fados are in 4/4 with a fixed-length harmonic cycle (typically 8–16 bars of I–V alternation before the repeat).

### Phase 2: Select the Poem (For Castiço)

4. **Choose the poetic genre.** Common structures:
   - **Quadra** (quatrain) — 4 lines × 7 syllables — the most basic, used by Fado Menor.
   - **Quintilha** — 5 lines.
   - **Sextilha** — 6 lines.
   - **Décima** — 10 lines.
   - **Setessílabo** — 7-syllable lines.
   - **Decassílabo** — 10-syllable lines.
5. **Match syllable count to melodic phrase length.** The fadista must fit the poem's metrical scheme to the melody's structure. A mismatch breaks the performance.
6. **Interpret the poem's emotional content** to guide phrasing, register, and ornamentation. Texts are often by known poets (erudite poetry) or improvised; themes include *saudade* (longing), love, loss, the sea, and Lisbon's streets.

### Phase 3: Guitarra Accompaniment Craft

7. **Set tuning.** Modern Lisbon tuning for the 12-string guitarra (6 courses): D A B E A D (low→high). Older recordings use "F tuning" (F C D g c d).
8. **Compose/recall the fixed accompaniment motif.** The hallmark of fado castiço: a short melodic-harmonic motif cycled repeatedly over I–V, played as a continuous arpeggio pattern with subtle variations. The accompanist does NOT write a separate accompaniment — the recurring motif IS the accompaniment.
9. **Right-hand technique** (with unhas — fingerpicks on index finger):
   - **Rasgado** — strumming across courses (flamenco-like)
   - **Arpejo** — rolled-chord fingerpicking
   - **Contracanto** — counter-melodic line that fills the vocalist's phrase-end gaps
   - **Tremolo** — rapid repeated notes
10. **Lay the viola foundation.** On-beat chord changes, bass-root reinforcement, steady rhythmic pulse.

### Phase 4: Fadista Performance Craft (Estilação)

11. **State the melody plainly.** The first iteration is a clear, unornamented statement of the mother melody.
12. **Estilar (ornament the melody).** On each subsequent verse iteration, the fadista:
    - Bends pitch (portamento on key words)
    - Stretches/compresses syllables rhythmically (tempo rubato)
    - Adds appoggiaturas, mordents, passing notes around the fixed skeleton
    - Varies dynamics dramatically (p→f on climactic words)
    - Alternates chest/head register
13. **Signal the song's close.** Every fado ends with a vocal climax — the final verse/refrain is repeated at higher intensity, and the guitars punctuate with a V–I cadential "full stop" (two-chord exclamation mark).
14. **Desgarrada (optional duel).** Two fadistas trade improvised lines on the same melody, trying to out-wit each other, with audience complicity.

### Phase 5: Fado-Canção (Alternative Path)

For composer-created fado-canção:
1. Select key and chord palette (may extend to ii–V–I, chromatic mediants)
2. Compose verse-and-chorus (estrofe–refrão) structure
3. Write a specific melody permanently fixed to a specific lyric
4. May orchestrate (piano, double bass, string section)
5. Fadista still applies estilação within the composed frame

## 4. Practitioner Examples

| Practitioner | Role | Key Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| **Alfredo Marceneiro** (1888–1982) | Fadista & melody creator | "Rei do Fado" (King of Fado). Composed Fado Bailado, Fado Cravo, Fado Pajem, Fado Vitória (~25 canonical melodies). Joiner by trade; never left Lisbon. His melodies became standard repertoire. |
| **Amália Rodrigues** (1920–1999) | Fadista (castiço & canção) | The global icon. Mastered both branches; made fado an international art form. Iconic recordings: *Lágrima*, *Gaivota*, *Barco Negro*, *Uma Casa Portuguesa*, *Ai Mouraria*. |
| **Carlos do Carmo** (1945–2021) | Fadista | Son of Lucília do Carmo; major interpreter. Fado Menor recording: *Por Morrer uma Andorinha*. |
| **Lucília do Carmo** | Fadista | Fado Mouraria recording: *Maria Madalena*. |
| **Maria Teresa de Noronha** | Fadista | Fado Corrido: *Corrido em Cinco Estilos*. |
| **Armandinho** (1897–1977) | Guitarrista | Legendary Portuguese guitar player; performed with Marceneiro. |
| **António Chainho** (b. 1945) | Guitarrista | Modern master of contracanto and guitarrada composition. |
| **José Nunes** | Guitarrista | Renowned for variations on Fado Lopes. |
| **Manoel Gomes** | Pedagogue | Author of *A Guitarra Portuguesa: Método Fácil* (1929) — first systematised method. |
| **Mariza** (b. 1973) | Fadista (contemporary) | Brought fado to 21st-century global audiences. |
| **Ana Moura** (b. 1980) | Fadista (contemporary) | Modern interpreter; blends fado with world music influences. |
| **Rui Vieira Nery** | Historian | Definitive *A History of Portuguese Fado* (2012). |

### Sections (Fado Evening Set Architecture)

| Section | Voice Mask | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **S0 — Opening Fado-Canção** | All voices (V0+V2+V3 + optional V4/V5) | Audience-recognisable melody settles the room. |
| **S1 — Fado Castiço Block** | V0+V2+V3 (+V1 optional) | Maximum estilação freedom; "conversation" between voice and guitarra. |
| **S2 — Marcha/Folclore Exhale** | All + optional handclaps | Rhythm-brightener; audience claps along. Minor→Major key shift. |
| **S3 — Second Castiço Block** | V0+V2+V3 (+V1) | Emotional intensification; desgarrada duel possible. |
| **S4 — Closing Fado-Canção** | All | High emotional climax; V–I guitar punctuation; audience carries out. |

### Rules (UnitMatrix constraints encoding the fado craft)

| # | Rule | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Motif invariance** | V0's accompaniment cycle repeats continuously — a break IS the song's end. The guitarra never has rests during the song. |
| 2 | **I–V harmonic lock** | Castiço harmony only alternates between tonic and dominant. No other chords. (Fado-canção may extend.) |
| 3 | **Free-verse slot** | V2's lyric text is a fill-in: any poem that fits the melody's phrase structure and syllable count. |
| 4 | **Estilação filter** | V2 ornament must preserve the skeletal contour of the mother melody. No wholesale recomposition. Ornamentation gradient rises per verse iteration. |
| 5 | **Vocal climax rule** | Every song's final stanza/refrain is repeated at higher intensity (louder, higher register, more rubato), followed by V0+V3 V–I cadential punctuation. |
| 6 | **Guitarra solo gate (guitarrada)** | In instrumental numbers, V2 is silent; V0+V1+V3+V4 have full freedom to improvise contracanto variations. |
| 7 | **Desgarrada interlock** | Two fadistas: strict alternation. Line A → guitarra response → Line B → guitarra response. No overlap. |
| 8 | **Call-response dialogue** | V0 (guitarra) and V2 (voice) interlock: V2 phrase ends → V0 contracanto fills the gap. |
| 9 | **No percussion rule (castiço)** | No drums. Only guitarra + viola. (Marcha/folclore pieces may add handclaps.) |
| 10 | **Poem-constrained prosody** | The poem's syllable count must match the melody's phrase structure exactly — failure = un-singable. |

## 8. Quirks & Pitfalls

- **Split-author confusion**: "Fado Menor" is a melody template, NOT a song title. Beginners may mistake the form name for the piece.
- **~200 mother melodies**: Learning the repertoire is the primary task for any aspiring fadista or accompanist. The canon is orally transmitted within the fado house community — no single standardised songbook.
- **Estilação vs deviation**: The fadista must ornament without destroying recognisability. Too little ornament = dull; too much = audience can't identify the fado. The line is social-contextual, not written.
- **Guitarra tuning variance**: Older recordings use "F tuning" (F C D g c d); modern Lisbon tuning is D A B E A D. Accompanists must recognise both. Coimbra fado uses a different tuning with rest-stroke technique.
- **"Fado is not sad"**: Despite the *saudade* stereotype, fado castiço includes profoundly joyful forms (Fado Corrido, Fado Bailado). The melancholic reputation comes from fado-canção and Amália's iconic repertoire.
- **Marcha/folclore blur**: These non-fado forms are sung in fado houses and treated with fado phrasing, but they are separate genres. Purists distinguish sharply.
- **Desgarrada limits**: The duel requires encyclopedic poem knowledge — the fadista must instantly recall and sing a fitting poem on any topic the previous singer sets.
