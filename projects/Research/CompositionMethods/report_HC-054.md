## Method Overview

Bluegrass Ensemble Arrangement is a **rotating-break song architecture** for acoustic string band. Born from Bill Monroe's Blue Grass Boys (1945) and crystallized when Earl Scruggs joined on 5-string banjo (October 1945), it defines the "classic bluegrass" sound: five acoustic instruments (banjo, fiddle, mandolin, guitar, upright bass) executing complementary rhythmic roles over a strophic song form, with instrumental breaks rotating between lead players, and a three-part vocal harmony stack (lead–tenor–baritone) singing only chord tones.

## Craft Procedure (how the human does it)

### 1. Song Form & Harmonic Grid
- Compose strophic AABA/ABAB song form (verse 8 bars, chorus 8 bars, optional bridge 8 bars).
- Fix key: open-string friendly (G, C, D, A). Banjo tuned gDGBD, fiddle GDAE, mandolin GDAE, guitar standard, bass EADG.
- Write chord progression: I–IV–V dominate. Minor vi and flat-VII (bVII) common in modal/dark tunes.
- Tempo: 110–160 BPM up-tempo; 70–100 BPM ballad/waltz (3/4 or 3/2 time).

### 2. Bass — Root-5 Walk (Voice 0)
- Upright bass plays quarter-note walk: root on beats 1 & 3, passing tones on 2 & 4.
- Never stops; never plays chords.
- Anticipates beat slightly (drive feel), never drags.
- G-run pattern on turnarounds in G: G–F♯–E–D–B–G (descending scalar walk).

### 3. Guitar — Boom-Chuck + G-Run (Voice 1)
- Bass notes on downbeats (1 & 3), strummed brush on offbeats (2 & 4).
- On turnarounds: descending G-run cliché.
- Fills between vocal phrases with flatpicked runs (single-note).
- Capo used to shift key while retaining open-string voicings.

### 4. Mandolin — Chop + Fills (Voice 2)
- Percussive muted chord strike on beats 2 & 4 (snare-drum backbeat function).
- Called "the chop": left hand lightly frets chord shape, right hand strikes with pick and immediately mutes.
- Between vocal phrases: flatpicked eighth-note runs fills the gap.
- Solo break: single-note melodic line, often rapid scalar runs.

### 5. Banjo — Scruggs-Style Rolls (Voice 3)
- 5-string banjo in open G tuning (gDGBD), three-finger picking (thumb–index–middle).
- **Roll vocabulary** (the core compositional device):
  - **Forward roll:** T–I–M–T–I–M–T–I (descending across strings)
  - **Backward roll:** M–I–T–M–I–T–M–I (ascending across strings)
  - **Forward-reverse roll:** T–I–M–T–M–I–T–M (hybrid)
  - **Mixed/alternating rolls:** free combination anchored to chord positions
- **Solo/break:** melody paraphrased using roll vocabulary (Scruggs style) — not exact melody, but rolling around it. Later "melodic banjo" (Bill Keith) played scalar versions.
- **Backup:** rolling fills between vocal phrases; during vocals, vamp by cross-picking chord positions or "chop" by stopping strings.

### 6. Fiddle — Double-Stop Backup + Melodic Break (Voice 4)
- **Backup:** double-stops (two-note chord tones) on beats 2&4 mimicking mandolin chop, but held longer (bowed, not choked). Long-bow sustained harmony under vocals.
- **Break:** sings the vocal melody with bowing ornaments (shuffle bowing, double shuffles, slides, grace notes, open-string drones).
- **Twin fiddle:** second fiddle harmonizes in parallel 3rds above or 6ths below.

### 7. Vocal Harmony Stack
The defining element of bluegrass arranging:

- **Lead** (middle voice): sings the composed melody.
- **Tenor** (high voice): chord tone immediately above lead. Usually a 3rd above; when lead is on the 5th of chord, tenor goes up a 4th to the root (octave above bass).
- **Baritone** (low voice): remaining chord tone below lead. Usually a 3rd or 6th below.
- **Rule:** All three voices sing chord tones ONLY (root, 3rd, 5th). No passing tones, no non-chord tones. Occasional ♭7 for blues color. No two voices sing the same pitch class.
- **Stack variants:**
  - Standard trio: baritone–lead–tenor (low→high)
  - High baritone trio (Stanley Brothers): lead–tenor–high baritone (baritone moves an octave above)
  - High lead trio (Osborne Brothers): low tenor–baritone–lead (lead on top)
- **Duets:** tenor above (brother duet sound — Monroe Brothers, Louvin Brothers).
- **Quartets:** add bass voice below baritone for gospel-derived quartet.

### 8. Break Rotation & Arrangement Arc
Standard bluegrass song layout:

| Section | Bars | What Happens |
|---|---|---|
| S0 Kickoff | 2–4 | Lead instrument states melody head (fiddle or banjo) |
| S1 Verse 1 | 8 | Lead vocal + full rhythm section; banjo fills between phrases |
| S2 Chorus | 8 | Vocal trio harmony enters; full drive |
| S3 Break 1 (banjo) | 8–16 | Banjo solo, melody paraphrase + rolls; rhythm section comps |
| S4 Verse 2 | 8 | Same structure, different fills (fiddle takes fills now) |
| S5 Chorus | 8 | Trio harmony, drive at maximum |
| S6 Break 2 (fiddle) | 8–16 | Fiddle solo, melodic with bow ornaments |
| S7 Bridge (optional) | 8 | Modal shift or breakdown feel (half-time rhythm) |
| S8 Final Chorus | 8 | Trio, full density, climactic |
| S9 Tag/Outro | 2–4 | G-run or V–I hit after a pause |

### 9. Drive Feel Execution
- All instruments anticipate the beat subtly (push feel, never relaxed or behind).
- Mandolin chop on 2&4 creates backbeat snare.
- Bass root-walk slightly ahead of the click.
- Banjo rolls are metronomic eighth-notes — no swing triplet feel.
- Monroe insisted sidemen "play with great precision and subtly anticipate the beat."

## Quirks & Pitfalls

1. **Banjo roll ≠ melody**: Scruggs-style banjo solos are roll patterns that *paraphrase* the melody, not play it note-for-note. A straight transcription of the vocal melody onto banjo sounds wrong — rolls must fit the right-hand picking pattern. Melodic banjo (Bill Keith) solved this by replacing open strings with scale positions.
2. **Vocal chord-tone rule is non-negotiable**: Beginners often sing a fixed interval (always a 3rd above). Bluegrass harmony shifts between 3rds and 4ths depending on chord position. Singing a fixed interval hits wrong notes.
3. **Mandolin chop is NOT a chord**: The left hand frets a chord shape but the right hand strikes AND mutes in one motion. If the note sustains, it ruins the percussive backbeat effect.
4. **No drums**: Bluegrass uses mandolin chop as snare backbeat and bass foot as kick. Adding a drum kit changes the genre fundamentally.
5. **Drive feel ≠ rushing**: The subtle anticipation is uniform across the band. If only one instrument pushes ahead while others play on the beat, it sounds like rushing. Monroe drilled this relentlessly.
6. **No vibrato on vocal harmony**: Classical vibrato on bluegrass harmony lines sounds out of place. Straight, clear, chord-tone-focused singing is the norm.
7. **G-run is key-specific**: The classic G-run only works in open-position G. In C or D, guitarists use different turnaround clichés.
8. **Not all instruments solo**: In classic bluegrass, the guitar rarely takes a break (lead guitar solos are a post-1970s innovation from Tony Rice and later). The expected rotation is banjo→fiddle→mandolin.
9. **Open strings are tools**: Banjo and fiddle players intentionally use open strings as drones within solos — unlike classical playing where open strings are avoided.
10. **Form is loose in performance**: While the arrangement script is fixed (kickoff, verse, chorus, breaks, tag), actual bluegrass performances stretch breaks to multiple choruses, trade fours between soloists, and modulate up a key for the final chorus.
