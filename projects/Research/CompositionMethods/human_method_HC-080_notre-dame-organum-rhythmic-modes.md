## What it is

Organum is the earliest Western practice of adding voices to plainchant: one voice sings the chant (*vox principalis*, later called **tenor**, from Latin *tenere* "to hold"), while an added voice (*vox organalis*, the "vining voice") weaves around it. The Notre-Dame school turned an improviser's craft into **written composition**: Léonin compiled the *Magnus Liber Organi de Gradali et Antiphonario* (the first large-scale corpus attributable to a named composer, written "to prolong the divine service"), and Pérotin revised it, replacing free-rhythm passages with **measured discant clausulae**, raising the texture to three and four voices, and inventing the first coherent system of rhythmic notation in Western music — the **six rhythmic modes**, derived from the metric feet of classical poetry.

This method is the historical bridge between monophonic chant and all later polyphonic craft (it seeds the motet — cross-reference HC-074 isorhythmic motet, which recycles this tenor-based architecture a century later).

## The craft procedure — how a human composer did it, step by step

### Stage 0 — the chant source
1. Choose a pre-existing liturgical chant for a high feast (Gradual, Alleluia, Responsory, Benedicamus Domino — e.g. *Viderunt omnes*, *Sederunt principes*, *Alleluia Nativitas*). The chant is never invented: it is quoted as the **cantus firmus**.
2. Decide which portions are set polyphonically (*organaliter* — sung by solo cantors) and which remain monophonic (*choraliter* — sung by the choir). In an Alleluia only the opening is set; the long *Jubilus* is left to the choir. This creates the work's outer structure.

### Stage 1 — organum purum (florid organum over sustained tenor)
3. Take the tenor note-by-note through the syllabic, non-modal stretches of the chant: each chant note becomes a **long, unmeasured pedal point** ("the tenor pauses if a dissonance appears" — Anonymous IV).
4. Compose the *duplum* (second voice) against it: a florid, freely-rhythmed melismatic line in ligatures — up to ~40 notes per tenor note (Anonymous IV: the duplum "had to be sung fast… so the text progressed very slowly"). This is why Pérotin later abbreviated it.
5. Govern the vertical world: begin and end phrases on consonances (unison, fifth, octave); approach cadential consonances from above (7–8, 5–8 over the tenor tone); the fourth is consonant only as a compound (octave + fourth); octave doubling (*magadization*) is acceptable when men and boys sing together.
6. Drape the duplum so it "explores the harmonious interplay with the tenor, building up to a change of harmony at the end of a melisma where another syllable is produced at a different pitch."

### Stage 2 — discantus (measured note-against-note organum)
7. Where the chant becomes melismatic (ligatures), switch regime: **both** voices become measured and modal. The tenor abandons free longs and moves in **mode 5** (all longs, in groups of three — spondee), while the duplum moves in **mode 1** (long–short, trochee) or another upper-voice mode.
8. The sections where both voices run in modal rhythm are **clausulae** (puncta) — the rhythmic heart of the piece. Modal rhythm freed music "from its ties to text."
9. Close each discant section with a **copula**: the tenor sustains the penultimate (or last) tone while the duplum returns to a florid cadential flourish ending on a consonance. (Garlandia: copula "is between organum and discant.")
10. Alternate freely: *organaliter* [organum purum → copula → discant → copula → …] with the closing phrase *choraliter* (monophonic chant). In Léonin's *Benedicamus Domino*, "Benedicamus" is set as florid organum over sustained tenor; "Domino" runs as discant in the rhythmic modes, finalized by a florid cadence over a sustained tenor.

### Stage 3 — the rhythmic modes (the notation craft)
11. Notate rhythm by **ligature patterns**, not note shapes — a singer reads which of the six modes a passage is in from the stereotyped grouping of joined notes, then continues in that mode to the cadence:
    - Mode 1 (trochee, long–short): ligature groups 3,2,2,2… — ♩. ♪ barred in 3/8 or 6/8.
    - Mode 2 (iamb, short–long): 2,2,2…3.
    - Mode 3 (dactyl, long–short–short): 1,3,3,3… — ♩. ♪ ♩ in 6/8.
    - Mode 4 (anapaest, short–short–long): 3,3,3…1 (rare).
    - Mode 5 (spondee, long–long): 3,3,3,3… — tenor only, normally in groups of three.
    - Mode 6 (tribrach, short–short–short): 4,3,3,3… — upper voice.
12. All modes obey the **ternary principle** (subdivisions divisible by 3 — theologically glossed as the Trinity, practically a flexible ternary swing).
13. Vary density with *fractio modi* (break a long into shorter units — insert a larger-than-usual ligature or special signs) and *extensio modi* (combine two units into one — insert an unligated long); ornament with the **plica** (an added breve on a weak beat — upper/lower neighbor if the surrounding notes agree, passing tone if they are a third apart) and the **climacus/currentes** (rapid descending figure; last note longa, second-last breve, the rest share a longa's space).
14. Organize modal phrases into **ordines**: a phrase of one or more statements of the mode's pattern ending in a rest; perfect ordines end with the first note of the pattern + a rest substituting the second half, and predominate in practice.

### Stage 4 — expanding the texture (Pérotin's revision)
15. Replace organum purum passages with **discant clausulae**: Pérotin "abridged" the Magnus Liber (*abbreviavit*) — some 154 clausulae attributed to him — introducing varied tenor modes and new duplum lines, increasing rhythmic organization and diversity. The manuscripts preserve **462 clausulae**, many as substitute/replacement sections (the same clausula appears in variant settings: *Domino*, *Et gaudebit*…).
16. Compose **organum triplum and quadruplum**: with 3–4 voices, organum purum is impossible — *all* parts must be modal and organized by the rhythmic modes. Pérotin's *Viderunt omnes* (Christmas 1198) and *Sederunt principes* (St Stephen 1199, for the dedication of a new wing of Notre-Dame) are the only large-scale quadrupla: massive tenor pedal points in mode 5 sustain long swings between consonant harmonies while the three upper voices interlock in mode 1, using repetition and voice-exchange figures (Anonymous IV's *colores armonicae artis* — "abundance of striking musical embellishments").
17. Compose **conductus** in the same modal craft when the text is new (not chant): note-against-note (*cum littera* texted / *sine littera* melismatic sections), three- and four-part conductus necessarily in discant style (*Salvatoris hodie*, *Dum sigillum summi Patris*, monophonic *Beata viscera*).
18. **Seed the motet**: take a discant clausula and supply the upper part with a new text — the *motellus* — so that while the tenor sings a single chant syllable, the upper voice pronounces several words; the first polytextual pieces, and the birth of the motet.

### Stage 5 — the working loop
19. Compose, then perform in the liturgy; the surviving sources (MS F Florence Pluteo 29.1 — 1,023 compositions; W1 St Andrews; W2) show the repertory was continuously re-edited, substituted, and diffused across Europe — composition as a living, revisable practice.

## Practitioner examples

- **Léonin** (fl. 1150s–d. c. 1201) — *magister cantus* of Notre-Dame; compiler of the *Magnus Liber Organi*; the two-part *Viderunt omnes* (tenor drone + florid duplum), *Benedicamus Domino* (organum purum → discant → florid cadence); Anonymous IV: *optimus organista* ("best composer of organum").
- **Pérotin** (fl. c. 1200) — *optimus discantor*; quadrupla *Viderunt omnes* (1198) and *Sederunt principes* (1199); tripla *Alleluia Nativitas*, *Alleluia Posui adiutorium*; ~154 clausulae; conducti *Salvatoris hodie*, *Dum sigillum summi Patris*, *Beata viscera*; texts by Philippe le Chancelier; commissioned by Bishop Odo (Eudes) de Sully's 1198–99 edicts calling for *organa tripla vel quadrupla*.
- **Anonymous IV** (English student, c. 1275) — the chronicler whose report (including "the tenor pauses if a dissonance appears", the *colores* of the quadrupla, and the Leonin/Pérotin attributions) is our primary source.
- **Johannes de Garlandia** (fl. c. 1240–80) — *De mensurabili musica* codified the three polyphonic species (organum, copula, discant) and the modal system.
- **Parallel/adjacent traditions**: *Musica enchiriadis* parallel organum (9th c.), Aquitanian St. Martial school (Codex Calixtinus), English discant (3-voice parallel first-inversion writing — distinct from fauxbourdon).
- **Modern torchbearers** (performance practice, not composition): Hilliard Ensemble, Deller Consort, Tonus Peregrinus; Kronos Quartet's string-quartet arrangement of *Viderunt omnes*.

## UnitMatrix integration (Musicom engine)

### Voices
- **V0 = Tenor / cantus firmus spine** — the invariant chant quotation, the piece's identity anchor. In organum purum cells its notes are maximally elongated (whole-section pedals); in discant cells it runs mode-5 all-longs. Never composed, always quoted.
- **V1 = Duplum** (vox organalis) — the composed voice: florid melismatic cells over pedals (purum), mode-1 modal cells (discant), copula cadence flourishes.
- **V2 = Triplum** (organum triplum/quadruplum) — second organal voice, higher register, modal (mode 1 or 6), voice-exchange partner of V1.
- **V3 = Quadruplum** — highest voice, modal, completes the 4-voice "swings between consecutive harmonies."
- **V4 = Choraliter/performance layer** (optional metadata voice) — tags per column: `purum / copula / discant / choraliter`, mode number, syllable underlay, ordo/rest positions.

### Sections
Columns = the chant's textual units (syllables/words of the tenor) plus the formal alternation:
- S0 organaliter opening → S1 discant clausula → S2 copula → S3 next chant word (purum) → S4 discant → … → Sn choraliter close (monophonic chant, V0 alone, or unison).
- Equivalently: columns may be clausula slots (substitution-friendly — the F manuscript shows the same clausula in variant settings), which maps perfectly to UnitMatrix section replacement.

### Rules to encode
1. **Cantus-firmus constraint**: V0 pitch sequence = fixed chant vector; every section's terminal landmark = a chant pitch (each column ends on its tenor note).
2. **Consonance gate** (the harmonic rule): at every column boundary (syllable change / copula cadence) vertical intervals must be unison, fifth, octave, or octave+fourth; approach cadential consonances from a 7–8 or 5–8 motion; if a dissonance would land on a tenor long, the tenor pauses (rest insertion) — the engine's suspension/pedal logic.
3. **Mode assignment**: discant columns → V0 mode-5 cell (longa–longa groups ×3, ternary), V1/V2/V3 mode-1 (or mode-3/6) cells; purum columns → V0 longa pedal spanning full section, V1 unmeasured florid (engine: high-density subdivided cells under a static V0).
4. **Density contrast** (the structural engine): purum 20:1–40:1 note ratio, discant ~2–3:1, choraliter 1:0; Pérotin's quadrupla add voices while keeping V0 slow — equivalent to adding voices with equal-length cells (zero-drift invariant holds because V0 pedals span whole sections).
5. **Ternary rhythm**: all modal cells quantize on 3-divisible grids (6/8 feel — 480 tpb × 3 = triplet-friendly); fractio = subdivide (density operator), extensio = merge (cell-length operator); plica = weak-beat neighbor insertion; ordo = phrase cell + rest at pattern end.
6. **Voice-exchange/repetition figures** (quadrupla *colores*): V1/V2/V3 exchange pitch content between adjacent columns at fixed offsets — implementable as permutation operators on voice-section cells.
7. **Substitutability**: clausulae are replaceable sections — the framework keeps a clausula library per tenor syllable; swapping a cell re-voices the whole (the historical *abbreviavit* workflow).

## Quirks & pitfalls
- **No tempo/fixed rhythm in purum sections**: modern transcriptions (Waite 1954) imposed strict modal rhythm on everything; current scholarship treats purum as flexible declamation. For engine realization, treat purum cells as rubato/loose (humanize layer), discant cells as strict.
- **Mode 5 in the tenor only** — never assign mode 5 to upper voices; mode 6 is upper-voice only; mode 4 is near-theoretical.
- **Ligature ≠ syllable**: modal notation breaks down when syllables change quickly (syllabic notation reported as difficult by Anonymous IV) — keep one syllable per column.
- **The 4th as dissonance**: perfect fourth is consonant only when it is the octave above a tenor note (compound); beware parallel 4ths — the "tenor pauses" rule is the historical escape hatch.
- **Text incomprehensibility**: Pérotin's quadrupla are so diminuted the text is lost — acceptable theatrically, but keep the V4 syllable layer aligned for the engine.
