## 1. Method name & tradition

**Danmono (段物)** — the core sectional-variation form of Japanese koto (sōkyoku, 箏曲) solo composition. *Dan* (段) means "step/column/level"; a danmono piece is a fixed number of **dans**, each an exactly equal-length movement that varies the material of the first. Developed and fixed in the 17th century by the **Yatsuhashi school** (Yatsuhashi Kengyō, 1614–1685, the "Father of Modern Koto") and transmitted through the later **Ikuta** and **Yamada** schools.

- **Tradition / Culture:** Japanese court-adjacent chamber music (sōkyoku) — koto (13-string zither, movable bridges *ji*, finger plectra *tsume*), often in *sankyoku* ensemble with shamisen and shakuhachi; the solo koto version is the canonical one today. Yatsuhashi, a blind *kengyō* (highest rank of the blind musicians' guild), originally a shamisen player, learned the koto from a court musician and was the first to bring the instrument from the court to the general public, adapting the **hirajōshi** tuning and the **insen (In) scale** from shamisen practice. All danmono repertoire was composed by ~1700; repertoire is small (eight pieces) and deliberately uniform.
- **Key figures:** Yatsuhashi Kengyō (Rokudan no Shirabe, Midare, Hachidan — attributed), Kitajima Kengyō, Kurahashi, Yasumura, Tomino Koto, Mitsuhashi (Kumoi Kudan), Mitsuzaki Kengyō (Aki kaze no kyoku); school heads Ikuta Kengyō (Ikuta-ryū, 1656–1688) and Yamada Kengyō (Yamada-ryū, 1757–1817); modern lineage Michio Miyagi (1894–1956). Scholarship: Willem Adriaansz (*Research into the Chronology of Danmono*, Ethnomusicology 11/1, 1967; *A Japanese Procrustean Bed: A Study of the Development of "Danmono"*, JAMS 23, 1970), William P. Malm (*Traditional Japanese Music and Musical Instruments*).

## 3. The craft procedure in detail (how the human does it)

### Stage 0 — apprenticeship, instrument & transmission
1. Learn orally by ear from a master (kengyō tradition); the koto is taught through **tablature + rote imitation** — traditional notation (*kengen-fu* lineage) records **string numbers** (一 二 三 … 十三, one per string), finger strokes, and left-hand signs, NOT absolute pitches; the pitch content lives in the tuning. Blind musicians internalized the repertoire entirely by ear.
2. Master the right-hand plucking with *tsume* (ivory/bamboo plectra on thumb, index, middle) and the left-hand techniques that define the style: **kizuki** (basic finger plucks), **oshiide** (left-hand pressure on the string behind the bridge, bending pitch up mid-resonance — the signature hirajōshi ornament), **hajiki** (snapping release), and bass-register accompaniment strokes on the lower strings.

### Stage 1 — fix the tuning (the tuning IS the composition's pitch field)
3. Choose the piece's tuning — for the danmono core: **hirajōshi** (平調子, from shamisen practice): five-note field with semitone pattern **2–1–4–1–4**; on the koto's D-tuning, open strings give **D–F–G–A–C** (+ octaves across the 13 strings). The melody of *Rokudan* moves on the associated **In/insen scale** (D–E♭–G–A–C family); the missing/in-between degrees are produced by *oshiide* bends, so the tuning constrains everything above it.
4. Variant: **hon-kumoijoshi** tuning for *Kumoi Kudan* ("nine dan in hon-kumoijoshi"); other koto tunings (kokinjoshi, nakazora, etc.) belong to the kumiuta/repertoire outside danmono. Danmono composers reused the same tunings piece to piece — originality was not the goal.

### Stage 2 — compose dan 1 (the theme)
5. Compose the **first dan as the theme statement**: a complete melodic sentence of fixed length — **52 beats** (13 bars of 4/4; Adriaansz counts 104 half-beats — same duration, doubled unit) — slow, long note values, wide registral sweep, built from the tuning's pitch field.
6. Give it a **recognizable opening gesture** (each dan, including the theme, will begin with a recognizable variant of this figure) and end it with the piece's **cadential formula** (a fixed dan-end cadence, typically arriving on the tonic register of the tuning).
7. Optionally precede dan 1 with the **kandō** — a short 4- or 8-beat introduction (in *Rokudan* the first dan itself carries 4 extra beats: 56 vs 52 — the sources differ on whether this is intro or extra dan-beats; either way, first column ≠ dan columns).

### Stage 3 — compose dans 2–N (variation chain, same length, denser figuration)
8. For each further dan, **vary the theme while keeping the length EXACTLY equal** (52 beats) and keeping the theme's structural tones at phrase/cadence points. The variation operators, applied by hand at the instrument:
   - **Rhythmic subdivision** — the engine of the form: dan 1 moves in long values; each dan subdivides more (quarters → 8ths → 16th-note figuration in later dans of *Rokudan*), with figuration density monotonically increasing dan to dan.
   - **Ornamentation** — *oshiide* bends, snaps, and left-hand pitch alterations decorate the skeleton.
   - **Register & figuration exchange** — melody climbs or drops; accompaniment figures and melodic figures trade string registers.
   - **Technique rotation** — different pluck/bend stroke combinations per dan give surface contrast over the same frame.
9. Every dan closes with the **same cadential formula** on the tonic — the dan-end pillars that let the ear mark each equal column.
10. Fix the number of dans by the title: **Godan (5), Rokudan (6), Shichidan (7), Hachidan (8), Kudan (9), Kumoi Kudan (9)**; *Midare* (乱れ, "disorder") is the sanctioned exception — classified as danmono but irregular in length (counted as 10 dan in Ikuta-ryū, 12 in Yamada-ryū); Mitsuzaki Kengyō's *Aki kaze no kyoku* opens with a danmono-form introduction.

### Stage 4 — set the tempo curve
11. **Per-dan envelope**: each dan begins slowly, accelerates internally, and returns to slow at its end. **Global envelope**: the piece accelerates dan by dan, and at the very end of the final dan it decelerates and closes slowly (Adriaansz 1970). The tempo curve is part of the composition, not mere performance.

### Stage 5 — ensemble setting (optional, sankyoku)
12. In the original *sōkyoku* setting the koto leads; **shamisen** doubles/supports with its own line and **shakuhachi** weaves heterophonic ornaments above; today the solo koto version (*Rokudan*'s koto part) is the widely known one. The ensemble version is the same composition with support voices fitted around the fixed dan columns.

### Stage 6 — fix & transmit
13. Fix the piece in tablature (string numbers + finger/oshiide signs) per school; the **Ikuta-ryū** and **Yamada-ryū** keep their own versions (e.g. *Midare* dan counts differ); players learn multiple school versions of the same piece. Because the form is strict and the repertoire small, pieces are immediately recognizable as danmono — "listeners unfamiliar with the style have trouble distinguishing one piece from another" (Adriaansz).

## 4. Practitioner examples

- **Yatsuhashi Kengyō** (1614–1685) — *Rokudan no Shirabe* (六段の調, "Music of Six Steps"; six 52-beat dans, first +4), *Midare* (乱れ — *midare rinzetsu*), *Hachidan* (attributed; some sources credit Kitajima/Kurahashi); "Father of Modern Koto"; adapted hirajōshi and insen scales from shamisen; also created the *kumiuta* song cycle.
- **Disputed/other composers** (Adriaansz 1967 table, sources *Sokyoku taiisho* 1779, *Buso gafu taiseishu* 1812, *Koto no kumiuta* 1822, *Kumiuta zenshu* 1941): *Kudan* — unknown/Kitajima Kengyō; *Shichidan* — unknown/Yasumura; *Godan* — Kitajima or Ikuta/Tomino Koto; *Kumoi Kudan* — Mitsuhashi; *Midare* — Yatsuhashi/Kurahashi. Authorship was communal and transmission oral — typical of the era.
- **Mitsuzaki Kengyō** — *Aki kaze no kyoku*, whose introduction is in danmono form.
- **School heads** — **Ikuta Kengyō** (Ikuta-ryū) and **Yamada Kengyō** (Yamada-ryū) fixed the performing versions still taught; Yamada-ryū adds vocal/shamisen color.
- **Modern lineage** — **Michio Miyagi** (1894–1956) carried the instrument's technique/notation forward (300+ works, 17-string bass koto; *Haru no Umi*), showing the same koto-craft pedagogy in a new idiom; **Tadao Sawai**, **Kazue Sawai** (internationalization).
- **Scholarship** — Willem Adriaansz (danmono chronology and "Procrustean bed" analysis), William P. Malm (koto history; also notes the disputed authorship of *Rokudan*).

## 5. UnitMatrix mapping

### Voices
- **V0 = Koto melody spine** — the theme (dan 1) and all variations; carries the opening gesture and the dan-end cadence; the piece's identity anchor across every column.
- **V1 = Koto accompaniment layer** — left-hand/low-register bass strokes (sustained tones, cadential support, ostinato-ish figuration under V0); in solo-koto practice the left hand plays the bass strings while the right hand carries the melody — two hands, one instrument, one voice-pair.
- **V2 = Shamisen support** (sankyoku, optional) — doubling/support line fitted to the same dan columns, sparser than V0.
- **V3 = Shakuhachi ornament line** (sankyoku, optional) — heterophonic elaboration above V0 with breath-phrase shaping.
- **V4 = Metadata** — tuning tag (hirajōshi / hon-kumoijoshi), dan index, kandō marker, tempo-curve markers, technique tags (oshiide/hajiki events).

### Sections
Columns = the **dan chain**, each column EXACTLY equal in ticks — the form's hard invariant (the engine's zero-drift rule IS the danmono rule):
S0 Kandō (4–8 beats, optional prelude before dan 1) → S1 Dan 1 (theme, 52 beats) → S2 Dan 2 → S3 Dan 3 → S4 Dan 4 → S5 Dan 5 → S6 Dan 6 (final, decelerando + closing cadence) — a Rokudan realization: 7 columns (or 6 if the kandō is folded into dan 1's 56 beats). Dan column length = 52 beats × 480 ticks/beat = **24,960 ticks**; 13 bars of 4/4 per dan. For Kudan: 9 dan columns (10 with kandō); for Kumoi Kudan, the same with the hon-kumoijoshi pitch set.

### Rules to encode
1. **Dan-length invariance (the form):** every dan cell in every voice pads to the identical terminal landmark (52 beats; dan 1 = 56 when the kandō is folded in). `validate()`'s zero-drift gate is the compositional rule, not just an engine check.
2. **Tuning pitch-set constraint:** all melodic material drawn from the hirajōshi field D–F–G–A–C (+octaves, 13-string register D→d'); In-scale bias for *Rokudan*-type melodies (E♭ as bend-target); hon-kumoijoshi variant for *Kumoi Kudan*. Intermediate pitches allowed ONLY as short oshiide ornaments (bend into/out of a field tone).
3. **Theme-skeleton preservation:** dans 2–N share dan 1's structural tones at phrase starts and the dan-end cadence; variation operators = rhythmic subdivision (density monotonically non-decreasing), ornamentation, register shift, technique rotation — no new structural pitches.
4. **Opening-gesture recognition:** each dan begins with a recognizable variant of the piece's opening figure (identity across equal columns).
5. **Dan-end cadential formula lock:** every dan closes with the same cadence type landing on the field's tonic.
6. **Tempo-curve envelope:** per-dan slow→fast→slow rubato profile; global accelerando across dan columns; final column ritardando to the slow close.
7. **Sankyoku separation (optional):** V2/V3 double V0 heterophonically, sparser, never crossing above the melody's structural register except in cadence fills.
8. **Kandō gate:** S0 exists only before dan 1, 4–8 beats, V1/V4 support only, ends on the opening gesture's first tone.
9. **Zero-drift + humanize:** danmono is rubato music — grid-locked MIDI will read as machine; apply strong per-dan accelerando humanize profiles (stretched onsets late in dan, early in fast runs) while keeping the column terminal landmarks untouched; `validate()` before export.

## 6. Quirks & pitfalls

- **Two beat-count conventions:** 52 beats/dan (traditional, = 13 bars of 4/4) vs 104 half-beats/dan (Adriaansz). Pick ONE in code and document it; the tick math must not mix them (104 half-beats = same duration as 52 quarter-beats only if the unit halves — beware double-counting).
- **First column ≠ dan columns:** *Rokudan*'s dan 1 has 4 extra beats (56) / a separate kandō intro. Model the kandō as its own S0 column so the six dan columns stay byte-equal — that equality IS the form.
- **Tablature, not notes:** traditional notation is string numbers + finger signs; pitch content follows from the tuning. Transpose the tuning to a concrete pitch set BEFORE writing units, or the "notes" are meaningless.
- **No functional harmony:** the pitch field is a hemitonic pentatonic mode + bends; do not harmonize in Western chord grammar — there is no chord progression to encode.
- **Rubato is normative:** strict quantization + steady tempo destroys the form's identity; the tempo envelope must be composed into the humanize profile, not left to chance.
- **Originality was not the goal:** Adriaansz notes the pieces sound alike by design; "fixing" the formula (varied dan lengths, new modulations) breaks the genre. *Midare* is the single sanctioned irregularity.
- **Attribution is contested:** *Rokudan* may not be by Yatsuhashi (sources differ: Kitajima). Cite authorship as attributed/disputed, not fact.
