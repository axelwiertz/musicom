## 1. Description

Partimento is a pedagogical and compositional practice developed in the Neapolitan
conservatories (Naples, c. 1690–1830). A **partimento** is a single bass line —
unfigured or lightly figured — that encodes a complete harmonic and contrapuntal
composition within itself. The student's task is to **realize** the bass line at the
keyboard: to improvise (or write out) upper voices that follow the voice-leading
schemata implicitly embedded in the bass.

Unlike figured bass (which provides explicit interval numbers above each bass note),
partimento relies on the student's internalized library of **schemata** — stock
voice-leading patterns learned through years of keyboard drill — to infer the
correct harmony from the bass's motion, scale degree, and registral context.

This is a fundamentally **human cognitive process**: pattern recognition, schema
matching, and real-time contrapuntal improvisation. It is not an algorithm; it is
a trained musician's craft, transmitted master-to-student through singing,
playing, and memorization.

### Why It Matters

Partimento is the missing link between thoroughbass (figured bass realization as
accompaniment) and free composition. Masters like **Francesco Durante**, **Leonardo
Leo**, **Fedele Fenaroli**, and **Giovanni Paisiello** used partimenti as both
teaching tools and compositional sketchpads. **J.S. Bach** was influenced by
related Italian traditions (his pupil Krebs studied Italian partimento methods).
**Mozart** and **Beethoven** both studied Italian counterpoint traditions that
overlap with partimento practice. The method directly produced the harmonic
vocabulary of the Classical style.

## 2. Craft Steps — How a Human Executes It

### Step 1: Sing and Play the Bass Line Alone

The partimento bass is read at the keyboard. The student sings the bass aloud while
playing it, internalizing the harmonic trajectory. The bass's contour — rising
thirds, descending tetrachords, scalar runs, leaps — signals which schema is
active.

### Step 2: Identify Scale Degrees and Cadential Points

The student marks the key, identifies each bass note as a scale degree (1–7), and
locates cadences. Cadences in partimento are the structural pillars: the bass
patterns `2–7→1` (authentic cadence), `4–3→4` (half cadence), or `6–5→6` (deceptive)
are recognized by their characteristic bass motion.

### Step 3: Match Schemata to Bass Patterns

The student scans the bass for known **voice-leading schemata**. The core Neapolitan
repertoire includes (using Robert Gjerdingen's terminology from *Music in the
Galant Style*):

- **Romanesca**: bass descends 1–7–6–5 (or 6–5–4–3); upper voices move in parallel
  sixths with a standard chord sequence (I–V6/4–vi–V or vi–V–IV–III).
- **Prinner ripieno**: bass descends 1–7–6 (or 4–3–2); two upper voices descend in
  parallel tenths, converging on the third above the bass.
- **Fonte**: bass moves 6–5–4–3 in the relative minor; two upper voices descend by
  step in contrary motion to the bass.
- **Monte**: bass ascends 1–2–3–4 (or 5–6–7–1); upper voices ascend in parallel
  sixths.
- **Fenaroli schema**: bass descends 6–5–4–3 with a 5–6 sequence above, creating a
  descending sequential chain.

Each schema has a fixed upper-voice template. The student's trained ear recognizes
the bass pattern and recalls the template.

### Step 4: Improvise Upper Voices at the Keyboard

Using the matched schemata, the student improvises right-hand upper voices in real
time. The key constraints are:

- **Smooth voice leading**: upper voices move by step or small leaps; avoid
  parallel perfect fifths and octaves.
- **Schema fidelity**: the upper-voice contour must match the recognized schema
  template (e.g., parallel sixths for Romanesca, parallel tenths for Prinner).
- **Cadential arrival**: upper voices must reach the correct chord tones at
  cadential bass notes (e.g., leading tone resolves to tonic at authentic
  cadence).

### Step 5: Add Diminutions and Embellishments

Once the harmonic skeleton is realized, the student adds melodic diminutions —
passing tones, neighbor tones, suspensions, runs — to the upper voices. This
transforms the dry schema realization into expressive music. Suspensions are
particularly important: the standard 4–3 suspension, 9–8 suspension, and 7–6
suspension are applied at cadential points.

### Step 6: Add Contrapuntal Voices (if writing for ensemble)

For multi-voiced realization, the student adds inner voices following the same
schema templates, maintaining voice independence. The bass line's implied harmony
distributes across SATB or string quartet textures. The partimento thus becomes a
full score: the bass was always the composition's spine.

## 3. Practitioner Examples

### Francesco Durante (1684–1755)
Durante's partimenti (published as *Studio per suonare il basso*) are the
foundational repertoire. His bass lines systematically drill each schema:
Romanesca in minor, Prinner in major, Fonte at internal cadences. Students played
these daily, memorizing the schema patterns by ear and hand.

### Fedele Fenaroli (1730–1818)
Fenaroli's *Partimenti ossia Basso numerato* codified the pedagogy into a graded
curriculum: Book 1 = simple cadences and rule-of-the-octave harmonizations; Book
2 = sequences and schemata; Book 3 = imitation and fugue. Every Neapolitan
conservatory student (including Paisiello, Cimarosa) worked through these.

### Giovanni Paisiello (1740–1816)
Paisiello's partimento treatise for the Russian court (written for the
Princess—later Empress) adapted the Neapolitan method for royal pedagogy. His
examples show how partimento bass lines directly generate operatic arias — the
bass schemata become the harmonic backbone of the vocal line.

### J.S. Bach (1685–1750) — Parallel Tradition
Bach's keyboard works (Inventions, Sinfonias, Preludes from the WTC) exhibit
partimento-like thinking: the bass line in the C-major Prelude (WTC I) follows a
prinner-like descent, and the harmonic sequence is a textbook Romanesca.
Bach's student Lorenz Mizler translated Italian partimento treatises into German,
bridging the traditions.

### Mozart (1756–1791)
Mozart's keyboard improvisations — documented by contemporaries — followed
partimento-like procedures: he would play a bass line and realize it extemporaneously,
weaving in schemata he had absorbed from Italian-influenced pedagogy. The first
movement of K.545 (C-major Sonata) opens with a prinner-romanessa hybrid.

## 4. Musical Elements Framework Mapping

| Element | Role in Partimento |
|---|---|
| **PITCH** | Upper-voice pitches are determined by schema templates above the bass. The bass line's scale degrees drive harmonic selection. Suspensions and passing tones add chromatic and diatonic diminutions. |
| **RHYTHM** | Partimento bass lines carry implicit rhythm: cadential bass notes are typically longer (dotted half, whole note), sequential bass segments move in equal note values (quarter notes), and diminutions subdivide into eighths/sixteenths. The human performer chooses rhythmic realization at the keyboard. |
| **HARMONY** | The core element. Each schema implies a specific chord progression. The Romanesca = I–V6/4–vi–V; the Prinner = I–V6–vi (or IV–V–vi); the Fonte = vi–V–IV–III (relative minor). Harmony is encoded *in the bass motion itself*, not in external chord symbols. |
| **STRUCTURE** | Partimenti follow standard forms: binary (A–B), ternary (A–B–A), or rondo. Cadential bass points define section boundaries. A modulation to the dominant or relative major marks the end of the A section; the return to tonic marks the reprise. |
| **TEXTURE** | Realization texture ranges from 2-voice (bass + one right-hand line) to 4-voice (SATB). The schema determines texture density: Romanesca naturally produces 3-voice texture (bass + two parallel upper voices); cadential points thicken to 4 voices with suspensions. |

## 5. UnitMatrix Integration

### Concept
A partimento bass line maps to a single **Voice row** (the bass). The realization
of upper voices fills additional **Voice rows**. Each **Section column** corresponds
to a formal segment bounded by cadential bass points.

### Voices (Rows)

| Voice Row | Role | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Voice 0 (Bass) | Partimento bass line | The source bass notes — scale degrees, cadential targets, sequential segments. This row is *input* — the human-composed spine. |
| Voice 1 (Right Hand 1 / Melody) | Upper voice from schema template | Pitches determined by the active schema (parallel sixths for Romanesca, tenths for Prinner, etc.). Includes diminutions and suspensions. |
| Voice 2 (Right Hand 2 / Inner) | Second upper voice or harmonic filler | Fills out the chord; follows the schema's second upper-voice strand. In 2-voice realization, this row is empty. |
| Voice 3 (Optional / Inner 2) | Inner voice for 4-voice texture | Added at cadences for suspension chains (4–3, 7–6, 9–8). May be silent during sequential passages. |

### Sections (Columns)

| Section | Bass Pattern | Active Schema | Harmonic Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| A1 (Tonic area) | 1–7–6–5 | Romanesca | HOME → LIFT |
| A2 (Sequence) | 6–5–4–3 | Fenaroli/Fonte | LIFT → TENSE |
| Cadence 1 | 2–7→1 | Authentic cadence | TENSE → HOME |
| B (Dominant area) | 5–6–7–1 | Monte | HOME → LIFT (modulation) |
| Cadence 2 | 4–3→4 | Half cadence | LIFT → TENSE |
| A' (Reprise) | 1–7–6–5 | Romanesca | HOME → LIFT |
| Final Cadence | 2–7→1 | Authentic cadence | TENSE → HOME |

### Cell Content

Each cell $U_{v,s}$ contains:

- **Voice 0 (Bass), all sections**: `{PITCH}` = bass note sequence (scale degrees
  realized as MIDI pitches in the key); `{RHYTHM}` = note durations (cadential
  notes longer, sequence notes equal); `{HARMONY}` = implied chord at each bass
  note (derived from schema); `{STRUCTURE}` = cadence type at section boundary.
- **Voice 1 (Melody), all sections**: `{PITCH}` = upper-voice pitches from schema
  template + diminutions; `{RHYTHM}` = rhythmic realization (may be more active
  than bass with passing tones); `{TEXTURE}` = dynamic marking / articulation.
- **Voice 2–3, cadence sections only**: `{PITCH}` = suspension chains; `{RHYTHM}`
  = syncopated suspension rhythm (prepare-suspend-resolve).

### Rules Encoding the Method

To encode partimento in the Musicom engine, the following rule set governs the
UnitMatrix:

1. **Bass-First Rule**: Voice 0 is authored first — it is the human-composed
   partimento. No other voice may contradict the bass's implied harmony.
2. **Schema Matching Rule**: For each Section, the bass pattern is matched to a
   schema library (Romanesca, Prinner, Fonte, Monte, Fenaroli). The matched schema
   determines the upper-voice pitch template for Voice 1.
3. **Voice-Leading Rule**: Upper voices must move by step or third; parallel
   perfect fifths and octaves between any two voices are forbidden.
4. **Cadential Rule**: At cadential sections, the bass follows 2–7→1 (authentic)
   or 4–3→4 (half). Upper voices must include a suspension (4–3 or 7–6) resolving
   to the final chord tone.
5. **Diminution Rule**: Upper voices may add passing tones on weak beats between
   chord tones; neighbor tones may ornament cadential resolutions.
6. **Texture Rule**: Sequential sections use 2–3 voices (sparse); cadential
   sections use 3–4 voices (dense, with suspensions).
7. **Modulation Rule**: Section B bass modulates to the dominant or relative major
   by pivoting on a common chord; the schema for Section B reflects the new key.
