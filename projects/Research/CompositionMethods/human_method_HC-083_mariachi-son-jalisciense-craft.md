## 1. Method name & tradition

**Son jalisciense composition** — the craft of building the fast, copla-driven song form of western Mexico's mariachi tradition: a fixed **estribillo** (refrain) chained to **coplas** (verse stanzas, often improvised), set over the signature **sesquialtera** rhythm (alternating 6/8 and 3/4 bars), harmonized by a tonic–dominant **armonía** (vihuela + guitar + guitarrón) and doubled/answered by **violins in parallel thirds** and a **trumpet obligado**.

- **Tradition / Culture:** Mexican mariachi, rooted in the *son jalisciense* of Jalisco/Nayarit (19th c.; the same basic strings/rhythms/melodies as the sones of Veracruz and the Huasteca — all descendants of Spanish baroque guitar/violin practice brought to New Spain). The old ensemble was one vihuela, two violins and a guitarrón (which by the 19th century largely replaced the harp); **trumpets were added in the 1910s–1920s and became a fixture by the 1930s**, giving the modern mariachi its sound. The genre took national prominence in the first half of the 20th century via presidential inaugurations, radio, and the *charro* film era (Jorge Negrete, Pedro Infante, Lucha Reyes). Mariachi song genres include sones, rancheras, corridos, huapangos, jarabes, boleros, danzones, cumbias, polkas and waltzes. **UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, Representative List (2011).**
- **Key figures:** Mariachi Vargas de Tecalitlán (founded by Gaspar Vargas, led by Silvestre Vargas from 1928; violinist-arranger Rubén Fuentes from 1950; trumpeter Miguel Martínez), composer Blas Galindo (*Sones de Mariachi* suite, 1940), ranchera canon José Alfredo Jiménez, Tomás Méndez (*Cucurrucucú Paloma*), singers Jorge Negrete, Pedro Infante, Lucha Reyes (*La Tequilera*), later Vicente Fernández. Ethnomusicology: Jesús Jáuregui (provenance/attribution studies of *El Son de la Negra*), Jonathan Clark (mariachi history).

## 3. The craft procedure in detail (how the human does it)

### Stage 0 — apprenticeship (oral/aural transmission)
1. A *sonero* grows up inside the tradition: sones are learned **by ear** in the family/ensemble, text and tune passed singer-to-singer; there is no authoritative score for the folk sones — Jesús Jáuregui's decades of research on *El Son de la Negra* show the tune "has undergone modifications and arrangements that can hardly be attributed to a single author or epoch" (collective authorship, living variants). Notation (when used at all) is a memory aid.
2. The apprentice learns the two body skills simultaneously: **strumming** (vihuela/guitar rasgueados and guitarrón bordones) and **singing/dancing** (the copla singer also knows the zapateado, since the dance steps accent the same hemiola).

### Stage 1 — fix the harmonic cell and the key
3. Choose a **major key** (sones jaliscienses are "mostly in major keys") and fix a **tonic–dominant cell**: the harmony alternates essentially between I and V (occasional IV), with no chromatic excursions. The whole son is composed over this cell — the armonía section repeats it bar after bar while the melody, coplas and obligados spin above it.
4. The bass side is assigned to the **guitarrón** (6-string, fretless, re-entrant tuning A–D–G–C–E–A, developed from the Spanish *bajo de uña*): it plays **bordones** — open-string bass notes outlining root and fifth of the cell — frequently **doubled at the octave** (a guitarrón signature), with short walking pickup figures that lead into each downbeat.

### Stage 2 — set the sesquialtera (the son's rhythmic engine)
5. Lock the **sesquialtera**: one bar of **6/8** followed by one bar of **3/4** (3+3 vs 2+2+2), alternating throughout — this is the documented rhythmic pattern of the son jalisciense, carried by the armonía (vihuela + guitars) and guitarrón together. Both bars contain the same six eighth-note units, so the pattern is a true hemiola (3:2 grouping switch), not a meter change of length.
6. **Vihuela** (5-string, high-pitched, the mariachi's characteristic small guitar) strums **rasgueados** in the high register, marking the hemiola's offbeat/backbeat energy with its bright tone; the **guitar(s)/armonía** strum the chord rhythm. The resulting pattern is the famous "son rhythm": accented dotted-quarter (6/8) bars alternating with three-quarter (3/4) bars.
7. Optional percussive layer: dancers **zapateado** (stomp) on a wooden platform, accenting the hemiola; sones are fast (allegro) and the stomp pattern doubles the armonía accents.

### Stage 3 — compose the text: estribillo + coplas
8. Write/choose the **estribillo** (refrain): 1–2 short lines, fixed, repeated verbatim — the song's identity hook (e.g. *"La Negra"*'s refrain).
9. Compose the **coplas** (verses): short stanzas — typically 4-line **cuartetas** or 6-line **sextillas** (rhyme scheme abcbdb), with each line ending on a strong downbeat. Coplas are **syllabic** (one note per syllable, in 6/8 declamation), high in register, ornamented lightly; in performance coplas may be improvised or replaced (the "sonecitos del país" practice of trading verses). Subjects: love, betrayal, regional pride, animals, humor, machismo — never long narrative (that is the corrido's job).
10. The song alternates: **estribillo → copla → estribillo → copla…**; the singer leads the coplas solo, the ensemble joins on the refrain.

### Stage 4 — voice the instruments around the melody
11. **Violins** (two, the traditional duet) play the vocal melody **in parallel thirds** (the mariachi's signature "tercia" sound), in a high bright register, with ornaments (appoggiatura/mordent); in instrumental passages the first violin carries the tune and the second shadows it in thirds.
12. **Trumpet** (added 1910s–30s; Miguel Martínez of Mariachi Vargas codified the style): during the vocal coplas it **rests or plays short obligado answers** between phrases (call/answer separation); in the instrumental sections it **doubles the melody at the octave** or takes the lead phrase; its bright, projecting tone rides above the strings.
13. All players sing: mariachi practice has every musician double as chorus/backup vocalist on the estribillo.

### Stage 5 — assemble the form and fix the arrangement
14. Chain the sections: **instrumental introduction** (armonía alone states the hemiola cell) → **estribillo** → **copla 1** → **monte/instrumental interlude** (violin and trumpet trade phrases; register rises; the *monte* is the son family's instrumental improvisation block — cognate across son jarocho/jalisciense practice) → **estribillo** → **copla 2** → **instrumental interlude (faster, higher)** → **remate** (cadential flourish: V–I with a strummed rasgueo finish, often capped by the *grito* — the mariachi shout "¡arriba! / ¡olé!" — and a zapateado stamp).
15. The arrangement is **fixed by ensemble tradition**: each mariachi keeps its "house version" of a son (El Son de la Negra exists in countless arrangements — Blas Galindo's 1940 concert-suite adaptation for *Sones de Mariachi* is only the best known); the ensemble's director/violinist (e.g. Rubén Fuentes for Vargas) shapes entrances, doublings, and the remate.

## 4. Practitioner examples

- **Mariachi Vargas de Tecalitlán** (Tecalitlán, Jalisco; founded by Gaspar Vargas, led 1928– by Silvestre Vargas; from the 1930s in Mexico City) — the canonical ensemble: dozens of recordings, film work, and the defining arrangements of *El Son de la Negra* and the son/ranchera repertoire; Rubén Fuentes (violinist/arranger, artistic director from 1950) and **Miguel Martínez** (trumpeter) shaped the modern mariachi sound.
- **Blas Galindo** (1910–1993) — composer who adapted *El Son de la Negra* (1940) in his *Sones de Mariachi* suite (premiered in New York), carrying the folk son into the concert hall; the version most associated with the Ballet Folklórico.
- **Jesús Jáuregui** — ethnomusicologist whose 20+ years of provenance research (presented 2010, Nayarit) documents the collective, multi-author history of *El Son de la Negra* — the textbook case of son transmission.
- **The charro-cinema singers** — Jorge Negrete, Pedro Infante, Lucha Reyes (*La Tequilera*): brought sones and rancheras to national film/radio fame in the 1930s–50s.
- **José Alfredo Jiménez (1926–1973)** — songwriter whose rancheras (*El Rey*, *Si Nos Dejan*, *Que Bonito Amor*, *La Media Vuelta*) are "regarded as the basis of modern regional Mexican music" — the ranchera side of the same mariachi song craft; performed by mariachi ensembles with the same armonía/sesquialtera accompaniment language (in 3/4 or 2/4).
- **Vicente Fernández** — the later ranchera voice (*Volver Volver*, *El Rey*), keeping the mariachi arrangement tradition alive into the 21st century.
- Related family members: **jarabe tapatío** (the national dance; its standard music by Jesús González Rubio, 19th c., arranged in the 1920s as a medley of son rhythms) and **huapango de mariachi** (Huasteca son performed by the full ensemble).

## 5. UnitMatrix mapping

### Voices
- **V0 = Vocal lead** — estribillo + coplas; syllabic 6/8 declamation, high register, light ornaments, *grito* marker at remate.
- **V1 = Violin duet** — vocal melody doubled in **parallel thirds**; ornaments; leads the monte instrumental blocks.
- **V2 = Trumpet** — obligado answers between vocal phrases (rest during coplas), octave doubling / lead phrase in monte; bright register above strings.
- **V3 = Vihuela** — rasgueado strum dictionary marking the hemiola (offbeat/backbeat attack map, high register).
- **V4 = Guitar / armonía** — chord-strum rhythm, I–V cell, the 6/8↔3/4 bar-pattern lock.
- **V5 = Guitarrón** — bordones (root–fifth open-string bass), octave doublings, walking pickups into downbeats; tonic–dominant skeleton.
- **V6 = Zapateado (optional)** — dance-platform stomp accents doubling the hemiola; stamps at remate.

### Sections
Columns = the **son form chain** (each bar is 6 eighths whether 6/8 or 3/4, so bar lengths are naturally equal — zero drift is trivial):
S0 Intro (armonía alone: hemiola cell stated) → S1 Estribillo → S2 Copla 1 → S3 Monte I (violin↔trumpet antiphony) → S4 Estribillo (repeat) → S5 Copla 2 → S6 Monte II (faster/higher) → S7 Remate (V–I rasgueo + grito + stomp). All voices padded to full column length; `validate()` must pass.

### Rules to encode
1. **Sesquialtera lock:** even/odd bar grouping alternates 3+3 (6/8) and 2+2+2 (3/4); armonía + guitarrón accent maps follow the group; both bars = 6 eighth-note units (equal tick length).
2. **Harmony cell:** I–V alternation only (occasional IV); guitarrón root–fifth bordones with octave doubling; no chromatic harmony; mostly major keys.
3. **Parallel-thirds duet:** V1 derived from V0 by transposing a 3rd (with harmonic adjustment to the I–V cell), ornaments seeded.
4. **Trumpet separation:** V2 rests during vocal coplas; answers between phrases; octave-doubles V0/V1 in monte sections only.
5. **Copla prosody:** 4- or 6-line stanzas (cuarteta/sextilla abcbdb), syllabic setting, line ends on downbeats; estribillo verbatim-repeated (identity anchor, V0 invariant).
6. **Monte antiphony:** violin and trumpet alternate phrases (≥1 phrase rest between), register ascends across Monte I→II.
7. **Remate:** V–I cadential formula, strummed rasgueo, optional grito (shout) + zapateado stamp at final beat; fast tempo throughout.
8. **Vihuela strum dictionary:** per-bar attack maps (6/8 bar: accents on 1 and 4 eighths; 3/4 bar: accents on 1,3,5 eighths) — the hemiola made audible.
9. **Zero-drift:** equal-length bars; free vocal melisma quantized to the 6-eighth grid; `validate()` gate.
10. **Humanize:** guitarrón/vihuela strict (groove anchors), vocal rubato + portamento, violin slight layback; remate grito as loud short event.

## 6. Quirks & pitfalls

- **Collective authorship:** sones are folk items — *El Son de la Negra* has no single composer; model the *process* (estribillo/copla/sesquialtera/remate), not a fixed score. Don't attribute the tune to Blas Galindo alone (he *adapted* it in 1940).
- **Sesquialtera ≠ meter change:** 6/8 then 3/4 is a regrouping of the same six eighth notes — keep both bars the same tick length or you break zero-drift.
- **Parallel thirds must fit the I–V cell:** a naive third above the melody can land outside the chord — adjust to the nearest chord tone (the human violinist does this by ear).
- **Trumpet etiquette:** the trumpet does NOT double the singer during coplas; doubling appears only in instrumental blocks — encode the separation or the texture collapses into mud.
- **Ranchera vs son:** the ranchera (3/4 or 2/4) is a sibling genre with the same ensemble — don't force ranchera waltz rhythm into the sesquialtera rule; keep the method scoped to the son.
- **Fast tempo + high register is the idiom:** sones are allegro and bright; slowing them or dropping the tessitura reads as a different genre.
