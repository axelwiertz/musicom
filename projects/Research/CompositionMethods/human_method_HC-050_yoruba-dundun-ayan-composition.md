# HC-050: Yoruba Dùndún Talking-Drum Ensemble Composition (Àyàn Tradition)

**Layer:** human (→ concrete)

**Tradition / Culture:** Yoruba (southwest Nigeria) — Àyàn (Ayan) hereditary drumming lineage, rooted in ancestral veneration, courtly oríkì praise poetry, and religious òrìṣà devotion (Oyo Empire → present, ~16th c.–present).

**Primary Musical Elements:** RHYTHM, PITCH (speech-surrogate tone mapping), TEXTURE, STRUCTURE

## Description

The dùndún hourglass tension drum is both a musical instrument and a speech surrogate — it can "speak" Yoruba by mapping the three linguistic tones (High/Mid/Low → Do/Re/Mi) to variable pitches produced by squeezing the tensioning cords while striking the membrane. The Àyàn composition craft is built on:

1. **Hereditary oral transmission** — learned within the Àyàn family guild (patrilineage stemming from Àyàngalu, the mythical first drummer). The craft is not learned from notation; it is absorbed through years of apprenticeship, attending ceremonies, memorizing the repertoire of oríkì (praise poems), òwe (proverbs), and historical narratives of the community.
2. **Speech-tone composition** — the compositional act begins with selecting or composing a Yoruba text. The text's inherent tone sequence (High/Mid/Low) and prosodic rhythm become the fundamental compositional material. The master drummer holds this text in mind and maps it to the dùndún's pitch parameters.
3. **Three performance modes** — the same composed material can be rendered in *speech mode* (free rhythm, no accompaniment, pure text conveyance), *dance mode* (fixed timeline accompaniment with 12- or 16-pulse patterns), or *musical mode* (abstracted from text, purely rhythmic-melodic).
4. **Ensemble hierarchy** — the Ìyáàlù dùndún (mother/lead drum) carries the composed text. Supporting drums (gángan, keríkerì, ìsáájú, ìkehìn, gúdúgúdú) provide ostinato accompaniment and response patterns, never duplicating the lead's text.

## Craft Procedure (Step-by-Step)

### Phase 1: Learn the Àyàn Repertoire (Apprenticeship)
- Live with/under a master Àyàn drummer for years
- Internalize the tone patterns of Yoruba language (L/M/H = ìsàlè/àárín/òkè)
- Memorize corpus of oríkì (praise poems for orisa, kings, ancestors, towns), òwe (proverbs), and historical narratives
- Learn the traditional timeline patterns (12-pulse for certain ceremonies, 16-pulse for others)
- Master the physical technique: left hand squeezes tension cords to bend pitch, right hand strikes membrane with curved stick

### Phase 2: Compose (Select/Create Text)
- Choose the occasion: which orisa is being honored? Which elder/chief/king? What community event?
- Select an existing oríkì from the lineage repertoire, OR extemporize a new text adapting formulaic praise phrases
- Internalize the tone sequence Low→Mid→High contours of each Yoruba syllable — this IS the melodic material
- Map the speech tones to drum pitches: Low (okun, loose cords) → deepest pitch; Mid (àárín, relaxed) → middle pitch; High (òkè, squeezed) → highest pitch
- Glides (rising/falling diphthongs) map to pitch bends on the drum

### Phase 3: Arrange Ensemble Parts
- **Lead (Ìyáàlù dùndún):** carries the primary text — each drum stroke "speaks" a syllable. In speech mode: free rhythm, each syllable gets a single stroke with appropriate pitch and squeeze duration. In dance mode: the same text is compressed/timed to fit the timeline grid.
- **Èjìn (second Ìyáàlù):** if present, provides a two-note bass melo-rhythmic ostinato shadowing the lead's tonal center.
- **Gángan/Àdàmò:** half-size tension drum, often doubles the ìyáàlù at higher register or plays independent rhythmic response.
- **Keríkerì/Ìsáájú/Ìkehìn:** middle-sized supporting drums — fixed ostinato patterns (often short repeating cells), never "speak" text, provide rhythmic groove.
- **Gúdúgúdú:** kettle drum (non-tension, fixed pitch) — low-pitched timekeeper, marks the timeline's fundamental pulse.

### Phase 4: Set the Timeline (Dance Mode)
- Choose 12-pulse or 16-pulse timeline pattern depending on ceremony
- Assign timeline to gúdúgúdú or gángan supporting drums
- The lead ìyáàlù compresses its vocal text's rhythm to align onsets with the timeline grid
- Syllables that fall between timeline pulses are shortened/elided or "re-spoken" in drum language

### Phase 5: Perform the Composition
- **Speech mode:** Lead ìyáàlù alone performs text in free rhythm. No accompaniment. Listeners hear the text spoken through the drum.
- **Dance mode:** Ensemble enters sequentially: timeline (gúdúgúdú) → support ostinatos → ìyáàlù lead with text. Lead cycles through text passages, call-response with chorus refrains.
- **Musical mode:** Text abstracted; ìyáàlù plays rhythmic-melodic patterns derived from oríkì but no longer "speaking" recognizable words — pure music.
- **Escalation:** As performance progresses, density increases: support drums intensify, timeline accelerates slightly, lead moves higher in pitch register, more text material is introduced.
- **Close:** Convergence on final cadential phrase — the lead repeats the final line of the oríkì, support drums cut out one by one, gúdúgúdú holds pulse to final stroke.

## UnitMatrix Integration

### Voices

| Voice | Instrument | Role | Behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Voice 0** | Ìyáàlù dùndún (lead) | Speech-tone spine — carries composed text as pitch+rhythm. Invariant text identity per piece, but pitch varies with tone-mapping. | Primary melodic: three pitch levels (L/M/H) mapped to Yoruba syllables. Onset rhythm matches syllable rhythm of the composed text. Glides as pitch bends. |
| **Voice 1** | Gángan/Àdàmò | Higher-register support. Ostinato or heterophonic doubling. Does NOT speak text. | Rhythmic ostinato (fixed repeating cell, 1–4 bars). Can shadow Voice 0's contour or provide independent response phrase. |
| **Voice 2** | Keríkerì/Ìsáájú/Ìkehìn | Middle drum support. Fixed ostinato phrase. | Short repeating ostinato (1–2 bars). Percussive, fills between Voice 0 onsets. No pitch variation (fixed membrane tension). |
| **Voice 3** | Gúdúgúdú (kettle) | Timeline foundation. Invariant pulse grid. | Low-pitched pulse: 12-pulse or 16-pulse timeline. Continuous, no rests. The temporal anchor. |
| **Voice 4** | Èjìn (2nd ìyáàlù, optional) | Bass melo-rhythmic shadow. Root/5th ostinato in low register. | Two-tone ostinato (tonic and dominant of the oríkì's tonal center). Invariant throughout. |

### Sections

| Section | Voice Mask | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **S0_Intro_Timeline** | V3 (gúdúgúdú) only | Timeline established alone, 1–2 cycles |
| **S1_Support_Entry** | V2/V3 (+V1/V4) | Support drums enter sequentially |
| **S2_Speech_Mode** | V0+V3 (optional V1/V2 light) | Lead performs text in free rhythm over minimum support; or S2 may be SKIPPED if only dance mode |
| **S3_Dance_Mode** | V0+V1+V2+V3+V4 | Full ensemble. Lead's text compressed to timeline. Call-response structure. |
| **S4_Intensification** | All voices, denser | Density increase: shorter stroke values, higher register on lead, support drum intensification |
| **S5_Cadence_Close** | V0→V3 only → silence | Lead performs final line → V3 sole → final stroke |

### Rules

1. **Text-to-tone lock** — Voice 0's pitch sequence is constrained by the Yoruba tone map of the composed text (L→low pitch, M→mid pitch, H→high pitch). Pitch deviations limited to ±1 interval/tone for expressive glide.
2. **Non-text voices** — Voices 1–4 never carry speech tones. V1 may shadow V0's contour rhythmically but does NOT reproduce the tone sequence. This preserves the primacy of the ìyáàlù as the "speaker."
3. **Timeline invariance** — Voice 3 (gúdúgúdú) holds an invariant pulse grid (12 or 16 pulses per cycle). The grid never changes within a piece. Cycle length defines the temporal universe.
4. **Support ostinato constraint** — Voices 1, 2, 4 play fixed repeating cells (1–4 bars). Variation is limited to dynamics/accents, never the pattern itself, unless the master cues a section change.
5. **Density progression** — The number of voices active and their rhythmic density increase monotonically from S1→S4. Maximum density at S4 (all voices, shortest note values). S5 collapses.
6. **Three-mode gate** — The composition exists in three modes (speech/dance/musical) which are distinct renderings of the same text/pitch material. The mode determines Voice 0's rhythm: free in speech, grid-aligned in dance, abstracted in musical.
7. **Call-response structure** — In S3/S4, Voice 0's phrases alternate with a fixed chorus response (played by V1/V2). The response pattern is invariant for the entire piece. Voice 0 cannot overlap the response gap.
8. **Cadence formula** — S5 close follows a fixed pattern: last tone repeated → EITHER held decay OR three rapid strokes (șákàrà). Gúdúgúdú converges to final beat.
9. **Zero-drift** — All section lengths are defined in timeline cycles (12 or 16 pulses). Section boundaries fall on the first pulse of a cycle. No phrase overhangs the boundary.

## Quirks and Pitfalls

- The text-to-tone mapping is not one-to-one — Yoruba has phonetic tone sandhi (tone changes based on adjacent syllables) which the master navigates by ear. This cannot be reduced to a lookup table.
- Dùndún pitch is continuous (not discrete like piano) — the three tone levels are relative, not absolute frequencies. In rehearsal, the master adjusts "High" to the drum's maximum tension and "Low" to the drum's minimum, with "Mid" centered. Different drums = different absolute pitches.
- The same oríkì composition can take very different forms when rendered by different Àyàn lineages (Oyo vs. Ibadan vs. Lagos) — the text is fixed, but the rhythmic feel, support patterns, and timeline choice vary.
- "Speech mode" has no fixed tempo — the lead paces syllables by felt prosody, not meter. This is hard to represent in a tick-based grid; use large sparse ticks with rest padding.
- The gúdúgúdú timeline is NOT "drumset" — it is a single low-pitched drum playing an invariant pattern. Do not represent it as a complex multi-drum part.
