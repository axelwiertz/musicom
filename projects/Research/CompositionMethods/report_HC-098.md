## 1. Method Summary

The **Renaissance imitative motet** is a through-composed polyphonic setting of a Latin sacred text built as a **chain of "points of imitation"** — one per clause of the text. Each clause receives a short melody (a *soggetto*) that is stated successively by each voice in staggered entry (the point), then abandoned as the next clause's soggetto takes over. Texture is continuous, seamless, without bar lines; the piece is the sum of its overlapping phrase-units. It is the direct ancestor of the Baroque fugue (HC-055) but differs fundamentally: text-driven (not subject-driven), modal (not tonal), through-composed (no episode/stretto architecture), with texture varied per clause for **word clarity** (homophonic declamation inside the polyphony).

This entry fills a gap in the framework: fugue (HC-055), canon (HC-089), species counterpoint (HC-021), and Notre-Dame organum (HC-080) were documented; the 16th-century phrase-by-phrase imitative motet — the central compositional craft of the High Renaissance and the model behind Fux's Palestrina style — was not.

## 2. The Craft Procedure (how a human executes it, step by step)

1. **Choose the text; parse into clauses.** Antiphon, psalm verse, sequence stanza, Song of Songs excerpt, or ceremonial Latin prose. The composer marks syntactic units; each clause = one musical section (one point). Palestrina: "The text in his works takes on such importance that it often defines the entire structure of the composition."
2. **Fix voice count and mode.** 4 voices is the norm (cantus/superius, altus, tenor, bassus); 5–6 for ceremonial motets (Josquin *Miserere* = 5vv, *Praeter rerum seriem* = 6vv). Mode (Dorian/Phrygian/Lydian/Mixolydian or transposed) fixes pitch set, final, reciting tone, cadence degrees.
3. **Compose a soggetto per clause.** 2–8 notes, syllabic to lightly melismatic, single climax, recognizable interval skeleton + rhythm profile, cadence-ready tail. Jeppesen's Palestrina melody rules: stepwise bias, leaps ≤ 5th with stepwise compensation, range ≤ 10th–12th. Josquin abandoned long melismas for "shorter, repeated motifs between voices."
4. **Run the soggetto through the voices (the point).** Entry order (S–A–T–B typical), entry interval (unison/octave/4th/5th, diatonically adjusted — modal transposition, never chromatic), entry distance (1–2 breves of the tactus). Each voice restates the whole soggetto — Josquin's *Ave Maria…virgo serena*: "each voice enters by restating the line sung before it."
5. **Write free continuation.** Voices that finished the soggetto add derived counter-fragments, cadential approaches, or — the signature move — **anticipate the next clause** (the "run-through": one voice begins the next soggetto before the current point completes). Short text fragments tossed between voices = *attacchi*.
6. **Cadence at the clause end.** Modal clausula: 2→1 in one voice, 7→1 in another, prepared 4–3 suspension in a third; landing on the final or a mode-appropriate degree (Zarlino). Cadences **overlap** the next point — the "no-breath" continuity ("cascading, passing chords… absence of an obvious beat").
7. **Vary texture per clause for text clarity.** Full points / reduced duets / **homophonic declamation** blocks. Palestrina's homophonic-inside-polyphony and text intelligibility are his documented hallmarks; Tallis' *If Ye Love Me* opens homophonically; Josquin's *et incarnatus est* in *Missa Pange lingua* goes homophonic at the doctrinal words.
8. **Word-painting and declamation discipline.** Long syllables → longer values; accents on the tactus downbeat; melismas reserved for key words/final syllables; descending motion on *descendit*/*de coelis*, rising on *ascendit* (Josquin's "early form of word painting").
9. **Check the Palestrina constraint set.** Dissonance only as weak-beat passing notes or prepared suspensions resolved down by step; no leaps into dissonance; no exposed tritone (*mi contra fa*); singable ranges and breaths per line. (Codified later by Fux as species counterpoint, HC-021; by Jeppesen from the sources.)
10. **Assemble through-composed; copy into choir books.** String points in text order; final cadence on the modal final; no literal repeats (a recurring motto is the only exception). Parts were copied voice-by-voice into choir books, not as a score.

## 3. Practitioner Examples (how they actually used it)

| Practitioner | Works / Practice |
|---|---|
| **Josquin des Prez** (c.1450–1521) | *Ave Maria…virgo serena* (c.1485) — canonical point-of-imitation motet, each text line stated in turn by all four voices; *Domine, ne in furore* (one voice presents a motive, others imitate); psalm motets *Miserere mei Deus* (5vv, Savonarola-influenced, ostinato reciting-tone phrase), *De profundis*, *Dominus regnavit*; *Praeter rerum seriem* (6vv). "Sections akin to fugal expositions occurring on successive lines of the text." |
| **Adrian Willaert** (c.1490–1562) | *Verbum bonum et suave*, ceremonial motets at St. Mark's Venice; line to the Venetian polychoral (Gabrieli) school. |
| **Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina** (c.1525–1594) | 300+ motets. *Sicut cervus* (descending soggetto on "As the hart longs"), *Canticum canticorum* motets (1584), *O bone Jesu*, *Super flumina Babylonis*. Post-Tridentine "ideal Catholic composer": homophonic declamation for clarity, disciplined dissonance, text defines structure. |
| **Orlande de Lassus** (c.1532–1594) | *Prophetiae Sibyllarum*, psalm motets; expressive declamation within the imitative frame. |
| **Tomás Luis de Victoria** (c.1548–1611) | *O magnum mysterium*, Tenebrae responsories — imitation + homophony with expressive dissonance. |
| **Byrd & Tallis** (England) | *Cantiones sacrae* (1575); English anthem-motets; Tallis *If Ye Love Me* (homophonic opening for word clarity), Byrd *Ave verum corpus* (late apex). |
| **Claudio Monteverdi** (1567–1643) | Early motets (*Cantate Domino*) in imitative motet craft, bridging to the Baroque. |
| **Theory/Pedagogy** | Zarlino, *Le istitutioni harmoniche* (1558) — modal & cadence rules; Fux, *Gradus ad Parnassum* (1725) — Palestrina as eternal model (species counterpoint); Jeppesen (1939); Peter Schubert, *Modal Counterpoint, Renaissance Style* (1999/2008) — the "soggetto + run-through" teaching method. |

## 4. UnitMatrix Mapping (Musicom engine)

**Layer target:** concrete — scored polyphony realizing material through the UnitMatrix; no algorithmic/AI generation involved (free continuation is rule-encoded craft constraint, not stochastic generation).

**Voices & Sections:**
- Voice 0 = **Cantus/Superius** (lead: first entry, word-painting, melismas)
- Voice 1 = **Altus** (second entry at 4th/5th, free continuation)
- Voice 2 = **Tenor** (modal anchor, historically the cantus-firmus descendant; middle entry)
- Voice 3 = **Bassus** (final/root definer, last entry)
- Voice 4 = quinta/sexta vox (5–6vv ceremonial motets)
- Voice 5 = organ *colla parte* doubling (render-time doubling of V0–3, per 16th-c. practice)
- Sections = **one per text clause**: S0 clause 1 point → S1 clause 2 point → … → Sn final clause + final cadence; each section an integer number of breve/tactus units (zero-drift invariant).

**Rules to encode:**
1. Entry stagger — 1–2 breve offsets between entries; no simultaneous soggetto onsets within a point.
2. Entry interval — unison/octave/4th/5th with **diatonic adjustment** (modal transposition table per section).
3. Soggetto identity — one template per section (pitch skeleton + rhythm profile); entries = template + transposition + offset, not four hand-written lines.
4. Free-continuation generation — derived fragments, cadential approach, or run-through anticipation of the next clause.
5. Modal pitch set — per-mode diatonic set, final, reciting tone; cadence degrees (Zarlino); musica ficta only at cadences (raised leading tone).
6. Clausula formula — 2→1 + 7→1 + prepared 4–3 at each point end; final section on the modal final.
7. Jeppesen/Palestrina dissonance rules — weak-beat passing, prepared suspensions, no leap into dissonance, no tritone.
8. Voice ranges & leap compensation — cantus c′–g″, altus g–d″, tenor c–a′, bassus F–d′; range ≤ 10th–12th; stepwise return after leaps > 3rd.
9. Syllabic declamation — accent on tactus, melisma ≤ 4–5 notes on key words.
10. Texture-alternation flag — full-point / duet / homophonic per clause (text clarity driver).
11. Through-composition — no literal section repeats (optional recurring motto).
12. Zero-drift — integer-breve sections; run-through overlaps modeled at cell level (see quirks).

## 7. Quirks & Pitfalls

- **Run-through breaks naive section seams.** The signature motet gesture (one voice anticipating the next clause before the current cadence) crosses section boundaries. Model: last cell of section N carries, in the anticipating voice, both the cadence unit and the first unit of the next soggetto; the next section's grid stays integer-aligned. Do NOT force strictly non-overlapping sections.
- **Not a fugue.** No tonal answer, no countersubject, no episodes/stretto. Applying fugal rules corrupts the text-driven point-chain architecture.
- **Modal, not tonal.** Cadences land on modal finals/reciting degrees (Phrygian color on E in mode 3); musica ficta is local (cadential leading tone), never a global key signature.
- **Underlay is composition.** 16th-c. text-underlay rules (Zarlino/Stoquerus) are compositional decisions; the declamation rule must be active, not cosmetic.
- **Horizontally conceived, vertically heard.** Parts were composed/copied voice-by-voice (choir books), yet the texture is judged vertically; the UnitMatrix's equal-length voices reproduce this correctly.
- **Relation to HC-021 (species counterpoint).** Species = the pedagogical exercise distilled from Palestrina's style; this method = the applied compositional craft in real motets. Constraint set shared; unit of work differs (phrase/point vs cantus-firmus exercise).
