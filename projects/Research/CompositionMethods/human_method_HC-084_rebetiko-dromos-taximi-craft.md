## 1. Method name & tradition

**Rebetiko song-craft** — the urban popular song of the poorest Greeks, from the late 19th century to the 1950s: a modal, orally-composed strophic song whose melody follows a **dromos** (a "road" — a Greek popular mode derived from the Ottoman makam system), opened and interleaved with the **taximi** (a freely improvised unmeasured prelude), set to traditional Greek/Anatolian **dance rhythms** (zeibekiko 9/8–9/4, hasapiko 4/4, hasaposerviko 2/4, tsifteteli 4/4…), and carried by the **bouzouki** — the emblematic instrument that also *is* the rhythm section (in classic Piraeus style there is no drum kit; the bouzouki's strumming patterns are the drums).

- **Tradition / Culture:** Greek urban song — rebetiko/rembetika (ρεμπέτικο). Cradles: the ouzeri, the *tekedes* (hashish dens), the prison, and the coffee shops of the Ottoman port cities — Smyrna (İzmir), Constantinople (Istanbul), and later Piraeus, Athens, Thessaloniki. Two streams fused in the 1930s: the **Smyrna style (Smyrneïka)**, Ottoman café music with violin, oud, kanonaki, santouri, politiki lyra, cimbalom (brought to mainland Greece by the refugees of the 1922 Asia Minor Catastrophe and the 1923 population exchange), and the heavier **Piraeus style** of the mainland *mánges* — bouzouki, baglamas, guitar. Classic phase by the end of the 1930s; Metaxas censorship from 1937; post-war "Westernization" (Tsitsanis) into *laïko*; revival from the 1960s–70s. **UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity (2017).**
- **Key figures:** Markos Vamvakaris, Yiorgos Batis, Anestis Delias, Stratos Pagioumtzis (the "Piraeus Quartet"); Smyrna style — Roza Eskenazi, Rita Abatzi, Marika Papagika, the Smyrneiki Estudiantina, studio directors Panagiotis Toundas & Spyros Peristeris; Vassilis Tsitsanis (bridge to laïko), Sotiria Bellou, Marika Ninou, Manolis Chiotis (4-course bouzouki), Manos Hatzidakis (1948 lecture legitimizing the genre). Scholarship: Gail Holst-Warhaft (*Road to Rembetika*), Elias Petropoulos, Stathis Gauntlett, Risto Pekka Pennanen, Nikos Ordoulidis ("The Greek popular modes", 2011), Tony Klein.

## 3. The craft procedure in detail (how the human does it)

### Stage 0 — apprenticeship (oral/aural, "play by ear")
1. Rebetiko is learned **by ear** inside the milieu — no scores, no written theory ("for most Greek musicologists, urban memory-based music has been the Low Other", Pennanen). Musicians speak of *παίζω με το αυτί* ("pézo me to aftí" — play by ear): rely on experience and alertness, not notation (Ordoulidis).
2. The apprentice absorbs the three-word aesthetic Hatzidakis distilled: **meraki** (love/devotion), **kefi** (joy/verve), **kaimos** (sorrow/longing) — the song must carry all three.
3. Early practice (Petropoulos): in the teké, singers sat in a circle, each adding a verse often unrelated to the previous one, no refrain, simple easy melody — a song could go on **for hours**. The recorded repertoire regularizes this into strophic form with refrains.

### Stage 1 — choose the dromos (the modal "road")
4. Pick the **dromos** (δρόμος, pl. δρόμοι — "road/route") — the modal pathway of the song. Names derive (with a few exceptions) from Turkish makam names: **ousák** (uşşâk), **hitzáz** (hicâz, the "phrygian dominant"), **rast**, **saba(h)**, **kiourdi** (kürdî), **nahawand**; plus Western-style **matzóre** (major) and **minóre** (minor). The makam system is the "melodic core" of any rebetiko composition (Wikipedia; Pennanen).
5. Fix the mode's behavioral grammar — this is the real craft, not a scale:
   - **Idioms** (ιδιωματισμοί, *idhiomatismí*): idiosyncratic note behaviors. E.g., in **ousák** the 2nd degree is unstable — sounds flat in ascent, natural in descent; the 6th degree gravitates toward the 7th in ascending melodies around the tonic (Ordoulidis).
   - **Rast idiom**: in ousák and hitzáz, an ascending movement from the **rast/matzóre pentachord below the tonic** toward it (e.g., G–A–B–C–D approaching D) is a mode-typical gesture, not a modulation.
   - **Dominant notes / élxis** (δεσπόζοντες φθόγγοι, *dhespózontes fthóngi*): the notes a mode "enjoys to go in for" — a central/dominant tone that **draws peripheral notes toward itself** (gravitation); each dromos has its own élxis.
   - **Cadences** (*katalíxis*): each dromos has distinct **interim** (mid-song) and **final** (end) cadential formulas, like the Byzantine *íhoi*.
6. Note the *conceptual* frame: Greek musicians teach the dromoi as 8-note scales, but the operative units are **tetrachord/pentachord behavior + idioms + élxis + cadences** — the song is composed along the road, not inside a scale.

### Stage 2 — set the taximi (the free prelude)
7. Compose/improvise the **taximi** (ταξίμι, from Arabic *taqsim*): a freely improvised, **unmeasured** prelude within the chosen dromos, played at the **beginning or the middle** of the song. Its job: display the player's talent/imagination/skill, and **emphasize the tonality and the dromos** of what follows. It moves through the mode's idiomatic phrases and closes on a cadential formula that launches the metered song.
8. During the taximi the other instruments may hold the **íso** — a single sustained drone note (the tonic) — the only accompaniment under the free melody.

### Stage 3 — set the rhythm (the dance IS the form)
9. Choose the dance rhythm, which dictates meter, tempo and accent grammar:
   - **Zeibekiko** — 9/4 or 9/8, the solo male "eagle" dance, no fixed steps, improvised figures in a one-metre circle; classic grouping **2+2+2+3** (clap pattern: two 8ths, two 8ths, two 8ths, three 8ths). Slow, heavy, kaimos-laden.
   - **Hasapiko** — 4/4 ("the butcher's dance", from Constantinople; *hasapikos varys* slow version); **hasaposerviko** — the fast version in 2/4.
   - **Tsifteteli** — 4/4, the women's dance (from *çiftetelli*).
   - Also: karsilamas/antikristos 9/8, aptalikos 9/4 slow / 9/16 fast, syrtos 4/4, bolero 3/4, kalamatianos 7/8 (3+2+2).
10. **The bouzouki accompaniment is the drum set.** In classic Piraeus style there is no percussion section: the trichordo bouzouki plays rhythmic strumming patterns whose bass-stroke/damped-stroke alternation (the "dum-tek-tek" of zeibekiko) marks the dance's accent groupings. The *komboloi* (worry beads) drawn against a fluted drinking glass adds a glass-click time layer in the teké/taverna and later on records. Smyrna style used the toubeleki (hand drum) instead.

### Stage 4 — compose the song (strophic, syllabic, modal)
11. Write the verses to the **rhythm grid**: 8-syllable or 15-syllable (political-verse) demotic lines, syllabic setting (one note per syllable), phrase-end melisma and ornaments; themes of the marginalized urban world — love, loss, exile, poverty, prison, hashish (the *hasiklidika*), the mother, death — under the meraki/kefi/kaimos triad.
12. Set a **verbatim refrain** (the recorded-era regularization of the teké chain-of-verses): strophic form verse → refrain → verse → refrain; a song may carry several verses with one fixed refrain, or be a chained *amanés*.
13. **Amanedes** (αμανέδες): a distinct sub-form — an improvised, unmeasured lament in a dromos, built on "aman aman" exclamations; the most pointedly oriental songs in the repertoire (banned from some radio stations in 1938).

### Stage 5 — voice the ensemble and fix the arrangement
14. Assign the core trio: **bouzouki** (lead melody + taximi + strummed rhythm), **baglamas** (small high bouzouki — portable, prison-made, easy to hide — doubling the melody an octave up with light ornaments), **guitar** (harmonic-rhythmic bed, later double bass for the root line). Smyrna style substitutes/extends: violin, oud, kanonaki, santouri, politiki lyra, cimbalom, toubeleki.
15. **Harmonize from the mode, not from functional harmony**: rebetiko songs are played on chord-capable instruments but the chord vocabulary "derives from the tonal resources of the modes used" — chords are mode-tone stacks (e.g., in D-ousák songs the tonic chord is D minor-type; in matzóre, D major), *not* a Western functional progression. Cadence chords follow the dromos' cadence formulas.
16. **Band direction by ear**: at the gig, the bouzouki player announces the next song to the band as **"D minóre zeimbékiko"** — tonality + first chord + rhythm — and the band follows by recognizing the song or its modal structure. No sheet music, no key signature talk.
17. Chain the form: **taximi → verse 1 → refrain → instrumental interlude (taximi or orchestra break) → verse 2 → refrain → closing taximi + ritardando cadence**. For dancing sets, the **hasapiko service** — a chained sequence of hasapiko songs played without gap, accelerating from the slow 4/4 hasapiko into the fast 2/4 hasaposerviko — is the standard taverna practice.

## 4. Practitioner examples

- **Markos Vamvakaris** (1905–1972) — the "patriarch of the bouzouki"; his first bouzouki recordings in Greece (October 1932) marked the real beginning of the instrument's recorded career; Piraeus-style zeibekika/hasapika/hasiklidika (*Frangosyriani*, *Vre ti manna sou*); sang in a quiet, hoarse, unforced voice per the teké aesthetic.
- **The Piraeus Quartet (I Tetras tou Pirea)** — Vamvakaris (bouzouki), **Yiorgos Batis** (guitar/baglamas), **Anestis Delias**, **Stratos Pagioumtzis** — the classic 1930s group that fixed the Piraeus sound on shellac.
- **Smyrna style** — **Roza Eskenazi** (Smyrna Trio with violinist Dimitrios Semsis and oudist Agapios Tomboulis), **Rita Abatzi**, **Marika Papagika** (1918 *Smyrna Minore*), the **Smyrneiki Estudiantina**; studio directors **Panagiotis Toundas** and **Spyros Peristeris** shaped recorded rebetiko from the mid-1920s.
- **Vassilis Tsitsanis** (1915–1984) — brilliant bouzouki player and prolific composer (hundreds of songs), developed the post-war "Westernization" that set the base for *laïko*; *Synnefiasmeni Kyriaki* ("Cloudy Sunday") is the canonical example; mentored singers **Sotiria Bellou**, **Marika Ninou**, **Ioanna Georgakopoulou**, **Stella Haskil**.
- **Manolis Chiotis** — in 1953/56 added the **fourth string course** to the bouzouki (tetrachordo), guitar-like playing techniques, Latin (rumba/mambo) influences; set the stage for electrification and the bouzouki's wider use by **Mikis Theodorakis** and **Manos Hatzidakis**.
- **Manos Hatzidakis** — 1948 lecture that shook the establishment and legitimized rebetiko as art; adapted classic rembetika in his 1951 piano suite *Six Folk Paintings*.
- **Revival** — Grigoris Bithikotsis (1960 Vamvakaris recordings), **Stelios Kazantzidis** (popularized *Synnefiasmeni Kyriaki*), Giorgos Zambetas; scholars Holst-Warhaft, Petropoulos, Gauntlett.
- **UNESCO** — rebetiko inscribed on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, 2017.

## 5. UnitMatrix mapping

### Voices
- **V0 = Bouzouki lead spine** — the dromos carrier: taximi melody (free cell) + song melody; also strummed rhythm in its low register when doubling as accompaniment (trichordo practice).
- **V1 = Second bouzouki / guitar accompaniment** — per-rhythm strum dictionary (zeibekiko 2+2+2+3 stroke pattern, hasapiko 4/4, hasaposerviko 2/4, tsifteteli 4/4); chord skeleton built from the dromos' tones.
- **V2 = Vocal lead** — strophic verses + verbatim refrain; syllabic 8-/15-syllable setting; melisma and ornament at phrase ends; the meraki/kefi/kaimos carrier.
- **V3 = Baglamas / tzouras** — high octave doubling of V0/V2 with sparse ornaments; the "tinny" top layer.
- **V4 = Íso drone** — sustained tonic pedal, active during taximi cells (and optional under verses); the only accompaniment in free sections.
- **V5 = Bass (double bass)** — root movement/walking line under V1's chords (later-period laïko-ward texture; optional).
- **V6 = Komboloi / toubeleki percussion** — glass-click or hand-drum pattern marking the dance grouping (strict, sparse).
- **V7 = Metadata** — dromos tag, idiom tags, rhythm tag, section tags, taximi markers, the "D minóre zeimbékiko" band-call string.

### Sections
Columns = the **rebetiko song chain** (meter constant per song; bar length = 9/8·9/4 or 4/4 or 2/4 grid, zero-drift trivial):
S0 Taximi (free, unmeasured; V0 solo over V4 drone; opens and closes on the dromos cadence) → S1 Verse 1 → S2 Refrain (verbatim repeat) → S3 Interlude (instrumental: taximi variant or orchestra break, V2 silent) → S4 Verse 2 → S5 Refrain → S6 Closing Taximi / Cadence (ritardando + final katalixis). **Hasapiko service**: chain the S1–S5 block twice more (S7–S11) with tempo/meter acceleration from slow hasapiko (4/4) to hasaposerviko (2/4) — a multi-song column set.

### Rules to encode
1. **Dromos pitch-set constraint:** each voice's melodic material restricted to the chosen dromos' degrees (hitzáz: 1–♭2–3–♯4–5–♭6–♭7; ousák: 1–♭2–♭3–4–5–♭6–♭7 with unstable ♭2; rast/matzóre major; minóre nahawand minor; saba, kiourdi variants), transposed to the fixed tonic.
2. **Idiom filters:** unstable-2nd behavior (flat ascending / natural descending in ousák); rast-pentachord-below-tonic gesture allowed only in ousák/hitzáz; élxis gravitation — approach the dominant tone from below, cadence to tonic per dromos formula (interim vs final katalíxis).
3. **Taximi cell:** S0/S3/S6 realized as free-rhythm units — for the engine, quantize the taximi skeleton to long-note values over the V4 drone with rubato (humanize), or hold V0 as a free cell padded to the column terminal landmark; must start and end on dromos-legal tones.
4. **Rhythm-grid lock:** per-song constant grid — zeibekiko 9/8 or 9/4 with accent grouping 2+2+2+3 (accent map on the 1st, 3rd, 5th, and 7th–9th eighths); hasapiko 4/4; hasaposerviko 2/4; tsifteteli 4/4; karsilamas 9/8 (2+2+2+3 or 4+2+3 variant) — all voices' accents obey the group.
5. **Strophic + verbatim refrain:** V2 verses vary per stanza; refrain cells are byte-identical across repeats (identity anchor); verse line = 8 or 15 syllables, one syllable per note except cadential melisma.
6. **Dromos-derived harmony:** V1 chords = mode-tone stacks, not functional Western progressions (no ii–V–I grammar); cadence chords follow the dromos' katalíxis set; tonic chord quality fixed by the dromos (minóre/ousák → minor-type tonic, matzóre/rast → major-type).
7. **Baglamas doubling:** V3 = V0/V2 transposed +12 (or +24), sparser (one ornament per phrase), never below V0.
8. **Interlude separation:** in S3/S6 V2 rests (instrumental blocks); V0 leads; V1/V6 continue the rhythm — call-and-answer between voice and bouzouki at phrase gaps.
9. **Acceleration (hasapiko service):** chained columns ramp 4/4 slow → 2/4 fast; tempo marking per column; ritardando only in the final cadence column.
10. **Zero-drift + humanize:** bouzouki/guitar/komboloi strict (rhythm anchors), vocal loose (rubato, portamento, phrase displacement), baglamas slight layback; taximi cells rubato-heavy. `validate()` gate before export.

## 6. Quirks & pitfalls

- **Dromos ≠ scale:** the dromos is a behavioral road (idioms, élxis, cadences), not an octachord — a naive pitch-set filter alone produces dull, un-rebetiko melody; encode the idioms (unstable 2nd, rast gesture, cadence formulas) as phrase-level rules.
- **No functional harmony:** the chords are mode-derived; forcing a Western ii–V–I under a hitzáz melody is the #1 genre-breaker. Also, Ottoman microtones (9-comma divisions) are approximated to equal temperament on the bouzouki — the engine should quantize to 12-TET and not try to be "authentically" microtonal here.
- **Rhythm lives in the strings:** classic Piraeus recordings have no drum kit — the bouzouki strum patterns ARE the drums. Adding a full drum kit reads as laïko/archontorebetiko, not classic rebetiko.
- **Playing by ear ≠ fixed score:** the tradition has no written theory and treats scores as memory aids; model the *grammar* (dromos + rhythm + form), and expect every performance to vary ornamentation freely.
- **Censorship era:** post-1937 recordings are self-censored (hashish/crime lyrics vanish) — a text-generation concern, not a music one; the music itself was never censored.
- **Zeibekiko vs hasapiko feel:** zeibekiko is solo, slow, introverted, "eagle" dance with no set steps; hasapiko is the chained line dance — don't mix the accent grammars.
- **Two bouzouki types:** trichordo (3 courses, classic Piraeus) vs tetrachordo (4 courses, post-1953 Chiotis) — the chord vocabulary differs; scope the method to the classic trichordo texture.
