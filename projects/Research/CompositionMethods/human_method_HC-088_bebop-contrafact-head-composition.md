## 1. Method name & tradition

**Bebop contrafact composition** — the jazz composer's craft of writing a *new melody* (the "head") over an
*existing, memorised harmonic progression* (a Tin Pan Alley standard, "rhythm changes", a blues, or another
jazzman's changes). The term *contrafact* (from the medieval *contrafactum*: new text to an existing melody)
was adopted by musicology for this bop procedure: **a new composition consisting of a new melody overlaid on
a familiar harmonic structure.**

- Why it dominated 1940s bop: chord progressions are **not copyrightable**, melodies are. A contrafact gave
  the bebop musician a *new, publishable, royalty-earning original* on which he could **immediately improvise**
  (he already knew the changes cold), without paying publisher fees or clearing rights on the standard.
- The harmonic skeleton is called the **vehicle**. The most contrafacted vehicle of all is **rhythm changes**
  (the AABA progression of Gershwin's *I Got Rhythm*, 1930) — second in jazz only to the 12-bar blues as a
  basic harmonic structure; it spawned hundreds of heads (Parker's *Anthropology*, *Dexterity*,
  *Moose the Mooche*, *Steeplechase*; Monk's *Rhythm-a-Ning*; Rollins' *Oleo*; Ellington's *Cottontail*).
- The method is a **composition/selection procedure**, not an algorithm and not sound production: the human
  composes a melody by ear against a chord skeleton he carries in his memory, testing it against a rhythm
  section until it swings.

## 3. The craft procedure, step by step (how the human does it)

1. **Choose a harmonic vehicle** — a progression you know by heart: a 32-bar AABA standard (*I Got Rhythm*,
   *Honeysuckle Rose*, *Indiana*, *How High the Moon*, *Cherokee*, *What Is This Thing Called Love*,
   *Sweet Georgia Brown*, *All of Me*), an ABAC standard, a 12-bar blues, or a jazzman's original changes
   (Dameron's *Lady Bird* — itself the vehicle for Miles' *Half Nelson*). Rhythm changes and blues are the
   default school exercises.
2. **Internalize the changes** — play them until the form and the voice-leading are automatic: the guide
   tones (3rd & 7th) of every chord, where the II–V–I motion sits, where the turnarounds land, the bridge
   modulation. The composer must *hear* the progression, not read it.
3. **Compose the head over the skeleton** — write a melody that is rhythmically angular and harmonically
   explicit, using the bop melodic grammar:
   - **Chord tones on strong beats** (root/3rd/5th/7th/9th/13th); chromatic passing and approach tones on
     offbeats;
   - **Approach-note vocabulary**: chromatic approach from a half-step above or below the target,
     **enclosures** (circle the target: above–below or below–above), the "bebop scale" (major/dominant
     scale with an added chromatic passing tone between 7 and 8) for running eighth-notes;
   - **"Running the changes"**: arpeggiate each chord's extended harmony (e.g., the famous descending
     dominant-7th arpeggios in the *rhythm-changes* bridge);
   - **Anticipation & back-phrasing**: attack on the "&" of beat 4, land on the next bar's downbeat;
     rest on beat 1 as often as you hit it — bop melody is defined by *what it leaves out* of the downbeat;
   - **Wide leaps and octave displacement**; long note then a burst of eighth-notes (the "quota" gesture);
   - **Blue inflections** (♭3/♭5 approach) kept in reserve for color.
4. **Recompose the changes when useful** (contrafact is not a mere "new tune on old chords" — bop composers
   routinely *reharmonized* the vehicle): add **II–V substitutions**, **tritone substitutions**, back-cycling;
   **compress** the form (*Ko-Ko* squeezes Cherokee's 64 bars into 32), **splice** vehicles (*Scrapple from
   the Apple*: Honeysuckle Rose A-section onto the I Got Rhythm bridge), or **replace the bridge**
   ("rhythm changes, different bridge" variants). Bar count and section length are the invariants that survive.
5. **Test it with the rhythm section** — play the head at tempo; it must *swing*, lie well on the horn, and
   be hummable. Adjust phrase lengths, contours, and chord hits until it locks with walking bass and ride
   cymbal. The head is a *tune*, not an exercise: if you can't hum it, it isn't a head.
6. **Improvise on it immediately** — the entire point of the device: new tune, familiar changes = instant
   improvisability. Parker's solos routinely *quote and develop the head's own motives* — the head seeds the
   solo vocabulary (head-chorus-solo-chorus-head arrangement).
7. **Publish/record it as an original** — new melody = new copyright, no permission or fees for the vehicle;
   recording labels and ASCAP-era royalties were a real driver (musicians were paid for originals, and the
   AFM recording ban of 1942–44 made new repertoire valuable).
8. **Pedagogical loop** — jazz education codified this as *the* composition exercise: "write a head over
   rhythm changes / a blues / Lady Bird changes"; and analysis runs the reverse direction (take any bop tune,
   find its source changes — Owens' and Williams' contrafact studies).

## 4. Practitioner examples

- **Charlie Parker** — the master of the method: *Ornithology* (with Benny Harris, 1946, on *How High the
  Moon*), *Ko-Ko* (1945, Cherokee **compressed** 64→32 bars), *Steeplechase*, *Dexterity*, *Moose the
  Mooche*, *Anthropology* (rhythm changes), *Donna Lee* (Back Home Again in Indiana; Davis credited,
  authorship debated), *Scrapple from the Apple* (1947, Honeysuckle Rose + rhythm-changes bridge splice),
  *Bird of Paradise* (All of Me), *Blues for Alice* (12-bar blues reharmonized with the "Bird blues"
  II–V cycle).
- **Dizzy Gillespie** — *Salt Peanuts* (rhythm changes variant, with Kenny Clarke), *Woody 'n' You*;
  his band premiered Dameron's *Hot House* (1945) on *What Is This Thing Called Love*.
- **Tadd Dameron** — *Hot House*; and *Lady Bird* (1939), an *original* progression that itself became the
  vehicle for Miles Davis' *Half Nelson* (1947) — the composer-as-vehicle-provider role.
- **Thelonious Monk** — *In Walked Bud* (1947, Blue Skies), *Evidence* (Just You, Just Me), *Bright
  Mississippi* (1961, Sweet Georgia Brown), *Rhythm-a-Ning* (1957, rhythm changes), *Hackensack*
  (Oh, Lady Be Good).
- **Miles Davis** — *Half Nelson* (Lady Bird), *Donna Lee* (Indiana), *Four* (rhythm-derived);
  *Milestones*/*So What* modal vehicles later spawned Coltrane's *Impressions* (1961, So What changes).
- **Sonny Rollins** — *Oleo* (1954, rhythm changes), recorded on *Saxophone Colossus* beside his own
  originals — head-writing and vehicle-writing side by side.
- **Duke Ellington** (pre-bop roots of the device) — *Cottontail* (1940, rhythm changes), *In a Mellow Tone*
  (1939, Rose Room); James Moody — *Moody's Mood for Love* (I'm in the Mood for Love changes).
- **Historical precedent** — medieval *contrafactum* (new sacred text over secular melody); Gounod's
  *Ave Maria* over Bach's C-major Prelude BWV 846 (1853); 20th-c. classical contrafacts (Berio quoting
  Mahler, Crumb quoting Chopin nocturnes).

## 5. Musical Elements Framework reading

- **HARMONY** — the *fixed spine*: the borrowed progression (roman-numeral skeleton per section), guide
  tones, II–V–I motion; optional recomposition operators (tritone sub, II–V insertion, back-cycling,
  compression/splice) that preserve bar count.
- **PITCH** — the *composed layer*: the head melody, built by chord-tone targeting + chromatic approach/
  enclosure vocabulary + extension tones + octave displacement.
- **RHYTHM** — bop time-feel: swing eighths, anticipations, back-phrasing, rest-on-the-downbeat,
  long-note-then-runs density gestures.
- **STRUCTURE** — the 32-bar AABA / ABAC chorus or 12-bar blues, locked by the vehicle; head–solo–head
  arrangement.
- **TEXTURE** — head in unison or bop-harmonized (thirds/sixths, or four-horn "sax soli" block chords),
  over comping + walking bass + ride cymbal; solo sections thin the texture to rhythm section + soloist.

## 6. UnitMatrix mapping

**Voices:**
- **V0 — Lead horn (head melody).** Alto sax / trumpet / tenor. Carries the composed contrafact head in
  head sections and the improvised line in solo sections. This row *is* the new composition.
- **V1 — Piano (or guitar) comping.** One chord per bar (or per half-bar at turnarounds) from the borrowed
  changes; shell/four-note voicings, comping rhythm (off-beat stabs, sustained guide-tone clusters), silent
  under exposed head phrases where the lead is unaccompanied.
- **V2 — Bass.** Walking line (quarter-notes) derived from the same skeleton: roots on strong beats, approach
  tones/chromatic passing on weak beats, II–V approach patterns, occasional pedal/breaks.
- **V3 — Drums (channel 9).** Ride cymbal swing pattern + hi-hat 2&4 + sparse snare/bass-drum comping; the
  time-feel anchor; continues through rests.
- **V4 — Second horn (optional).** Bop harmony in thirds/sixths below the lead, or unison doubling; or a
  four-horn block-chord "soli" section (sax-section voicing, drop-2).

**Sections (AABA vehicle, 8 bars each; blues vehicle = 12-bar units):**
- S0 = Head A1 (statement, phrase 1+2), S1 = Head A2 (repeat/sequence with new ending), S2 = Bridge B
  (contrast: new register/contour, rhythm-changes bridge = dominant cycle arpeggios), S3 = Head A3
  (final A, cadence). Then S4 = Solo chorus 1 (lead improvisation over identical skeleton), S5 = Solo
  chorus 2 (piano solo / different voice), S6 = Head out (final head chorus, often with shout/ensemble
  punctuation before the last A). Each section = 8 bars × 1920 ticks = 15,360 ticks at 480 tpb/♩=120–200
  (bop tempos 150–300+; ♩≈180–220 typical for heads).

**Rules (what the engine would encode):**
- **Changes-lock:** harmony skeleton fixed from the source vehicle (roman-numeral table per section); the
  contrafact changes *melody only* unless a recomposition operator is explicitly logged (tritone sub,
  II–V insertion, back-cycle, compression/splice) — and any operator must preserve section bar counts
  (the UnitMatrix zero-drift invariant).
- **Chord-tone anchor:** strong beats (1, 3 of the bar and downbeats of phrases) land on chord tones
  (3rd/7th = guide tones preferred; 9th/13th as color); offbeats may be chromatic.
- **Approach grammar:** every melodic target approached by half-step from above or below, or enclosed
  (above–below / below–above); runs use the bebop scale (diatonic + chromatic passing tone).
- **Anticipation rule:** phrase-final notes may attack on the "&" of 4 and resolve across the barline;
  beat 1 of most bars is rest or pickup, not an attacked downbeat (density of downbeat attacks ≤ ~50%).
- **Density gesture:** phrases alternate long values with bursts of swing eighths (2–8 notes); monotone
  rhythmic density rejected.
- **Form lock:** 32-bar AABA/ABAC or 12-bar blues; sections equal length; turnaround on section ends
  (V7–I or bVI–bII–I etc. per vehicle); head–solo–head arrangement order.
- **Bridge contrast:** bridge section must change register/contour vs A sections (e.g., rhythm-changes
  bridge = dominant-cycle arpeggiation); same skeleton, new melodic character.
- **Comping separation:** V1 silent or sparse during unaccompanied head phrases; V2 walks continuously;
  V3 ride pattern continuous (swing ratio ~0.65 long : 0.35 short in performance).
- **Playability:** lead range within instrument range; no long sustained pedal tones; heads typically
  ≤ octave-and-a-half tessitura with occasional leap.
- **Humanize:** loose jazz profile — lead σ 8–15 ms timing, ~10–20 ms layback, swing eighth durations;
  bass steady with slight layback; ride cymbal tight (σ 2–4 ms). Swing feel must be applied consistently
  across V0/V2 melody eighths, else the head reads straight.

## 7. Quirks & pitfalls

- **The copyright engine is the method's fuel.** Harmony is uncopyrightable, melody is not — so the bop era
  produced hundreds of heads in a few years, many dashed off in the studio (Parker reportedly composed heads
  minutes before recording dates). Expect short, functional heads, not developed compositions.
- **Authorship disputes are endemic** (*Donna Lee* Parker vs Davis; *Ornithology* Parker vs Harris; *Four*).
  Record-credit and oral attribution conflict; treat attributions as "credited to".
- **Compression breaks source geometry.** *Ko-Ko* (64→32 bars) shows the vehicle can be structurally edited;
  a contrafact analyzer must track the edit (source bars → target bars map), and the UnitMatrix sections
  (not the source tune) are the invariant.
- **A contrafact must stand alone.** If the melody is weak the trick is exposed — the test is hummability
  and swing, not harmonic cleverness. Parker's heads survive because they are *tunes*.
- **Swing notation problem.** Tick-grid MIDI with equal eighths reads as straight; swing must be encoded
  either as duration ratios or via the humanizer's swing profile, consistently with the ride pattern —
  otherwise the head sounds mechanical (the exact failure the humanize layer exists to prevent).
- **Vehicle concentration.** Bop repertoire leans heavily on a handful of skeletons (rhythm changes, blues,
  Cherokee, Honeysuckle Rose, How High the Moon). Modern practice composes *new* vehicles (Dameron's
  Lady Bird, Coltrane's Giant Steps) and then contrafacts *those* — the method scales to any fixed skeleton.
- **Terminology collision:** "contrafact" in musicology also covers medieval *contrafactum* and classical
  recompositions (Gounod/Bach, Berio/Mahler) — same device, different eras.
