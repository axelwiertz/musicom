# HC-058: Samba Batucada Bateria Arrangement — Carnival Percussion Suite (Escola de Samba Craft)

## Layer
**human (→concrete)** — target layer: concrete (UnitMatrix realization of layered percussion writing).

## Tradition
Afro-Brazilian — Rio de Janeiro / São Paulo, 1920s–present; samba de enredo / batucada of the escola de samba bateria; UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage (2012 samba, expanded 2022). Rooted in 19th-century Afro-Brazilian batuque, lundu, maxixe, and the Candomblé terreiros of "Little Africa" (Praça Onze, Cidade Nova). Heirs include pagode, samba-rock, MPB bateria sections, and global samba bateria groups.

## Description
The bateria (or batucada) is a large single-section percussion orchestra of 50–300 drummers that provides the rhythmic engine for a samba school's carnival parade. Every year the school's *mestre de bateria* (master drum-conductor) writes a new arrangement for the year's samba de enredo theme song — a 2–4 minute composition cycled for the ~90-minute desfile. The mestre's craft is not note-by-note notation (most ritmistas are ear-learners) but **part stratification**: a fixed hierarchy of interlocking rhythmic layers (surdo foundation → caixa engine → repinique call-and-response → tamborim colour → shaker sheen) over the samba de enredo's A–B–A–B verse–chorus song form. The mestre also composes breaks (*paradinhas/viradas*), cue signals, and density arcs. The arrangement is taught by call-and-repeat across rehearsal months.

## Craft Steps (how the human does it)

### 1. Fix the song — the samba de enredo
The school's board selects or commissions the year's samba de enredo (competitive song selection via *disputa* heats). The mestre receives the final 2–4 min A–B–A–B or A–B–B–C form with:
- Two distinct verse melodies (Parte A, Parte B, typically 8 bars each)
- A shorter repeated chorus (Refrão, often 4 bars)
- A bridge / *cabeça* (head) if present
- Lyrics in *quadra/métrica* 6–8 syllable lines (Portuguese)

### 2. Set the andamento (tempo anchor)
The mestre fixes the parade tempo: **100–140 bpm** (Grupo Especial schools ~120–130). This determines the surdo foot-pulse grid and every part's subdivision feel.

### 3. Write the surdo spine (the "feet")
The surdo section is three size-pitched drums played with mallets:
- **Surdo 2 (Segunda)** — smaller, higher tune; plays beat **1** (the "call"). Pitch ~D2–F2.
- **Surdo 1 (Primeira)** — larger, deeper; plays beat **2** (the "response" / answer). Pitch ~C2–Eb2.
- **Surdo 3 (Terceira / Cortador)** — smallest, highest; plays **syncopated "cutting" variations** between beats (rhythmic gloss & swing).

The mestre writes the surdo 1+2 two-beat pattern first — this is the **heartbeat**, unchangeable, the timing anchor for 300 people. The surdo 3 *corte* line is composed to fill the gap, adding swing density but never overriding the 1+2 anchor.

### 4. Layer the caixa (the "engine")
Next, the caixa (snare drum — with snares engaged, metal/tarol shell) gets its part. The caixa plays continuous 16th-note subdivisions (semínima pontuada / sextina feel) with syncopated accents. The mestre selects the *pegada* (grip/groove flavour):
- **Caixa em cima** (popular in Mangueira): snare held high, bright dry sound, strict 16th-note grid with accent on 2&4.
- **Caixa marcado** (Salgueiro style): heavier backbeat, more accent on the "and" of beats.
- Provides the "swing" — the subdivisional motor.

Every bateria's caixa style is a school identity marker (Mangueira vs Portela vs Imperatriz).

### 5. Add repinique (the "conductor")
The repinique (tenor drum, stick+hand technique) serves as the mestre's executive instrument:
- Reinforces the basic pulse and 16th-note engine alongside caixa.
- Plays **chamadas** (calls) — distinct opening figures that start the samba and cue section transitions.
- Leads **paradinhas** (breaks): stop-time call figures where bateria responds with a composed unison rhythm (call-and-response).
- The Mestre himself often plays repique to signal the arrangement's form.

### 6. Compose tamborim "lines" (the "design")
The tamborim section is the **colour voice**. The mestre writes specific rhythmic "lines" (*designs* or *levadas*) — syncopated patterns that float over the groove:
- **Carreteiro** (most common): a standard 4-beat syncopated pattern: `X..X.X..X..X.X..` (where X = hit).
- **Partido alto**: more open, off-beat heavy pattern derived from samba de partido alto tradition.
- Tamborim lines can change per section (verse → chorus → bridge) making them a form-marking device.

Each tamborim player also *virada* (turns the drum during performance) to alter pitch/timbre, adding textural variety.

### 7. Add the shimmer layer — chocalho, ganza, agogô, reco-reco
Continuous-shake and fixed-pattern parts fill the high-frequency space:
- **Chocalho/Ganza**: constant shake subdivision (16th/8th triplet grids) — the "shimmer blanket." Never stops.
- **Agogô**: syncopated two-note bell pattern (high–low interlock).
- **Reco-reco**: scrape pattern accenting clave-like phrases.
The mestre decides which of these are active in which section (on/off mask).

### 8. Compose breaks (paradinhas / viradas)
The mestre writes 2–4 bar **break patterns** that punctuate the arrangement:
- **Paradinha** (break): all instruments except surdo 1+2 stop on a cued call; bateria plays a brief composed unison phrase, then resumes. Functions: section divider, energy reset, dramatic punctuation.
- **Virada** (turn): a 1–2 bar leading phrase that ends a section and "turns" to the next (verse→chorus). Usually surdo-led: a rhythmic cadence ending on beat 3.
- The repique plays the call; the bateria responds with the composed break phrase.
- The mestre also composes the **parada final** (final cadence / stinger cadence) for the song's end.

### 9. Arrange the section arc
The mestre designs the arrangement's dynamic and textural arc over the samba de enredo form:

| Song Section | Typical Bateria Treatment |
|---|---|
| *Intro / Cabeça* (4–8 bars) | Repique solo call + surdo beat alone → caixa enters → tamborim enters → full bateria at verse |
| *Parte A* (verse 1, 8 bars) | Full groove (surdo+caixa+repique+shimmer), tamborim carreteiro, moderate density |
| *Refrão* (chorus, 4–8 bars) | Full bateria, the densest: tamborim "designed" line changes, chocalho shake continuous, highest energy |
| *Parte B* (verse 2, 8 bars) | Slightly reduced register (maybe drop agogô), or a paradinha half-way, then rebuild |
| *Refrão* (chorus repeat) | Full again, optional virada into bridge |
| *Ponte* (bridge / terceira parte) | Paradinha or tamborim-only call–response; contrast section |
| *Refrão Final* (final chorus) | Maximum density + register: all instruments, surdo 3 most active, tamborim decorated |
| *Parada Final* (coda, 2–4 bars) | Composed unison cadential figure, final crash on beat 3 |

The mestre also decides the **number of repeats** of the complete song cycle (a 90-minute parade cycles the samba enredo ~30–40 times, but the arrangement's section-level dynamics apply per cycle).

### 10. Teach by ear (oral transmission)
The mestre teaches the arrangement over months of rehearsal (June → February Carnival):
- Plays each part on a demonstration instrument.
- Battery learns by call-and-repeat (no notation — *orelhada* method).
- Section leaders (*diretores de naipe*) drill their sections.
- Full bateria runs the composition at increasing tempo, locked to the school's *puxador* (lead singer) + *cordas* (cavaquinho + guitar + violão) harmonic section.

### 11. Final polish — cue discipline & walking order
The mestre drills the walking order (highest-pitch instruments closest to conductor, surdos farthest back) and visual cue signals (apito whistle: single blast = attention, double = paradinha, long trill = virada, three shorts = parada final). In parade, the bateria plays the arrangement by memory, cued by the mestre facing backwards at the front.

#### Rules (encoding the method)

1. **Surdo invariance** — V0 (surdo 1+2) strikes beat-1 and beat-2 ONLY, every bar, never rests, never decorates. Offsets: `surdo_2_tick = bar_start`, `surdo_1_tick = bar_start + half_bar`. Violation = destroyed timing reference.

2. **Caixa continuous-subdivision** — V2 must fire every 16th-note (or at minimum every 8th-note) with zero gaps. The accent profile can shift but the grid never skips.

3. **Repique entrance gate** — V3 plays ONLY in (a) intro chamada, (b) paradinha calls, (c) virada lead-ins, (d) final parada. Tacet during full groove sections.

4. **Tamborim design per section** — V4 pattern switches at section boundaries. No design change mid-section. Maximum 4 distinct designs per arrangement (carreteiro, partido-alto, chorus-special, bridge-response).

5. **Shimmer continuity** — V5 (chocalho/ganza) continuous during all non-break sections. Muted exclusively during paradinha break bars.

6. **Paradinha break composition** — When a paradinha is called (V3 call), V0 continues surdo 1+2 anchor, ALL other voices (V1, V2, V3, V4, V5, V6) play a 1–2 bar composed unison phrase then **cut to silence** for 1–2 beats. Groove resumes. Duration: ≤ 2 bars.

7. **Virada cadence rule** — At section-end virada, all voices accent on `beat_3_tick` (3rd crotchet of the bar). This is the universal "turn" signal in Rio samba.

8. **Density arc monotonic** — Per song cycle, density rises from S0 (thin) → S2 (max) → S3/S4 (sustain) → S5 (drop) → S6 (max) → S7 (cadence). No global energy peak before the 70% mark of the cycle.

9. **Walking order not encoded in MIDI** — Physical layout (repique front → shakers → tamborim → caixa → surdos back) is a performance-direction concern; pitch register in MIDI assignment approximates the sonic stratification.

10. **Agogô section mask** — V6 active during Refrão sections only. Never during verse.

11. **Parada final convergence** — Last 2 bars: all voices converge to a composed unison phrase landing on beat-3 as a single crash (all instruments simultaneously). Zero drift — every voice ends on the same tick.

12. **Tempo stability** — Andamento must hold ±2 bpm throughout; no accelerando/ritardando in competition samba. (Some contemporary escolas add micro-variance in esquenta warm-up, but the arrangement proper is isochronous.)

### Pitch Mapping
Since bateria instruments are mostly unpitched percussion, the UnitMatrix "PITCH" element is approximated as:
- **Surdo 1–3**: ascending pitch band (MIDI note 36, 43, 48 — representative anchor pitches for render; actual is timbral not melodic)
- **Caixa / Repinique**: MIDI note 47 (shared, distinguished by articulation/channel)
- **Tamborim**: MIDI note 77 (high-pitched "tock")
- **Chocalho / Agogô / Ganza**: MIDI notes 82 / 68+72 (shaker/bells)
- Rhythm values encode the part timbrally through the percussion channel assignments

### Potential Pitfalls / Quirks

- **No pitch melody**: The samba bateria arrangement is purely rhythmic — pitch functions as register stratification (low→high = surdo→caixa→tamborim). The UnitMatrix voice model can still encode this via MidiPercussion instrument selection and velocity/articulation, but the "Pitch" element is used as register ID, not melodic content.
- **Oral tradition translates imperfectly to rule-systems**: Many mestre choices ("this tamborim design sounds right here") are aural/heuristic, not codified. The rules above are an approximation for a software system.
- **No song melody**: The bateria does not play the samba de enredo *melody* — that belongs to the *puxador* (lead singer) + cordas (cavaquinho + violão + guitar). The UnitMatrix design should not attempt to encode the song melody; it only handles bateria percussion.
- **Paradinha timing is critical**: In a real bateria of 300 people, the break's stop and restart must be flawlessly synchronized. The 1–2 beat silence gap must be an *exact* number of ticks — even a 1-tic offset from any voice = trainwreck.
- **Surdo numbering confusion**: Some escolas label surdo 1 as the highest-pitched and 2 as the lowest; others (the majority) use 1=lowest, 2=middle. This document uses the majority convention. Check the specific escola's convention when modeling.
- **No cuica included** (optional): Cuica friction drum is occasionally deployed mid-arrangement as a colour effect but is not core structural; not included in this voice map to keep the model focused.
- **Carnival duration**: The arrangement is written for a ~2-minute song that loops ~30× over 90 minutes. The UnitMatrix model described here covers a single song-cycle; extension to 30 cycles is a simple loop of section-sequence S0→S1→S2→S3→S4→S5→S6 (with S7 as final coda only on the last loop).
