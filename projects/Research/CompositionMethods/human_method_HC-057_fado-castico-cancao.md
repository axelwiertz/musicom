# HC-057: Fado (Portuguese Urban Song) — Fado Castiço & Fado-Canção Composition Craft

- **Layer:** human (→concrete)

## Description

Fado is Lisbon's urban song tradition, born in the early 19th-century working-class neighbourhoods of Mouraria, Alfama and Bairro Alto. Unlike most song traditions where melody AND words are a fixed pair, traditional fado (fado castiço, also called fado fado, fado clássico, or fado tradicional) operates on a **split-author principle**: the melody is a fixed "mother melody" from a shared repertoire of ~200 known tunes, while the poem (lyric) is freely chosen by the fadista (singer) each performance. The same melody can carry a hundred different poems over a career. This separation — fixed music × free text — is the tradition's architectural spine.

The essential instrumental pairing is the pear-shaped 12-string **guitarra portuguesa** (Portuguese guitar), which provides a characteristic recurring melodic-harmonic motif, and the **viola** (classical/Spanish guitar), which supplies steady harmonic rhythm and bass reinforcement. Together they create the unmistakable fado sonority: the guitarra weaves an invariant cyclic pattern in I–V harmony while the fadista bends, ornaments and improvises melodically on a pre-existing tune.

Fado splits into two main branches:
- **Fado castiço** (tradicional): the root form — ~200 fixed melodies, no chorus, free-verse, the fadista can estilar (stylise/ornament the melody freely)
- **Fado-canção** (song-fado): composer-created pieces where melody & lyrics are a composed inseparable pair, usually with a chorus (refrão)

## Craft Procedure (Step-by-Step)

### Phase 1: Fix the Fado Form (Castiço)

1. **Choose the melodic fado.** Select from the ~200 known traditional melody types. Each has a known name (Fado Menor, Fado Corrido, Fado Mouraria, Fado Bailado, Fado Cravo, Fado Pajem, Fado Vitória, Fado Lopes, Fado Alberto, Fado Modesto, etc.).
2. **Determine the tonal-harmonic axis.**
   - **Fado Menor** — minor key (usually Dm), slow/moderate tempo, sad/melancholic character, Dm–A7 harmonic base.
   - **Fado Corrido** — major key, fast tempo, cheerful/lively character, I–V scheme.
   - **Fado Mouraria** — major key, moderate tempo, serene longing, I–V scheme with Allegro marking and room for desgarrada duels.
3. **Fix the metre and duration.** All traditional fados are in 4/4 time with the same fixed-length harmonic cycle (typically 8–16 bars I–V alternation before the repeat).

### Phase 2: Select the Poem (For Castiço)

4. **Choose the poetic genre.** Typical lyric structures:
   - **Quadra** (quatrain) — 4 lines × 7 syllables each = the most basic, used by Fado Menor.
   - **Quintilha** — 5 lines.
   - **Sextilha** — 6 lines.
   - **Décima** — 10 lines.
   - **Setessílabo** — 7-syllable lines.
   - **Decassílabo** — 10-syllable lines.
5. **Match syllable count to melody length.** The poem's metrical scheme must fit the melody's phrase structure — the fadista must know both to align prosody.
6. **Interpret the poem's emotional content** to inform phrasing and ornamentation. The poem can be by a known poet (erudite poetry adapted) or improvised on the spot.

### Phase 3: Guitarra Accompaniment Craft

7. **Set Lisbon tuning.** Guitarra strings (6 courses × 2 strings each): D A B E A D (low→high). The instrument is a pear-shaped cittern with 12 steel strings.
8. **Compose/recall the fixed accompaniment motif.** The hallmark of fado castiço: a short melodic-harmonic motif repeatedly cycled over I–V, played as a continuous arpeggio/rasgueado pattern with subtle variations. The accompanist never writes a separate accompaniment — the motif IS the accompaniment.
9. **Play the right-hand pattern.** The foundation is thumb+index technique (with unhas — fingerpicks on index finger). Techniques include:
   - **Rasgado** (strumming across courses, flamenco-like)
   - **Arpejo** (rolled chord fingerpicking)
   - **Contracanto** (counter-melody that answers the vocal line's gaps)
   - **Tremolo** (rapid repeated notes)
10. **Lay the viola (Spanish guitar) foundation.** Harmonies in root position, on-beat chord changes, steady rhythmic pulse. May also play a counter-melody or lead in solo passages.

### Phase 4: Fadista Performance Craft (Estilação)

11. **State the melody plainly.** The first iteration is a clear statement of the mother melody.
12. **Estilar (ornament the melody).** On repetition, the fadista:
    - Bends pitch (portamento on key notes)
    - Stretches and compresses syllables rhythmically (tempo rubato, not strict time)
    - Adds appoggiaturas, mordents, passing notes around the fixed melody
    - Varies dynamics dramatically (piano→forte on climactic words)
    - Alternates between chest and head register for dramatic contrast
13. **Signal the song's close.** Every fado ends with a vocal climax — the final verse or chorus line is repeated at higher intensity, and the guitars punctuate with a V–I cadential "full stop" (two-chord exclamation mark).
14. **Desgarrada (optional).** If two fadistas share the stage, they can enter a sung duel — each responds to the previous line with a new poem on the same melody, trying to out-wit the other, with audience participation.

### Phase 5: Fado-Canção Composition (Alternative Path)

For **fado-canção** (composer-created song-fado), steps differ:
1. Select key and chord palette (may extend beyond I–V to include ii–V–I, chromatic mediants, etc.)
2. Compose a verse-and-chorus (estrofe-refrão) structure
3. Write a melody that rises to the chorus and climaxes on the final repeat
4. Fix lyrics permanently to the melody (no free-verse swap)
5. May add orchestration (piano, double bass, string section, rhythms beyond 4/4)
6. Fadista still applies estilação, but within the composed frame

## Practitioner Examples

| Practitioner | Role | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| **Alfredo Marceneiro** (1888–1982) | Fadista & melody creator (tradicional) | Composed Fado Bailado, Fado Cravo, Fado Pajem, Fado Vitória; known as "Rei do Fado" (King of Fado); joiner by trade; never left Lisbon; his melodies became part of the ~200 canonical repertoire |
| **Amália Rodrigues** (1920–1999) | Fadista (castiço & canção) | The global icon; mastered both castiço and canção; made fado an international art form; her interpretation of *Lágrima*, *Gaivota*, *Barco Negro* set the performance standard |
| **Carlos do Carmo** (1945–2021) | Fadista | Son of Lucília do Carmo; major interpreter of fado castiço and canção; recorded *Por Morrer uma Andorinha* (Fado Menor) |
| **Lucília do Carmo** | Fadista | Recorded *Maria Madalena* (Fado Mouraria) |
| **Maria Teresa de Noronha** | Fadista | Recorded *Corrido em Cinco Estilos* (Fado Corrido) |
| **Armandinho** | Guitarrista | Legendary Portuguese guitar player; performed with Marceneiro |
| **António Chainho** | Guitarrista | Modern master of contracanto and guitarrada composition |
| **José Nunes** | Guitarrista | Renowned for variations on Fado Lopes |
| **Manoel Gomes** | Pedagogue | Author of *A Guitarra Portuguesa: Método Fácil* (1929) — the first systematised guitarra method |
| **Mariza** | Fadista (contemporary) | Brought fado to 21st-century global audiences |
| **Ana Moura** | Fadista (contemporary) | Modern interpreter; fado and fado-influenced |
| **Rui Vieira Nery** | Historian | Definitive *A History of Portuguese Fado* (2012); fado scholarship |

## UnitMatrix Integration

### Voices & Sections

| Voice | Instrument | Role | Invariant? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **V0** | Guitarra Portuguesa — Accompaniment Motif | Recurring melodic-harmonic I–V cyclic pattern; the fixed invariant spine of the arrangement; never stops | INVARIANT (motif cycle repeats continuously) |
| **V1** | Guitarra Portuguesa — Contracanto | Counter-melodic line filling the vocalist's gaps; answers V2's phrase endings with guitarra-specific ornamentation | OPTIONAL (present in instrumental guitarrada; in songs, fills rests) |
| **V2** | Fadista (voice) | Melodic lead: states the selected mother melody, then applies estilação on repetitions. Free-verse text chosen per performance, ornaments variable | VARIABLE (pitch ornamented, rhythm rubato, but skeleton melody is recognisable) |
| **V3** | Viola (Spanish guitar) | Harmonic-rhythmic foundation: on-beat chord changes, bass-root reinforcement, steady 4/4 pulse | INVARIANT (continuous harmonic ground) |
| **V4** | Viola Baixo (acoustic bass) | Bass reinforcement of I–V root motion; optional added in contemporary practice | OPTIONAL |
| **V5** | Second Guitarra (optional) | In larger arrangements, second guitarra adds octave doubling, complementary contracanto | OPTIONAL |

### Sections (formal architecture for a fado performance set)

| Section | Content | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **S0 — Opening (Fado-canção)** | First piece in the set, usually a well-known fado-canção | The audience recognises the melody, the room settles; guitarra establishes the motif, fadista states the first poem |
| **S1 — Fado Castiço block** | 2–4 traditional fados (Menor/Corrido/Mouraria) in sequence | Maximum freedom for estilação; the "conversation" between voice and guitarra happens here |
| **S2 — Marcha or Folclore** | Rhythm-brightener; audience claps along | Minor→Major key shift; clapping encouraged; not a fado form but filtered through fado phrasing |
| **S3 — Second Fado Castiço block** | Another set of traditional fados, possibly with desgarrada duel | Emotional intensification towards the close |
| **S4 — Closing Fado-canção** | A lyrical, audience-carryout piece | The set ends the way it began — recognisable melody, high emotional climax, V–I guitar punctuation |

### Rules (encoding the fado craft in UnitMatrix terms)

| Rule | Description |
|---|---|
| **Motif invariance** | V0's accompaniment cycle repeats continuously — break is the song's end. No rests in the guitarra motif |
| **I–V harmonic lock** | The underlying harmony alternates between tonic and dominant for the entire piece; no other chords in castiço (canção may extend) |
| **Free-verse slot** | V2's lyric text is a fill-in-the-form: the performer chooses any poem that fits the melody's syllable count and phrase structure |
| **Estilação filter** | V2's melodic ornament must preserve the skeletal contour of the mother melody. No wholesale recomposition. Ornamentation gradient rises per verse iteration |
| **Vocal climax rule** | Every fado's final stanza/refrain is repeated at higher intensity (louder, higher register, more rubato), followed by V–I cadential punctuation from V0+V3 |
| **Guitarra solo gate (guitarrada)** | In instrumental numbers, V2 is silent; V0+V1+V3+V4 have full freedom to improvise contracanto variations |
| **Desgarrada interlock** | When two fadistas duel, the rule is strict alternation: line from Fadista A → guitarra response → line from Fadista B → guitarra response; no overlap |
| **Call-response dialogue** | Throughout a performance, V0 (guitarra) and V2 (voice) interlock: voice ends a phrase → guitarra fills the gap with a contracanto phrase |
| **No percussion rule (castiço)** | Traditional fado castiço uses NO percussion. Only guitarra and viola. Marcha/folclore pieces may add handclaps |
| **Poem-constrained prosody** | The syllable-count of the chosen poem must match the melody's phrase structure exactly — the fadista enforces this on selection |

## Quirks & Pitfalls

- **Split-author confusion**: The melody IS named (Fado Menor, Fado Corrido), but the piece is referenced by the poem's first line. A beginner might think "Fado Menor" is the song title, rather than the melody template.
- **~200 mother melodies**: Learning the repertoire is the primary task for any aspiring fadista or accompanist. There is no single standardised songbook — the canon is orally transmitted within the fado house community.
- **Estilação vs deviation**: The fadista must ornament without destroying recognisability. Too little ornament = dull; too much = the audience can't identify the fado. The line is social-contextual, not written.
- **Guitarra tuning variance**: Older recordings use "F tuning" (F C D g c d); modern Lisbon tuning is D A B E A D. Accompanists must recognise both. Coimbra fado uses a related but different tuning and technique (rest strokes vs free strokes).
- **Fado is not sad**: Despite the *saudade* stereotype, fado castiço includes joyful forms (Fado Corrido, Fado Bailado). The melancholic reputation comes from fado-canção repertoire and Amália's iconic interpretations.
- **Marcha/folclore blur**: These non-fado forms are sung in fado houses and treated with fado phrasing, but they are separate genres. Classifying them as "fado" is disputed among purists.
