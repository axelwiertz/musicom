## 1. Method Overview

Cuban rumba is a secular music-dance-song complex that emerged in the *solares* (courtyard tenements) of Havana and Matanzas in the late 19th century, created by newly freed Afro-Cubans synthesizing West African (Abakuá, Yoruba, Kongo/Bantu) and Spanish musical traditions. It is **not** a single rhythm but a family of three core styles — **yambú** (slow, oldest, duple-pulse), **guaguancó** (medium-fast, duple-pulse, most popular), and **columbia** (fast, triple-pulse 6/8/12/8, rural/Abakuá-derived).

Unlike HC-018 (Clave-Guided Montuno/Son Cubano), which is song-oriented with *tres* and *bass* as harmonic instruments, rumba is **drum-centric**: three congas (tumba/salidor, segundo/tres dos, quinto) form the core instrumental body, with claves, catá/guagua (bamboo log drum), and shakers as auxiliary. The vocals are call-and-response between a lead singer and chorus. The dance is inseparable — the quinto drummer directly dialogues with the dancer's movements, and in columbia the quinto phrases mirror the dancer's footwork beat for beat.

**Key principle**: everything is learned orally. No notation, no published method books — the knowledge passes from elder rumberos to younger ones through years of immersion in the *solares*. The craft is conversational, community-based, and fundamentally about creating a shared social space.

## 2. The Three Styles

### Yambú ("yambú de tiempo España")

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| Tempo | Slowest (~60–80 BPM) |
| Meter | Duple-pulse primary (2/4, 4/4), triple-pulse secondary |
| Dance | Couple or solo; feigned frailty (old-age movements); **no vacunao** |
| Quinto | Tone-slap-tone pattern alternating per clave cycle |
| Mood | Gentle, nostalgic, "old Spain" era |
| Origins | Earliest rumba style, colonial period |

### Guaguancó

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| Tempo | Medium (~90–120 BPM) |
| Meter | Duple-pulse primary |
| Dance | Couple; male attempts vacunao (pelvic thrust "vaccination"), female deflects (botao) |
| Quinto | Accents the vacunao moment; uses Matanzas-style lock vocabulary |
| Mood | Playful, flirtatious, competitive |
| Origins | Developed from *coros de guaguancó* (narrative song choirs) ~1900 |

### Columbia

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| Tempo | Fast (~130–180 BPM) |
| Meter | Triple-pulse primary (6/8, 12/8) |
| Dance | Solo male; acrobatic, martial-arts-inflected (juego de maíz), competitive |
| Quinto | Direct dialogue with dancer — phrases mirror footwork; switch phrases on demand |
| Mood | Energetic, virtuosic, masculine display |
| Origins | Rural sugar plantations/docks; Abakuá-derived drum patterns and chants |

## 3. Craft Procedure (How the Human Composer/Performer Works)

### Step 1: Fix the clave

Choose **rumba clave orientation** (3–2 or 2–3). The lead claves player establishes the tempo and feel. The clave is the **invariant spine** — it does not change for the entire rumba. All other parts reference it continuously. Players who violate the clave (play *cruzado*, or "crossed") are corrected on the spot.

### Step 2: Choose style & tempo

The lead singer or oldest rumbero declares the style (yambú, guaguancó, or columbia) either by verbal signal, by the opening drum entry sequence, or by the diana pitch/tempo. This dictates:
- Clave speed and orientation
- Drum part assignment (the tumba/salidor ostinato differs per style)
- Guagua pattern (duple cáscara vs. triple-pulse Abakuá erikundi pattern)
- Dance vocabulary

### Step 3: Assign drum roles (the layer mask)

Three conga drums + auxiliaries enter one at a time:

1. **Claves** start (the 5-stroke guide pattern)
2. **Catá/Guagua** joins (continuous cáscara pattern)
3. **Tumba/Salidor** (lowest conga) plays the fixed marcha ostinato — the downbeat-heavy open-tone foundation. In columbia, this is a six-cross-beat embellished pattern.
4. **Segundo/Tres dos** (middle conga) enters, playing the "tres golpe" (three-stroke) interlocking bridge pattern that fills the gaps in the tumba's open tone pattern.
5. **Quinto** (highest conga) enters last — the lead improvisatory voice. The quinto player does NOT repeat a fixed pattern; rather, they use a known vocabulary of tone-slap-tone "lock" phrases, alternating per clave cycle, and improvising new phrases that respond to the dancer and signal section changes.

**Key constraint**: the three conga drums' open tones must NOT coincide in the same subdivision — at most one open tone per tick across V1/V2/V3. Slaps may coincide.

### Step 4: The vocal architecture

The lead singer (who may also play claves) builds the song in three phases:

**Diana** (unmetered): The singer intones a short phrase with meaningless syllables (the "lloraíto" or cry). This establishes:
- The pitch center / mode
- The melodic kernel that the chorus will later sing as their refrain
- The emotional tone of the rumba
The diana is the single most identity-defining moment — Larry Crook notes it "also contains the first choral refrain."

**Canto** (narrative, over clave): The singer improvises *décimas* — ten-line stanzas of octosyllabic verse with ABBAACCDDC rhyme — stating the reason for the gathering, praising those present, or telling a story. The chorus is silent during the canto, only interjecting occasional "eh!" encouragement. This section can last many minutes (5–30+ cycles). The singer stretches time freely while the percussion stays locked to the clave cycle.

**Montuno** (call-response climax): The lead singer signals the transition — often with a rising melodic phrase or the shout "ya llegó el montuno!" — and the chorus enters with a short **fixed** refrain. The call-response exchange then escalates:
- Each cycle: lead improvises a new line over the frozen chorus pattern
- Quinto intensifies (more slaps, higher register, faster phrases)
- Vocal intensity rises
- Drum volume and density increase
- Tempo subtly accelerates
- The dancer's vacunao attempts peak

### Step 5: Dance-drummer dialogue

The dancer is not a passive element — they directly shape the drumming:

- **Guaguancó**: The female dancer's skirt-opening/closing tempo (the "botao") and hip movements cue the overall feel. The quinto must place a strong accent on the moment of the vacunao attempt, usually as the resolution of a phrase spanning >1 clave cycle.
- **Columbia**: The quinto player and dancer engage in real-time call-and-response. The pattern of quinto strokes and the dancer's steps are "at times identical, and at other times, imaginatively matched" (Peñalosa 2011). The quinto player must switch phrase vocabulary instantly when the dancer changes footwork. This is the tightest dancer-drummer coupling of any rumba style.
- **Yambú**: Gentler — quinto responds to the dancer's feigned frailty with softer, sparse phrases. No vacunao means no peak accent.

### Step 6: Performance arc (tension-build-release)

| Phase | Duration | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Drum Entry | 4–8 cycles | Clave → guagua → tumba → segundo → quinto: sparse accumulation |
| Diana | 2–4 cycles | Unmetered vocal warm-up, establishes pitch/melodic kernel |
| Canto | 8–32+ cycles | Décima narrative, chorus silent, quinto supporting fills |
| Montuno Entry | 1–2 cycles | Lead signals, chorus enters fixed refrain |
| Montuno Build | N cycles | Call-response escalation: each cycle louder/faster/denser |
| Climax | 4–8 cycles | Max density, quinto virtuosity peak, dance vacunao/acrobatics peak |
| Coda | 4–8 cycles | Reverse taper: drums drop out in reverse order, clave final stroke |

Total duration: 10–45 minutes (street), 3–8 minutes (staged).

### Step 7: Close

The lead singer signals winding down (typically a descending phrase or "ya, ya, ya"). Drums drop out in reverse order (quinto → segundo → tumba → guagua → clave). The clave may continue alone and finish with a final, sharp stroke.

## 5. UnitMatrix Integration

### Voices (8 total)

| Voice | Instrument | Role | Constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| V0 | Claves | **Invariant spine** | 5-stroke rumba clave (3–2 or 2–3), fixed for duration. Tick positions: 3-side {0,4,6,10,12}, 2-side {0,2,6,8,12} in 16th subdivision of 2/4. NEVER changes. |
| V1 | Tumba/Salidor | Low conga ostinato | Fixed pattern per style. Yambú/guaguancó: downbeat-heavy marcha (open on 1&3, slap on 2&4). Columbia: six-cross-beat embellishment. |
| V2 | Segundo/Tres dos | Middle bridge conga | Tres-golpe interlock. Fills open-tone gaps in V1. Max 1 open tone per subdivision across V1+V2. |
| V3 | Quinto | Lead conga — improvised | Vocabulary constraint: tone-slap-tone lock per clave cycle. Alternates between two lock orientations per clave cycle. Vacunao accent event >1 cycle. Dancer dialogue (columbia: mirrors footwork). |
| V4 | Catá/Guagua | Log drum cáscara | Fixed pattern. Duple: continuous 16th/8th pattern. Columbia: Abakuá erikundi (one hand clave, other hand four main beats). |
| V5 | Lead Vocal | Diana + Canto | Unmetered diana (1–2 cycle), then improvised décima over clave grid. Singer signals S3 transition. |
| V6 | Chorus | Montuno refrain | Fixed short phrase, entered only on S3 transition. Repeats cyclically. Overlapping: lead may start next call before chorus finishes response (overlap ≤1 beat). |
| V7 | Chekeré/Maracas | Subdivisional shaker fill | Continuous pulse at the subdivision level (16th notes in duple, 8th triplets in columbia). No rests. |

### Sections (7 stages)

| Section | Cycles | Description | Rules |
|---|---|---|---|
| S0 Drum Entry | 4–8 | Clave → guagua → tumba → segundo → quinto sparse accumulation | Enter order invariant, monotonic density increase within section |
| S1 Diana | 2–4 | Lead unmetered diana — pitch/melody kernel | Unmetered, no percussion volume increase |
| S2 Canto | 8–N (variable) | Lead improvised décima verse, chorus silent | Chorus V6 tacet, V5 must continue; quinto V3 supporting fills only |
| S3 Montuno Entry | 1–2 | Lead signals → chorus enters with fixed refrain | Signal mandatory; V6 enters frozen pattern |
| S4 Montuno Build | N (variable) | Call-response escalation — each cycle louder/denser/faster | V5 improvises new line per cycle over frozen V6; V3 density + register ramp |
| S5 Climax | 4–8 | Max volume, max quinto virtuosity, dance peak (vacunao/acrobatics) | Global max density; tempo at peak |
| S6 Coda | 4–8 | Reverse taper — drums drop out in order, clave last | Drop order: V3→V2→V1→V4→V0; final V0 stroke ends |

### Core Rules

1. **Clave invariance** (V0 fixed for entire rumba duration — no re-orientation mid-piece)
2. **Drum interlock** (at most one open tone per subdivision across V1+V2+V3 slaps may coincide)
3. **Quinto vocabulary mask** (phrases must draw from tone-slap-tone lock grammar — free within grammar)
4. **Vacunao accent** (guaguancó only: V3 must place accent on vacunao tick, typically phrase resolution spanning >1 clave cycle)
5. **Canto/montuno phase gate** (V6 cannot enter until lead signals S3 transition)
6. **Monotonic density progression** (each section S0→S5 is denser/louder/faster than the previous; S6 is release)
7. **Open-ended form** (S2 and S4 cycles count N is not predetermined — determined by participants)
8. **Dance-choreography coupling** (in columbia: V3 mirrors dancer footwork in real-time; in guaguancó: botao/vacunao rhythm cues accent timing)
9. **Montuno refrain freeze** (once V6 pattern enters, it repeats without variation — only V5 changes)

## 8. Quirks & Pitfalls

1. **Not son cubano/montuno**: HC-018 (Clave-Guided Montuno / Cuban Son) is a related but distinct tradition. Son uses *tres*, guitar, bass, and *bongós* as harmonic instruments with a fixed song form; rumba is drum-centric, with no harmonic instruments and an open-ended form that can last 45 minutes. Practitioners insist they are different.

2. **No notation tradition**: Unlike Western classical, rumba is 100% oral. Transcribing it to MIDI or notation is an approximation — the feel of the quinto lock, the timing of the vacunao accent, and the subtle clave orientation shifts cannot be notated precisely. Any UnitMatrix implementation will be a skeleton.

3. **Guarapachangueo complexity**: The *guarapachangueo* style (HC-048 sub-variant developed by Los Chinitos, 1970s) deliberately fragments the drum interlock into conversational "beats and rests" that sound unstructured to the untrained ear. This is impossible to encode as strict rules — it is an aesthetic, not a fixed pattern.

4. **Dance-drummer coupling is real-time**: In traditional columbia, the quinto player changes phrases based on what the dancer does *in the moment*. This feedback loop cannot be fully encoded in a pre-composition system like UnitMatrix unless we add real-time sensor input.

5. **Post-revolution institutionalization problem**: After 1959, staged/tourist rumba condensed the form to 3–8 minutes and dropped the open-ended canto/montuno flexibility. Many rumberos consider the "theater patio" version a different art form. Our HC-048 description describes the traditional *callejero* (street) form, not the staged one.

6. **Tempo drift in traditional performance**: Unlike Western metronomic pulse, traditional rumba tempo subtly accelerates through the montuno build and then releases in the coda. The UnitMatrix would need to model this as a tempo ramp across sections rather than a fixed BPM.

7. **Multiple quinto traditions**: The quinto vocabulary differs between the Havana style and the Matanzas style. Havana quinto is more linear and melodic; Matanzas emphasizes the tone-slap-tone lock alternation per clave cycle. Our description follows the Matanzas style (the Peñalosa analysis).
