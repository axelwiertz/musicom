# HC-005: West African Ewe Drumming — Cross-Rhythmic Architecture

## Tradition / Culture
West African — Ewe people of Ghana/Togo/Benin. Ancestral drumming tradition spanning centuries, preserved through oral transmission and apprenticeship. Closely tied to dance, ceremony, and social function (funerals, festivals, warrior rites).

## Description

Ewe drumming is a **layered polyrhythmic ensemble practice** where multiple percussion voices interlock around a single referential bell pattern. Unlike Western polyphony (where independent melodic lines create harmony), Ewe drumming creates a **cross-rhythmic texture** where each voice articulates a different rhythmic subdivision of a common pulse, producing an emergent composite pattern no single player performs alone.

The fundamental organizing principle is the **timeline** (the bell or gankogui pattern) — a cyclical asymmetrical pattern that serves as the rhythmic "ground zero" against which all other parts orient. All other instruments relate to this timeline through specific cross-rhythmic ratios (typically 3:2, 4:3, 6:4) and through **call-and-response** dialogue between the lead/master drum and the support drums.

The system is deeply pedagogical: drummers learn through apprenticeship, internalizing each part by singing syllabic mnemonics (vocalized drum language) before touching an instrument. The master drummer improvises within the framework, signaling shifts in dance, tempo, or section through specific "calls" on the lead drum.

## Musical Elements Framework

### PITCH
Ewe drumming is predominantly **non-pitched** in the melodic sense. Pitch enters through:
- **Drum tension tuning**: atsimevu and sogo are tuned to specific relative pitches (low/mid/high strokes: BASS, OPEN TONE, SLAP).
- **Timbral differentiation** substitutes for pitch — the three stroke types (bass, open, slap) function like a 3-level pitch palette.
- **Gankogui bell** has two pitches (high/low iron bell pair).

### RHYTHM
The primary element. Key concepts:
- **Timeline (bell pattern)**: Asymmetrical 12-pulse cycle (typically `[2-1-2-2-1-2]` or `[2-1-2-1-2-2]` grouping of 12 eighth-note slots). This is the invariant referential pattern.
- **Cross-rhythm**: Support drums articulate 3-against-2 (e.g., kidi plays a pattern emphasizing the 3-side while the bell marks the 2-side), creating persistent polyrhythmic tension.
- **Responsorial rhythm**: Master drum calls answered by ensemble responses, often overlapping.
- **Polyrhythmic density**: Each voice fills a different density stratum (bell = sparse anchors, shaker = continuous 8ths, support drums = mid-density, master drum = high-density improvisation).

### HARMONY
No harmonic system in the Western sense. The "vertical" dimension is **timbral-harmonic**: at any given pulse, the combination of simultaneous strokes across drums creates a timbral chord (e.g., bass+slap+bell = a specific "harmonic" moment). These vertical combinations are meaningful — certain simultaneous stroke combinations mark critical dance positions.

### STRUCTURE
- **Cyclic form**: Built on repeated timeline cycles (typically 12-pulse, sometimes 16-pulse).
- **Sectional progression**: Performance moves through phases: opening (bell+solo support), main section (full ensemble, dance), call-and-response dialogues, climactic breaks (master drum calls → ensemble responds with break pattern), and closing.
- **Master drum signals**: Lead drummer uses specific rhythmic "calls" (e.g., `GA-GA-GI-GI-GA`) to trigger section changes, tempo shifts, or dance transitions.
- **Nested phrase structure**: Short patterns (2-4 pulses) combine into longer phrases (12 pulses) which nest into sectional blocks (many cycles).

### TEXTURE
**Stratified polyrhythmic interlock**:
- **Layer 1 (Ground)**: Gankogui bell — invariant timeline, 1 cycle per 12 pulses.
- **Layer 2 (Support)**: Kidi and Sogo drums — repeating mid-density cross-rhythmic patterns.
- **Layer 3 (Elaboration)**: Axatse shaker — continuous subdivision (eighth notes), filling textural gaps.
- **Layer 4 (Lead)**: Atsimevu master drum — improvised calls, high-density variations, responsorial phrases.

## Craft Process — How a Human Does It

### Step 1: Learn the Timeline (Bell Pattern)
The drummer first internalizes the gankogui pattern by singing it as a syllabic mnemonic (e.g., "KI-TA-KI-KI-TA-KI-TA-KI-KI-TA-KI"). The timeline is the **anchor** — it never changes during performance. It is the "key" that all other parts lock into.

### Step 2: Internalize Each Support Part Via Vocal Mnemonics
Each drum part has a verbal mnemonic (vocal percussion). The student sings each part separately against the bell timeline until the cross-rhythmic relationship is felt in the body, not counted intellectually. For example, kidi's pattern might be sung as "GO-DO-GO-DO-GO-DO" against the bell's "KI-TA-KI-KI-TA-KI..." — the student learns to feel where kidi's strokes fall relative to the bell.

### Step 3: Add Parts Sequentially
In ensemble rehearsal, parts are added one at a time:
1. Bell alone (establish ground).
2. Add shaker (continuous pulse).
3. Add first support drum (kidi).
4. Add second support drum (sogo).
5. Master drum enters last.

Each new part must lock precisely with the timeline. If a player drifts, the master drummer isolates them and has them play alone with the bell until re-locked.

### Step 4: Master Drum Improvisation & Call-and-Response
The lead drummer (atsimevu) improvises within the framework, creating rhythmic variations that reference the timeline but elaborate on it. The master uses stock "calls" — recognizable rhythmic phrases that signal the ensemble to respond with a specific break pattern or transition to a new section. The master drummer also responds to dancers, matching rhythmic intensity to the dancer's movements.

### Step 5: Build to Climax Through Density and Register
The performance intensifies over time. The master drum increases density (more strokes per cycle), moves into higher register (slaps instead of bass tones), and the ensemble plays louder. Section transitions are triggered by a master drum call followed by an ensemble break — a brief unison or synchronized pattern that marks the boundary.

### Step 6: Closure
The master drum signals closure with a specific terminal call. All voices converge on a final synchronized pattern, typically landing on the first pulse of the timeline cycle.

## Practitioner Examples

- **Mustapha Tettey Addy** (1942–2021): Master Ewe drummer, preserved and taught traditional repertoire globally. His performances of *Atsiagbekor* (warrior dance) demonstrate the full call-and-response architecture.
- **C.K. Ladzekpo** (b. 1935): Ewe master drummer and scholar at UC Berkeley. His pedagogical work systematized Ewe drumming theory for Western students, emphasizing cross-rhythm as the fundamental organizing principle.
- **Gideon Alorwoyie** (1947–2024): Master drummer from Ghana, former lead drummer of the National Dance Company. Known for *Agbadza* and *Atsiagbekor* repertoire.
- **Hewale Sounds** (Ghana): Contemporary ensemble led by Dela Botri, practicing traditional Ewe repertoire with modern instrumentation.
- **Dagomba Drumming** (Northern Ghana): Related tradition using lunna (talking drum) — demonstrates the call-and-response principle in a different cultural context.

### Section Assignment (Columns)
| Section | Phase | Description |
|---------|-------|-------------|
| A | Opening | Bell alone → add shaker → add support drums one by one |
| B | Main dance | Full ensemble, steady groove, master drum improvising at medium density |
| C | Call-and-response dialogue | Master drum calls → ensemble responds with break patterns |
| D | Climax | Maximum density, master drum in high register (slaps), all voices at peak velocity |
| E | Closure | Master drum terminal call → ensemble converges on final unison on pulse 1 |

### Rules Encoding the Method
1. **Timeline invariance**: Voice 0 (bell) pattern is constant across all sections. Never modified.
2. **Cross-rhythm ratio**: Voices 1–2 must maintain 3:2 or 4:3 relationship to the timeline pulse grid.
3. **Call detection**: When Voice 4 (master) outputs a call phrase (high-density burst pattern), Voices 0–3 must respond with a designated break pattern in the next cycle.
4. **Density progression**: Overall stroke density increases monotonically from Section A → Section D, then drops at Section E.
5. **Timbral register mapping**: Master drum pitch class shifts from bass (low) → open tone (mid) → slap (high) as sections progress A → D.
6. **Convergence rule**: At section boundaries and closure, all voices synchronize to a shared downbeat (pulse 1 of the timeline cycle).
7. **Timeline cycle alignment**: All rhythmic events must align to the 12-pulse cycle grid. Section boundaries fall on cycle completions.
8. **Shaker continuity**: Voice 3 (axatse) plays on every pulse — never rests, never varies in density (only velocity varies for dynamics).
9. **Mnemonic encoding**: Each voice's pattern should be expressible as a singable syllabic mnemonic, ensuring human-playability.

### Musical Elements → UnitMatrix Cell Content
Each cell `U[v,s]` contains:
- `{RHYTHM}`: Stroke trigger pattern aligned to the 12-pulse cycle grid (onset positions + stroke type: BASS/OPEN/SLAP).
- `{TEXTURE}`: Density coefficient (strokes per cycle relative to the 12-pulse maximum).
- `{PITCH}`: Discrete stroke pitch class (BASS=low, OPEN=mid, SLAP=high) — 3-level palette.
- `{STRUCTURE}`: Section role tag (GROUND, SUPPORT, ELABORATION, LEAD, CALL, RESPONSE, BREAK).

## Why It Matters for Musicom

This method demonstrates a fundamentally different organizing principle from Western harmony-based composition:
- **Timeline-anchored structure** instead of harmonic progression — the UnitMatrix's structural column is governed by a cyclic rhythmic reference, not chord changes.
- **Cross-rhythmic interlock** as the primary textural engine — voices don't harmonize melodically, they create emergent composite rhythms through layered counterpoint.
- **Call-and-response** as a structural driver — the lead voice actively triggers section transitions, making the UnitMatrix dynamic rather than static.
- **Timbral pitch substitution** — a 3-level stroke palette (bass/open/slap) functions as a pitch surrogate, demonstrating that the PITCH dimension can be timbral rather than tonal.
- **Oral transmission model** — the mnemonic encoding rule ensures that generated patterns remain human-playable and singable, bridging algorithmic output and human practice.

This complements HC-002 (Gamelan Kotekan) — both use interlocking textures, but Ewe drumming emphasizes **cross-rhythmic tension** (3:2 ratios against a timeline) rather than **pitch-based elaboration** of a core melody. Together they show two distinct approaches to emergent composite texture in the UnitMatrix.
