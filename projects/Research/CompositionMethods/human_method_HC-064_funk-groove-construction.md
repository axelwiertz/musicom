# HC-064: Funk Groove Construction — The One / Chicken Scratch / Horn-Stab Interlock

**Tradition:** African-American Funk (USA, ~1965–present) — James Brown & The J.B.'s, Parliament-Funkadelic, The Meters, Sly & The Family Stone, Earth Wind & Fire, Tower of Power

**Layer:** human (→ concrete)

---

## Description

Funk groove construction is a **rhythm-section layering craft** where every instrument treats itself as a percussive component in a locked interlocking composite. The method is built on three non-negotiable pillars:

1. **The One** — the downbeat (beat 1 of every bar) is the heaviest, most emphasized event. All instruments converge on it. Everything else is ornament around that spine.
2. **Chicken-scratch rhythm guitar** — muted, percussive, syncopated chordal stabs played near the bridge with a light touch and rapid pick-hand strums; no ring, no sustain.
3. **Call-and-response horn stabs** — horn section (trumpets, saxophones, trombone) punches short rhythmic figures that answer the vocal or guitar call, never overlapping the lead, always locking into the same downbeat grid.

The result is a **composite pocket** — no single voice carries the melody in a traditional sense; the groove *is* the composition.

---

## Craft Procedure — How a Human Funk Composer/Arranger Actually Does It

### Step 1: Fix tempo, key, and harmonic cell

Choose a tempo between 90–115 bpm (classic funk) or 105–125 (faster funk). Pick a key (often B♭, C, E♭, F, or G — horn-friendly keys). The **harmonic loop** is typically a single chord (vamp) or a 2-chord oscillation (I–IV, I–♭VII, i–♭VII). There is rarely a chord progression longer than 4 bars. The harmony is **static or near-static** — changes happen slowly so the rhythm takes front seat.

### Step 2: Write the bass line (the pocket anchor)

The bass is the most important composed element — it defines the funk identity of the tune. The bassist plays:

- **Root on The One** (beat 1, strongest articulation)
- **Syncopated ghost notes, 16th-note rests, slides, muted pops** on beats 2–4
- Octave jumps, chromatic approaches to the root, and rhythmic anticipation (landing on the note just before beat 1)
- A **repeating 1- or 2-bar pattern** that is the tune's fingerprint

Bootsy Collins's formula: *"Play the root note on the first beat of every bar. Then feel the spaces between — everything else is what you do in those spaces."*

### Step 3: Lock the drum pattern

The drum pattern in funk is **sparse on the high frequencies, heavy on the backbeat and snare**. Classic Clyde Stubblefield / Jabo Starks approach:

- Kick drum: plays **syncopated patterns that interlock with the bass**, not four-on-the-floor. Often the kick hits on The One and then again on the "& of 2" or "3" in a call-and-response with the snare.
- Snare drum: **backbeat on 2 and 4** — crisp, loud, and dry.
- Hi-hat: **16th-note subdivision** with a relaxed swing (slight 16th-note triplet feel) — the "shimmer" layer.
- Ride cymbal: rarely used; the hi-hat is the primary timekeeper.
- Ghost notes on snare: soft, buzz-like strokes between backbeats that add texture.

### Step 4: Write the chicken-scratch guitar part

The rhythm guitar (e.g. Jimmy Nolen, Catfish Collins, Leo Nocentelli) plays:

- **Muted strums** (palm or fret-hand damped) — chord is heard as a percussive stab with pitch contour but no sustain.
- **Rapid pick-hand motion** — 16th-note subdivisions with **off-beat accents**. The chicken scratch is a pattern of quick strums where some strokes are muted and some ring briefly.
- **Single-note syncopated figures** — short melodic riffs that interlock with the bass, not full chords.
- **Position near the bridge** — bright, thin, trebly tone (often through a wah pedal cocked at a fixed position or active wah filter).
- The guitar **never plays sustained chords** or held notes — it's a percussion instrument.

### Step 5: Compose the horn stabs (call-and-response)

Horns (trumpet, tenor/alto sax, trombone) function as a **rhythmic punctuation section**, not a melodic solo voice:

- **Short stabs** — 1–3 notes, staccato, tutti, on a chosen subdivision (often beat 1, beat 3, or the "& of 2").
- **Call and response** — vocal shouts a phrase, horns answer with a figure; or guitar plays a riff, horns answer. The response never overlaps the call.
- **Harmonic stabs** — horns play block chords (3–5 voices, close voicing) or unison lines. No independent horn melody — they are arranged as a single rhythmic voice.
- **Space is as important as sound** — rests between stabs are composed and timed.

### Step 6: Write the vocal/shout part

The lead vocals (James Brown style) are:

- **Rhythmic shouts and grunts** rather than melodic singing — the voice is a percussion instrument.
- **Call phrases** that are short (1–4 beats) with a clear syncopated rhythm.
- **Gospel-derived ad-libs** — "Hit me!", "Take it to the bridge!", "Get up!" — that cue arrangement changes.
- **Melody is secondary to rhythm** in the classic funk vocal approach (later funk/soul singers like Stevie Wonder/Earth Wind & Fire brought more melody).

### Step 7: Add keyboard/organ pad (optional)

Hammond organ or electric piano plays:

- **Short chordal punches** (synced with or complementary to the horn stabs)
- **Sparse fills** — organ solo in the bridge, then returns to rhythmic comping
- Often **panned in the spaces** left by the guitar — listen to James Brown's organ on "Funky Drummer"

### Step 8: Arrange macro-form as a groove arc

The funk arrangement is not sectional AABA — it's a **single groove with dynamic states**:

| State | Description | Typical Duration |
|-------|-------------|------------------|
| Intro | Enter parts one by one (drums → bass → guitar → horns → vocal) | 4–8 bars |
| Main Groove | All parts locked, vocal call-and-response active | 16–32 bars |
| Breakdown | Thin texture (bass + drums only, or percussion feature) | 4–8 bars |
| Bridge/Change | Instrumental solo over same groove (organ, sax, guitar solo) | 16–32 bars |
| Climax | Max density: all horns, full shout chorus, tambourine/shaker, overlapping calls | 8–16 bars |
| Outro | Fade or tagged repeat of main riff with sudden stop | 4–8 bars |

There is no "chorus" in the pop sense — the entire track is the chorus. Variation comes from **dynamic shifts, voice entry/exit masks, and the intensity of the soloist**.

### Step 9: Rehearse to lock the pocket

James Brown famously fined musicians who played before or after The One. The rehearsal process:

- **Fixed arrangement by ear** — no written charts (in the classic era). Brown called out changes. Horns memorized stab positions.
- **The One is absolute** — all players internalize beat 1 to sub-millisecond precision.
- **Intensity is cued** — Brown's grunts signaled "more" or "break".
- **The groove must be danceable** — if dancers stopped, the groove was wrong.

## UnitMatrix Integration

### Voices & Sections Mapping

| Voice | Instrument Role | Layer Description | Section Behavior |
|-------|----------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| V0 | **Bass Spine** | Root on The One + syncopated ghost notes in the space. 1- or 2-bar repeating cell = piece identity anchor. Invariant across most sections. | Always on. Mutes only in breakdown state. |
| V1 | **Drum Grid** | Kick-snare pattern: kick syncopated (interlocks with V0), snare crisp on 2&4, hi-hat 16th-note shimmer. Locked groove cell. | Invariant. Snare rolls added in buildup/climax. |
| V2 | **Chicken-Scratch Guitar** | Muted staccato chord stabs on off-beat 16th-note grid. Short single-note riffs in spaces between bass notes. Bright, thin tone. | Always on. May drop to sparser texture in breakdown. |
| V3 | **Horn Stabs** | 3–5 voice block-chord punctuation (trumpet, tenor, trombone). Played tutti on chosen subdivisions. Call-and-response with V4 (vocal). | Active in main groove and climax. Tacet in breakdown and sparse verse sections. |
| V4 | **Vocal Shout / Lead** | Rhythmic call phrases, short syncopated shouts. Voice as percussion — melody secondary. Cues arrangement changes. | Solo in verse-like sections. Call-and-response with V3. Ad-lib fills in climax. |
| V5 | **Keyboard/Organ Pad** | Short chordal punches synced with or complementary to V3. Sparse organ fills. | Optional layer. Active in main groove, solo during bridge. |
| V6 | **Percussion Shimmer** | Tambourine, shaker, cowbell — continuous 8th/16th-note pulse. Adds intensity in climax. | Enters in main groove, loudest in climax. Tacet in breakdown. |

### Sections

| Section | Bars | Voices Active | Description |
|---------|------|---------------|-------------|
| S0 Intro | 4–8 | V1→V0→V2→V4 | Enter one voice per 2 bars; establish groove |
| S1 Main Groove | 16–32 | All (V3 may enter later) | Locked pocket; call-and-response active |
| S2 Breakdown | 4–8 | V0+V1+V2 sparse | Thin texture, bass/drums/guitar only; tension |
| S3 Bridge/Solo | 16–32 | V0+V1+V2+V5+V6 | Instrumental solo over same groove; organ or guitar |
| S4 Climax | 8–16 | Full tutti + V3 horns max | Max density, overlapping calls, tambourine |
| S5 Outro | 4–8 | V0+V1+V2 fade/tag | Tagged riff repeat, fade or hard stop |

### Rules

1. **The-One invariance** — every bar, all voices must place an event (or articulate an accent) on beat 1, subdivision 0. Voice 0 (bass) must play the root on beat 1. Zero-drift enforcement.
2. **Call-response interlock mask** — V4 (vocal) call and V3 (horn) answer must never overlap. The response occupies the space after the call ends, with ≥ 1 beat gap if both are in the same section.
3. **Chicken-scratch muting** — V2 (guitar) events always have velocity < 100, duration < 60 ticks (staccato), and pitch = chord root + 3rd + 5th muted (palm/fret damped emulated). No sustained notes.
4. **Horn block-chord rule** — V3 events are ≥3 simultaneous pitches (trumpet+alto+trombone range), short duration, played on the same onset. No solo horn lines.
5. **Bass ghost-note vocabulary** — between root articulations, V0 may add muted ghost notes (velocity < 40) on 16th-note subdivisions, never exceeding V0's bass register.
6. **Static harmony** — chord changes occur at most every 4 bars. Preferred loops: I–I (vamp), I–IV, I–♭VII, or i–♭VII.
7. **Density progression** — voices are added monotonically from S0 intro to S4 climax: no voice drops until S2 breakdown (selective mute) or S5 outro (fade).
8. **Section uniformity** — all voices' section cells must end exactly at the section's terminal landmark tick (no drift). Sections repeat identical cell content if same state recurs.

## Quirks & Pitfalls

- **The One is NOT the same as a strong downbeat in Western classical music.** In funk, The One is played slightly *ahead* of the beat in the feel (a controlled anticipation). MIDI-based implementations must account for this micro-timing (e.g. −5 to −15 ticks offset on beat 1 events).
- **The groove is the composition.** There is no melody that can be extracted and sung; the composite of all rhythmic interlocking parts *is* the tune. Attempting to reduce funk to a lead sheet loses its essence.
- **Static harmony does not mean static arrangement.** The interest comes from voice entry/exit masks, dynamic accents, and the soloist's rhythmic variation. A 6-minute funk track may have only 2 chords.
- **Drum kick and bass must interlock, not double.** If the kick hits every beat-1 simultaneously with the bass root, the pocket feels plodding. The kick should play a complementary syncopated pattern that intertwines with the bass without unison.
- **No written charts in the classic tradition.** James Brown's method was oral — he sang or grunted parts to musicians until they internalized them. Notation for funk should be sparse: a rhythmic grid with chord symbols.
