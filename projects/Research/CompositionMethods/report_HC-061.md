## Summary

Cantu in paghjella is a traditional Corsican a cappella polyphonic singing practice combining three male vocal registers that always enter a song in the same fixed order: **a secunda** (leading voice, medium register, carries the main melody), **u bassu** (lowest voice, harmonic foundation with sustained tones), and **a terza** (highest voice, ornamentation and melismas). The practice is both secular and liturgical, performed in churches, village squares, bars, processions, and social gatherings. Transmission is entirely oral by ear — no notation. The hallmark features include staggered entry, just-intonation tuning creating a "fourth sound" (emergent overtone), Phrygian/Dorian modal contours, tierce de Picardie final cadences, flexible rubato rhythm, and voice-leading where the bassu descends from mid-range to its lowest tone across each phrase.

## Craft Procedure (Detailed Human Workflow)

### Phase 1: Oral Transmission (Apprenticeship)
1. The apprentice singer **absorbs the repertoire by ear** through constant exposure in the community — church masses, confraternity rehearsals (settimane sante), village fairs (feste paesane), shepherds' vigils (veglie), and family gatherings.
2. No written notation is used. Master singers correct intonation, vowel colour, entry timing, and phrase shape **by ear in real time**, analogous to a sports coach correcting form.
3. The singer internalises the complete melodic and textual repertoire of their village's confraternity or singing group — a body of several dozen pieces, both sacred and secular.

### Phase 2: Trio Assembly
1. **Three male singers** form the core unit (historically all-male, women's participation is a recent and debated evolution).
2. **A secunda** — the most experienced singer with the largest repertoire. Medium vocal register. This singer:
   - Chooses the piece and sets the pitch
   - Sings the complete composed melody (the "skeleton" of the piece)
   - Determines tempo, phrasing, and overall interpretation
   - Is the "keeper of the melody"
3. **U bassu** — the singer with the deepest, steadiest voice. Physically demanding role requiring consistent breath support for long sustained notes. This singer:
   - Provides the harmonic foundation
   - Usually starts each phrase at a mid-range pitch and descends to the lowest note by the cadence
   - Sings longer, more stable notes over which the other two embellish
4. **A terza** — the singer with the highest, most flexible voice. Requires a particularly wide-ranged and agile instrument. This singer:
   - Rises above the secunda
   - Weaves in ornamentation, vocalisations, and virtuosic passages
   - Adds the "sparkle" — often the voice most impressive to listeners

### Phase 3: Composition / Selection
1. The secunda selects a **text** — traditionally in Corsican, occasionally Sardinian, Latin, or Greek. Common poetic forms:
   - **Octosyllables** (8 syllables per line) for secular songs
   - **Decasyllables** (10 syllables) for sacred or narrative songs
   - **Strophic** (same melody for each verse couplet)
2. The secunda selects (or composes, if a new piece) a **modal melody** from the traditional stock:
   - Predominantly **Phrygian** (with flattened 2nd degree) or **Dorian/Aeolian** modes
   - Characteristic Phrygian cadence: II♭ → I (e.g., C major on D Phrygian with F♮→E)
   - Melodic contour is conjunct, with occasional leaps of a 4th/5th at phrase boundaries
3. The secunda fixes the **tonal centre** by singing the opening note alone (the "intonazione").
4. **Key feature**: The song may be in a minor/Phrygian mode throughout, but it must **end on a major chord** (tierce de Picardie). This is a hard rule — no piece ends in a minor chord.

### Phase 4: Staggered Entry — The Fixed Invariant
The entry order is inviolable and repeats at each structural section:

1. **Secunda begins alone** (S0) — 1–2 phrases of solo melody. Establishes pitch centre, mode, tempo.
2. **Bassu enters** (S1) — after the secunda's opening gesture (typically 2–4 seconds), the bassu joins on a low sustained note. This note is typically the tonic or the 5th of the mode, creating the first harmonic pillar.
3. **Terza enters last** (S2) — after the bassu has stabilised (often at the next phrase boundary), the terza enters above with a melismatic, ornamented line.

This staggered entry is **notated** as a fixed voice-entry mask: [V0 solo → +V1 → +V2] — applied at each verse/strophe repeat.

### Phase 5: Voice Interaction and "Fourth Sound"
Once all three voices are active (S2), the texture follows established procedures:

1. **Bassu descent gradient**: The bassu starts each phrase at a mid-range pitch and **descends monotonically** (with possible stays) to the lowest tone by the cadence. This creates a physical "gravitational pull" feeling.
2. **Terza freedom**: The terza moves freely above, with melismatic runs on open vowels. It may echo the secunda's phrase tail, create decorative counterpoint, or hold a long high note.
3. **Just intonation**: Singers tune by ear to pure intervals, not equal temperament. This produces slight microtonal inflections that create **audible beating** between voices — a characteristic dense, shimmering texture.
4. **"Fourth Sound"** (a voce di l'anghjuli): When the three voices are well-tuned in a resonant space (stone church, village square), a **resultant overtone** emerges that sounds like a fourth voice coming from somewhere else. This is an acoustic phenomenon — the sum tone or difference tone from the combination of pure intervals — and is the most celebrated acoustic effect of paghjella.

### Phase 6: Cadence — Tierce de Picardie Convergence
The cadence formula is strictly:

1. **Bassu** lands on the **tonic** (or, in some regional variants, the 5th of the tonic).
2. **Secunda** lands on the **tonic** or the **3rd** of the final major chord.
3. **Terza** lands on the **major 3rd** above the tonic — this is the Picardy-third "lift" that characterises the tradition.
4. All voices converge on the **same vowel** (typically [a] or [o]) and sustain through the reverberation decay.

### Phase 7: Ornamentation and Variation
- **Secunda**: may vary melody slightly on strophic repeats (partial improvisation). Never invents new melody; varies the delivery.
- **Bassu**: may shift between sustained pedal and slow descent; occasionally adds a simple counter-melody in the lower register.
- **Terza**: the primary ornamental voice — mordents, turns, melismatic runs, held high notes, swooping entrances from above.
- **Mimofonia** (Nando Acquaviva's system): a gestural composition technique where the lead singer uses hand/finger signs to direct the bassu and terza's harmonic choices in real time (hold, go up, go down, leap, cadence). This is a living, corporeal composition method — the composer directs the harmony with his hands while singing.

### Phase 8: Macro-Form
The piece unfolds as a **breath-arch** — each phrase is a mini-arch:

```
[Solo secunda] → [Full trio entry] → [Echo/response] → [Cadence convergence] → [Decay/reset]
```

For strophic songs, this arch repeats for each verse. For through-composed sacred pieces, the arch may span the entire piece, with a final, conclusive tierce de Picardie that is not followed by another verse.

### Phase 9: Performance
- **Close proximity**: singers stand in a tight circle (facing each other, arms around shoulders) to blend timbres and feel each other's breath.
- **No conductor**: the group is directed by collective breath and the secunda's phrasing cues (and, in mimofonia, by hand gestures).
- **No vibrato**: classical vibrato is avoided; the voice is "open-throated" — a direct, powerful chest voice with minimum obstruction in the vocal tract.
- **Flexible tempo**: rubato, speech-like rhythm. No strict metre. Singers breathe together.

| S5 | Release/Decay | 1–4 | All or V0 only | Sustained final chord with decay to silence. May include a hummed fade. |

### Rules

1. **Fixed entry order**: [V0 solo]→[+V1 offset≥2 beats]→[+V2 offset≥1 bar after V1]. Never deviate from this sequence at any section start.
2. **Harmonic convergence**: Every S4 cadence: V1=tonic, V2=major 3rd. Final chord always major.
3. **Modal pitch constraint**: All pitches ⊆ chosen Corsican mode (Phrygian/Dorian/Aeolian with characteristic flattened notes).
4. **Bassu descent gradient**: V1 must start phrase at mid-range and descend monotonically (allowing holds) to cadence.
5. **Non-simultaneous entry**: V0/V1/V2 must never start a section simultaneously — always staggered.
6. **No instrument/percussion**: No percussion tracks, no instrumental programs. Pure vocal.
7. **Rubato flexibility**: Section durations may use fermata-style holds at cadence points. Not strict metronome.
8. **Tierce de Picardie guard**: Last cadence automatically checked for major 3rd convergence. If missing, force V2 to raise.
9. **Zero-drift invariant**: All active voices in any section must span exactly the same tick duration. Sustained notes pad to section length.
10. **Echo mirror**: If V3 (antiphonal echo) is active, its content must be a phrase-offset copy of V0's preceding material.

## Quirks / Pitfalls

1. **Equal temperament vs. just intonation**: The "fourth sound" effect that defines paghjella depends on just-intonation tuning between voices. If the UnitMatrix implements the pitches as equal-temperament MIDI notes, the beating and emergent overtone will NOT reproduce. The "fourth sound" is a real acoustic phenomenon that requires performance in resonant spaces with live voices — MIDI playback cannot replicate it. The UnitMatrix should document this limitation.

2. **No percussion**: Paghjella has no percussion, no drums, no rhythm section. The rhythm is speech-rubato. Adding a metronomic grid would break the tradition's feel.

3. **Gender**: Traditionally men-only (confraternal context). Modern mixed-gender groups exist but are debated. The UnitMatrix should default to male vocal registers but leave voice name/range configurable.

4. **Variability per village**: Every Corsican village has its own repertoire and slight tuning nuances. There is no single "authoritative" paghjella — it is a living local tradition. The rules above capture the common core.

5. **Mimofonia is rare**: The hand-sign direction system (Nando Acquaviva) is highly specialised and not universally practised. It's documented here as an advanced technique, not the norm.

6. **Tierce de Picardie is mandatory**: This is the one absolute harmonic rule. A paghjella that ends on a minor chord is considered wrong by practitioners.

7. **Open-throated vocal timbre**: The Corsican "open-throated" production (no vibrato, direct chest voice) is critical to the sound. The UnitMatrix cannot model timbre, but the documentation should flag that any MIDI rendering will lose the essential vocal quality.

8. **No conducting**: The group self-organises by breath. In the UnitMatrix, this translates to flexible timing and the rule that all voices must be able to "breathe together" at phrase boundaries — i.e., section lengths must accommodate breath phrasing, not just beat counts.
