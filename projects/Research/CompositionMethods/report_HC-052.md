## 1. Method Overview

Reggae riddim construction is a **bass-led ensemble craft** native to Jamaica. The English creole term "riddim" refers not just to the drum pattern but to the **complete instrumental loop** — bass line, drum groove, guitar skank, organ bubble, and optional horns — that forms the immutable identity of a song. Unlike Western songwriting where the vocal melody is primary, in reggae the riddim IS the composition. Multiple vocal songs can be recorded over the same riddim (the "one riddim, many songs" culture pioneered at Coxsone Dodd's Studio One). The producer's mixing board is a performance instrument in its own right — arrangement proceeds by subtractive masking: which voices are audible at any moment over the frozen loop.

Key human craft procedures:
- **Bass line = composition**: The bass line is composed first. It defines the song. No riddim without the bass.
- **Anticipation rule**: Bass note arrives BEFORE the downbeat (on "and of 4"), sustains through beats 1–2, re-articulates on beat 3 with the kick drum.
- **One-drop drum pattern**: Snare and kick drum strike TOGETHER on beat 3 (beats 1, 2, 4 have no kick). This "dropped" beat 1 is the reggae signature.
- **Offbeat stratification**: EVERY chordal instrument plays exclusively on offbeats — guitar skank on & of each beat, organ bubble on 2&4 + all upbeats. Downbeats are silence for harmony instruments.
- **Dub production as arrangement**: The mixing board's faders, spring reverb sends, tape echo feedback, and filter sweeps are used to create sectional form by subtraction. The loop never changes; the mix does.

## 2. Tradition & Culture

**Origin**: Jamaican popular music emerged from the fusion of:
- North American R&B and jazz (heard on Jamaican radio and sound systems in the 1940s–50s)
- Indigenous Jamaican mento (rural folk, banjo + rhumba box, calypso-adjacent)
- Cuban son rhythms (via records and broadcasts)
- African-derived drumming traditions retained in Revival Zion churches

**Historical phases**:
- **Ska** (~1957–1965): Up-tempo, piano/guitar on 2&4, walking bass, horn-driven. Ernest Ranglin, Skatalites.
- **Rocksteady** (~1965–1968): Slowed ska, bass became melodic, guitar skank solidified. Alton Ellis, The Techniques, Leroy Sibbles.
- **Roots Reggae** (~1968–1980): One-drop codified, bass anticipation rule, lyrical turn to Rastafari spirituality and political consciousness. Bob Marley & The Wailers, Burning Spear, Culture, King Tubby dub experiments.
- **Dub** (~1970s–present): Instrumental riddim tracks treated with studio effects — spring reverb, tape echo, filter sweeps, channel drops. King Tubby, Lee "Scratch" Perry, Scientist. The engineer as composer.
- **Digital/Dancehall** (~1980s–present): Riddims become synthesized, faster (75–90 BPM), drum-machine based. Sizzla, Capleton, Morgan Heritage. The "one riddim, many songs" culture reaches its peak with thousands of songs on any given digital riddim.

## 3. Craft Procedure (Detailed)

### Step 1 — Fix the Riddim Type & Tempo
- Choose the drum architecture: **One-Drop** (K+S on 3, no other kick — roots reggae signature), **Rockers** (K on 1&3, S on 2&4 — more rock, Sly Dunbar's innovation), **Steppers** (K on all four beats — high energy roots/dancehall), or **Rub-a-Dub** (sparse dancehall minimal, early digital).
- Set BPM: 65–80 (roots), 75–90 (digital/dancehall), 60–75 (rocksteady).
- Fix key: Dorian mode overwhelmingly dominant (i–VII–IV–V). The i chord establishes tonic; the ♭VII is the characteristic reggae harmonic move. Major-key I–VII–IV–I also appears (rocksteady ears, lovers rock).

### Step 2 — Compose the Bass Line (The Riddim)
This is the core compositional act. The bass line IS the song.
- **Anticipation rule**: The bass note arrives on beat 4.5 (the "and of 4") of the PREVIOUS bar, before the downbeat. It sustains through beats 1–2. It re-articulates on beat 3 with the kick drum. It holds through beat 4. This creates the characteristic forward-leaning, breathless push.
- Use root–fifth–octave vocabulary: Root on the 4.5 entrance, fifth on the 3 re-articulation, octave jumps, chromatic approach notes in between. Stay in the 40–100 Hz sub-heavy register.
- The bass cell is 2–4 bars, repeated identically throughout the song. No development. No modulation. The loop IS the composition.
- Bass tone: flatwound strings, tone knob rolled back to 30%, picked near the neck, heavy gauge strings (0.50+). In digital era: sine/triangle wave synth bass, low-pass filter, slow attack/release.

### Step 3 — Build the Drum Pattern
- **One-drop**: Kick drum and snare (rimshot) strike TOGETHER on beat 3. The kick is completely silent on beats 1, 2, and 4. Hi-hat plays open on all four offbeats (& of 1, 2, 3, 4) — the "chick-chick" sizzle. Cross-stick rim click optional on the & of 3.
- **Rockers**: Kick on beats 1 AND 3, snare on 2 and 4. Ride cymbal pattern more active. The groove sits heavier, closer to rock but with the reggae offbeat feel.
- **Steppers**: Kick on all four downbeats. The snare can be on 3 or vary. Used for high-energy dancehall.
- Fills are minimal — a single tom flam on the turnaround of a section, a roll into the next section's downbeat. The space between drum hits IS the rhythm.

### Step 4 — Guitar Skank
- Strum muted chords on the & of each beat (four times per bar). Palm-mute the strings immediately — each chord lasts less than an 8th note.
- Upper strings only: play only the top 3–4 strings (D, G, B, e). No low E or A strings in the voicing. This clears the bass frequency range.
- Chord quality matches the harmonic loop: Am7, Cmaj7, Dm7, G7, Fmaj7 depending on the loop. The chord voicing is frozen — same inversion each repetition.
- Tone: mid-frequency punch, light overdrive or clean, the "cluck" quality is from the palm mute.

### Step 5 — Organ Bubble (Jackie Mittoo technique)
- Right hand plays staccato chords on beats 2 & 4 (with the guitar skank).
- Left hand plays on ALL upbeats (the "ands" of 1, 2, 3, 4) — short chord stabs.
- This creates the rolling "bubble" texture: the two hands rhythmically interlock.
- Hammond C3 tone, Leslie speaker on slow speed, sometimes crossfading reverb/delay.

### Step 6 — Horn / Melodica Counter-Melody
- Compose a 2–4 bar antecedent-consequent unison/octave phrase for trumpet, sax, trombone (or melodica in digital era).
- The horn line fills the gaps left by the vocal phrase — answering the call in the call-and-response structure.
- Starts on beat 1 or the "and of 4", lands on root on beat 3. Never overlaps the lead vocal.
- Often: 2-bar phrase = answer to the previous 2-bar vocal phrase. Completes the 4-bar group.

### Step 7 — Dub Production (The Mix as Composition)
- **King Tubby's innovation**: The mixing board faders as compositional tools.
- **Arrangement by subtraction**: The riddim loop repeats unchanged. Section form is created by turning voices on/off:
  - Intro: Bass + drums + skank + bubble only.
  - Verse: Add vocal. Horns and percussion off.
  - Chorus: Full arrangement — vocal, horns, shaker, maybe extra percussion.
  - Dub break: Strip to bass + drums + spring reverb on snare + tape echo on random hits.
- **Signature effects**: Spring reverb (drench the snare), tape echo (repeat drum hits at rhythmic intervals, feedback drives the repeat count), filter sweep (low-pass filter on bass or whole mix, slowly open/close), channel mute automation (isolate one instrument per beat for a bar).

### Step 8 — Riddim Reuse ("One Riddim, Many Songs")
- The completed riddim track (instrumental loop) is recorded once. Multiple singers record verses over the same instrumental.
- Studio One's "Real Rock" riddim has appeared on 40+ recordings by different artists.
- In dancehall: a digital riddim is built (synths, drum machine), released as a "riddim" (instrumental), and dozens of singers record their own version over it.

## 4. Practitioner Examples

### Coxsone Dodd & The Studio One Session Band (Kingston, 1960s–70s)
Clement "Coxsone" Dodd founded Studio One, Jamaica's first independent recording studio. His house band included:
- **Leroy Sibbles** (bass): Claimed authorship of most classic Studio One riddims. His bass lines on "Real Rock", "Full Up", "Heavenless" defined the bass-anticipation rule.
- **Jackie Mittoo** (keyboards): The organ bubble authority. His spare, space-filling bubble on the "Cuss Cuss" riddim is the archetype.
- **Ernest Ranglin** (guitar): Credited with originating the muted offbeat skank. His top-3-strings-only voicing, palm-muted "chick" chord on the & of each beat.
- **Riddims as method**: No written scores. Riddims were worked out in the studio in real time, memorized, recorded in one take.

### Aston "Family Man" Barrett (Bob Marley & The Wailers, 1970s)
The embodiment of the bass anticipation rule. His bass for "No Woman No Cry" enters on the "and of 4" of the pickup bar, sustains the root through bars 1–2, hits the 5th on beat 3, descends to 3 on 4, then chromatic approach to next chord on "and of 4". The pattern is absolute — never deviates. He called this the "bass heartbeat." His other classic lines: "Three Little Birds", "Could You Be Loved", "Jamming", "Buffalo Soldier".

### Carlton Barrett (Drummer, Wailers)
Codified the one-drop drum pattern: kick and snare locked together on beat 3, hi-hat offbeats continuous. His drumming is paradoxically busy in its simplicity — the absence of kick on 1, 2, and 4 is the point. The snare rimshot on 3, open hi-hat sizzle on the & of 3, cross-stick accents. He never played a standard rock backbeat on a reggae track.

### Sly & Robbie (Sly Dunbar + Robbie Shakespeare, 1970s–present)
The rockers riddim architects. Robbie Shakespeare's bass was melodic, aggressive, anticipated both chord root AND rhythm. Sly Dunbar's rockers pattern (kick 1&3, snare 2&4) was a conscious departure from the one-drop — used on Peter Tosh's "Equal Rights" sessions, Grace Jones' "Nightclubbing". The "fish tank" bass sound (low-pass filter, heavy compression, moderate sub-bass boost) became the digital era template.

### King Tubby (Osbourne Ruddock, 1970s)
Invented dub mixing. He took completed riddim tapes and used the mixing console as a live instrument: spring reverb sends, tape echo feedback loops, channel mutes that dropped instruments in/out on unpredictable timings, high-pass/low-pass filter sweeps. His 1970s "King Tubby's Meets Rockers Uptown" is the ur-text of dub arrangement-by-subtraction.

### Lee "Scratch" Perry (Black Ark Studio, 1970s)
Approached the riddim as a living organism. Used unconventional recording techniques: microphones hung from trees, spring reverb on everything, faders thrown violently, tape delay as lead voice. The riddim was a canvas for alchemical transformation. Produced "Heart of the Gong", "Super Ape", "Roast Fish Collie Weed & Corn Bread."

## 5. Layer Classification

**Layer: concrete (target when implemented)**

Reggae riddim construction is a **concrete** human method: the bass line, one-drop pattern, guitar skank chords, and organ bubble are directly notatable as MIDI events. The dub FX layer (V7) operates as automated mix processing above the note data, but still expressed on a timeline. The "arrangement by subtraction" principle maps cleanly to the UnitMatrix section mask model.

### Rules (12 rules = encoding constraints)

1. **Bass-anticipation lock**: V0 note onset on "and of 4" (4.5) of preceding bar. Sustain through beats 1–2. Re-articulate on beat 3. Hold through beat 4. No downbeat bass.
2. **Offbeat dominance**: All rhythm section chordal entries (V2, V3) on & subdivisions only. Zero downbeat chord stabs.
3. **One-drop grid (roots)**: V1 kick+snare together on beat 3 only. No other kick. Rockers variant: kick on 1+3, snare on 2+4. Template set per riddim type.
4. **Harmonic loop invariance**: Chord progression ≤4 chords, repeating identically. No modulation. No section-specific harmonic change.
5. **Dorian/minor-pentatonic prevalence**: i–VII–IV–V or i–VII–VI–VII. The ♭VII chord (VII) is the characteristic reggae move. Major-key I–VII–IV–I valid but less common.
6. **Skank upper-strings-only**: V2 chords on D–G–B–e strings only (MIDI range ≥62). Palm-muted duration ≤ 16th note. Full rest between 8th-note offbeats.
7. **Layer-mask form**: The loop content (V0, V1, V2, V3) NEVER changes across sections. Sections are pure voice-on/off masks. Zero new note material.
8. **Horn gap-fill**: V4 must NOT overlap V5 vocal phrases. Fills gaps — at least 2 beats of silence between V4 phrase end and V5 phrase start.
9. **Dub FX submix**: V7 automation events do not alter note data. Reverb sends, echo feedback, filter cutoff — all mix-only.
10. **Call-response spacing**: Every V5 phrase must leave ≥2 beats gap within each 4-bar group for V4 horns or V6 backing chorus to answer.
11. **Tempo lock**: 65–90 BPM. No accelerando. No ritard. The riddim is frozen in time.
12. **Single-riddim reuse gate**: Any two recordings sharing the same riddim MUST have identical V0+V1+V2+V3 content. Only V4/V5/V6 differ.

## 9. Quirks & Pitfalls

- **Tempo too fast**: Most reggae newcomers set BPM too high. If it sounds rushed, drop to 70 BPM. The space between notes IS the groove. 70 BPM is surprisingly common in classic roots.
- **Bass on downbeat**: The most common beginner mistake. Bass must arrive on 4.5, NOT on 1. A downbeat bass sounds like rock/reggaeton, not roots reggae.
- **Too many drum fills**: The one-drop is defined by what's NOT played. Beat 1 has NO kick. The space between snare hits is 2 whole beats. Fills distract from the hypnotic loop.
- **Full-chord guitar skank**: Playing 6-string chords in the low register collapses the bass frequency space. Upper strings only — sacrifice voicing richness for bass clarity.
- **Harmonic development**: Reggae riddims don't modulate. An EDM composer tempted to add a bridge in a new key must resist. The loop IS the form.
- **No vocal space**: Reggae is call-and-response. If the vocal fills every beat, the horn has no answer space. Leave ≥2 beats per 4-bar group for the response.
- **Mix as afterthought**: In the Jamaican tradition, the mix IS the composition. The dub engineer is as important as the bassist. Don't treat production as a finishing step.
- **"One riddim, many songs" confusion**: When sampling existing riddims, the bass line and drum pattern are NOT property — they are the shared language of the tradition. Keep the riddim identical; vary only the topline.
