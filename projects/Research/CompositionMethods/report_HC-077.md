## 2. What the method is

Kulintang music is the most developed tradition of the archaic Southeast Asian **gong-chime culture** (with Javanese gamelan and Thai piphat as the region's other two major gong ensembles). The core instrument is a row of **5–9 graduated bossed gongs** laid horizontally on a frame (*antangan* = "to arrange"; *pasangan*), lowest gong on the player's left, struck on the bosses with two wooden beaters. The Maguindanao ensemble (*basalen* / *palabunibunyan* = "ensemble of loud instruments") adds four support instruments:

| Instrument | Description | Ensemble role |
|---|---|---|
| **Kulintang** | 5–9 bossed gongs on a horizontal frame | Primary melody; **conductor** of the ensemble |
| **Gandingan** | 4 large hanging thin-rimmed bossed gongs, paired low/high | Secondary melody; talking gongs (*apad*), contest virtuosity (*kulndet*) |
| **Agung** | 2 large wide-rimmed suspended gongs | Bass; sparse phrase-end punctuation |
| **Babandil** | 1 narrow-rim gong struck on the rim (*betay* stick) | Timekeeper (pulse grid) |
| **Dabakan** | Single-headed goblet/hourglass/tubular drum, rattan sticks | Tempo support; dampen/roll/open strokes; opens in *tidtu* |

**The central insight:** kulintang has **no fixed compositions** — it is organized by **rhythmic modes**, a musical unit that *binds the entire five-instrument ensemble together*. Each instrument plays its own rhythm for the mode; the modes differ by phrase length, melodic/rhythmic grouping, rhythmic emphasis, **opening formulas**, and **cadential patterns**. The kulintang player states the mode's theme and **dresses it up** with ornamentation — repetitions, extensions, insertions, suspensions, variations, transpositions — at her discretion, and simultaneously conducts: she sets rendition length and may change tempo mid-piece.

The human method in one line: **learn by ear → fix the mode → lay the pulse grid → lock bass punctuation → set support patterns → improvise the ornamented lead within the mode's parameters → open with kamamatuan (old) pieces, escalate to kagungudan (new) → play for the occasion** (wedding, feast, ritual trance, contest, courtship, or long-distance *apad* conversation).

## 3. Craft procedure in detail

### Step 0 — Learn the repertoire by ear
No indigenous notation exists; pieces pass orally from master to apprentice. The gongs are tuned by ear (*tongkol*: hammer the boss from inside to raise pitch, outside to lower) until the row's contour "sounds like a melody I know." Cipher notation (gongs numbered 1–8, lowest = 1) is a recent academic aid (Garfias; Butocan's *Palabunibunyan* collection).

### Step 1 — Assemble the ensemble and fix the mode
Full *palabunibunyan* = kulintang + gandingan + agung + babandil + dabakan; informal sessions may reduce to 3–4 instruments or kulintang solo. Choose the **rhythmic mode**:
- Maguindanao: **duyug, sinulog, tidtu, binalig, tagonggo**
- Maranao: **kapromayas** (romayas), **kapagonor** (onor), **katitik pandai** (kapaginandang)
- Sulu-type (Tausug/Samal/Yakan/Bajau/Dusun): **kuriri, sinug, lubak-lubak** (Tausug); **tini-id, kuriri** (Yakan); **ayas, kudidi, tidung** (Dusun)

Mode identity = number of beats per recurring phrase + melodic/rhythmic grouping + rhythmic emphasis + opening formula + cadential patterns. *Tagonggo* is the ritual exception: trance/healing accompaniment, unclassifiable as old/new.

### Step 2 — Choose the style register
- **kamamatuan** (Maguindanao) / **andung** (Maranao) — old style: slow, dignified, well-pronounced, moderate tempo, balanced, **few improvisations**; played by seniors; **always played first** (respect to elders).
- **kagungudan** (Maguindanao) / **bago** (Maranao) — new style: fast, showy, pulsating, **highly improvised**, virtuosic formulae; played after all kamamatuan pieces; the younger players' register.

### Step 3 — Lay the timekeeping grid
Babandil strikes its rim in a steady pulse (enters first on most modes — sinulog, duyug). **In *tidtu* the babandil is absent; the dabakan starts the piece.** The grid is invariant for the rendition.

### Step 4 — Lock the tempo-support drum
Dabakan keeps tempo with quick muted strokes — dampening, rolls, open strokes — via rattan sticks pivoted from the wrist. Mode-specific pattern. Special case: Maranao *Kasorondayong* = two dabakan players face-to-face in an **interlocking rhythm**.

### Step 5 — Set the bass punctuation
Agung (pair of large suspended gongs) plays low, long-decay strokes **only at phrase ends and cadential moments** — never continuous. Its placement defines phrase lengths.

### Step 6 — Set the gandingan (secondary melodic layer)
Four hanging gongs whose interval pattern **duplicates the four lowest kulintang gongs**:
- **Old formal role (traditionally women):** a *limited number of repeated patterns* providing a sonorous foundation that reinforces the mode.
- **Contest role (*kulndet*):** highly dense, complicated rhythmic patterns; two expert players (*pagaganding*) alternate renditions in rotation; assistants (*mulits*) hold the gongs steady against the ferocious tempo.
- **Speech role (*apad*):** using only the **three highest gongs**, mimic the intonation of the Maguindanao language — the "talking gongs." Used for long-distance messages, gossip, courtship ("*Singkaden Ka Singkaden*" = come here; "*Pagngapan ko seka*" = I am waiting for you), and warnings (e.g. Martial-law-era village alerts).

### Step 7 — Improvise the lead melody (the signature step)
On the kulintang: **state the mode's theme, then dress it up at discretion** with the ornament toolkit — repetitions, extensions, insertions, suspensions, variations, transpositions. Discipline: stay inside the mode's parameters (opening formula, phrase structure, cadential patterns); keep the theme recognizable; **never merely copy the previous player** (the audience expects surprise — imitation is "repetitious and mundane"). The lead is also conductor: rendition length and tempo are hers.

### Step 8 — Assemble the occasion arc
Order: **kamamatuan first** (dignity), **kagungudan after** (youth/virtuosity). Maguindanao weddings feature **solo gong contests** (agung, gandingan, kulintang) — a distinctive Maguindanao practice. Formal occasions follow strict rules; informal family sessions ignore them.

### Step 9 — Respect the social calendar
Prohibited: inside mosques, during Ramadan daytime (allowed at night after iftar), during mourning/funerals, and at the peak of planting/harvest. Played at: feasts, weddings, Hajj returnee celebrations, state functions, dignitary visits, court ceremonies, healing rituals (*pagipat*), parades, courtship (the *lamin* chamber context). Composition includes *knowing when not to play*.

## 5. UnitMatrix integration (Musicom engine)

**Layer:** concrete. All cells are real events (gong strokes, drum strokes); tags ride on cells. No chord row — pentatonic melody + gong decays only.

**Voices (rows):**
- **V0 — Babandil (timekeeper).** Invariant pulse grid for the rendition; one pitch class (rim-struck narrow gong); empty in *tidtu* mode.
- **V1 — Dabakan (tempo support).** Mode-specific stroke pattern (open stroke / dampened / roll) on a single drum; *Kasorondayong* uses two interlocking dabakan rows.
- **V2 — Agung (bass punctuation).** Sparse low long-decay events at phrase ends and cadential points only; the phrase-length definer.
- **V3 — Gandingan (secondary melody).** Old-style: limited repeated mode-support patterns. Contest: dense *kulndet* patterns with per-voice density caps and player-rotation tags. Speech (*apad*): 3-gong speech-tone row following a Maguindanao intonation contour (with a text metadata row).
- **V4 — Kulintang (lead melody + ornament).** Mode theme stated, then ornamented via operator set {repetition, extension, insertion, suspension, variation, transposition}; the conductor row (rendition length, tempo control).
- **V5 (optional) — Ritual/vocal row.** *Sagayan* trance dancer / *tunong* spirit-call moments (tagonggo).
- **V6 (metadata, non-sounding).** Mode tag, style gate (kamamatuan/kagungudan), occasion tag, per-set tuning table, apad text when present.

**Sections (columns)** = one mode rendition each, grouped by occasion arc:

| Cell | Mode | Style gate | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| S0 | duyug (Maguindanao) / romayas (Maranao) | kamamatuan | opening; dignified, few ornaments |
| S1 | sinulog | kamamatuan | dance-entertainment; moderate |
| S2 | tidtu | either | babandil absent — dabakan opens |
| S3 | binalig | kagungudan | showpiece; dense ornament, virtuoso |
| S4 | tagonggo | ritual (exempt) | trance/healing; sagayan; tunong calls |
| S5 | kulndet / apad (gandingan-led) | kagungudan | contest rotation or speech-conversation |

**Rules to encode:**
1. **Mode lock** — every section declares one rhythmic mode; per-instrument rhythm masks are mode-specific; no instrument may switch masks mid-section.
2. **Pulse invariance** — V0 (or V1 in tidtu) is a constant grid for the whole section; no tempo change without a tagged V4 conductor decision.
3. **Agung punctuation** — V2 events only at phrase ends/cadences; ≤1 per phrase; never continuous.
4. **Ornament-within-parameters** — V4 preserves the mode's opening formula, phrase length, and cadential pattern; allowed operators = {repetition, extension, insertion, suspension, variation, transposition}; theme identity must remain recognizable (≥N interval-skeleton features retained or rejected).
5. **Anti-imitation gate** — V4's event sequence must differ from the previous rendition's (audience rule: imitation = mundane).
6. **Style ladder** — kamamatuan sections precede kagungudan sections in an occasion arc; ornament density (operators per phrase) increases monotonically across the ladder.
7. **Tagonggo exemption** — ritual mode may appear in any position when tagged ritual; style ladder not applied.
8. **Pentatonic + per-set tuning** — pitches come from the section's pentatonic row; render with per-set interval offsets (approx. pelog/slendro), documented, not forced into 12-TET.
9. **Registration** — V4 highest, V3 secondary, V2 lowest; no lead/bass register crossing.
10. **Zero-drift** — all rows end at the section terminal landmark; V0/V1 grids fill exactly; `validate()` must pass.

**Distinct in engine terms:** HC-002 (kotekan) is a fixed composed hocket around a pokok — no improvisation, no mode masks. HC-046 (gending) has colotomic hierarchy + balungan cipher — HC-077's agung is punctuation, not colotomic structure, and there is no skeletal cipher line. HC-005 (Ewe) shares the invariant-timeline + improvising-master architecture but has no melodic lead. HC-077's unique engine feature: **named binding modes as per-voice rhythm masks + a conductor/lead voice whose ornament operators are bounded by mode parameters + an anti-imitation gate**.

## 8. Quirks & pitfalls

- **"Kulintang" names two different instruments.** The bossed gong-chime (this method) vs the North Sulawesi slab-key xylophone (*kolintang*). Do not confuse the ensemble craft with the slab-key idiom.
- **Rhythmic mode ≠ time signature.** A mode is a *binding unit* of phrase length + grouping + emphasis + opening formula + cadential pattern shared by all five instruments — not a meter to be notated as 4/4.
- **No fixed compositions, no fixed titles.** Labels vary household to household (Otto 1996); modes are revised in transmission. Model a *rendition*, not "the piece".
- **Improvisation is a requirement, not a decoration.** Anti-imitation is a social rule; an engine that always plays the same ornamentation is judged "repetitious and mundane" by tradition.
- **Tuning is per-set and non-standardized.** Approximately pelog/slendro interval patterns, unique per set; absolute pitch is meaningless across sets — model contour, document the tuning approximation when rendering with GM.
- **The lead is the conductor.** Rendition length and tempo are the kulintang player's live decisions; a fixed-length loop with a fixed tempo destroys the method.
- **Babandil absence in tidtu.** In *tidtu* the dabakan starts the piece — a mode-dependent timekeeper swap; do not always force the babandil row on.
- **Agung is punctuation, not rhythm.** Continuous agung = wrong; ≤1 stroke per phrase, long decay, phrase-end placement.
- **Tagonggo is ritual, unclassifiable as old/new.** It accompanies trance and the *sagayan* dance; do not force it into the style ladder.
- **Social calendar is part of the method.** Playing during prohibited periods (mosque, Ramadan day, mourning, harvest peak) is a cultural error, not a scheduling detail.
- **Gender/role history matters.** The gandingan's formal support role was traditionally women's; *kulndet* contest virtuosity is associated with male players (Terada). Keep the role tags honest.
- **Dedup lesson:** gamelan-family neighbours (HC-002 kotekan, HC-046 gending, HC-049 kecak) are all *fixed* or *ritual-vocal* — none covers the Maguindanao/Sulu *mode-bound improvisation* method; state the boundary explicitly in any future Southeast-Asian gong entry.
