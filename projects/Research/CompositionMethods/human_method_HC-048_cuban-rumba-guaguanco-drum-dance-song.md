## Description

Cuban rumba is a secular music-dance-song complex of Afro-Cuban origin, rooted in the *solares* (courtyard tenements) of Havana and Matanzas where freed descendants of enslaved Africans lived. It is not a single rhythm but a family of three main styles — **yambú** (slow, oldest), **guaguancó** (medium-fast, most popular), and **columbia** (fast, triple-pulse, rural/Abakuá-derived) — each with its own tempo, clave orientation, dance vocabulary, and drum-part architecture. The craft is fundamentally *conversational*: drum parts interlock polyrhythmically around the invariant clave, the lead singer improvises *décimas* over a call-and-response frame, and the lead quinto drummer dialogues directly with the dancer's choreography. Everything is learned orally — no notation, no written theory — and the entire complex is driven by a gradual tension-and-release arc that builds from the *diana* (vocal warm-up) through the *canto* (narrative verse) into the *montuno* (call-and-response climax).

## Craft Procedure (Step by Step)

### 1. Fix the clave orientation

Choose rumba clave (five-stroke two-cell guide pattern, 3–2 or 2–3). This **never changes** — it is the invariant spine that all other parts reference. The clave player sets the tempo and feel for the whole rumba.

### 2. Choose the rumba style

Select one of three traditional forms:

- **Yambú**: slowest tempo (~60–80 BPM), duple-pulse primary (2/4, 4/4), "old people's dance" — dancers feign frailty, no vacunao (pelvic thrust), men and women together. Flirtation is gentle.
- **Guaguancó**: medium tempo (~90–120 BPM), duple-pulse primary, the most popular form. Couple dance with the vacunao — the male dancer attempts a symbolic pelvic thrust ("vaccination") while the female deflects by turning away or covering with her skirt (botao). The quinto accents the vacunao moment.
- **Columbia**: fast tempo (~130–180 BPM), triple-pulse primary (6/8, 12/8), rural origin, traditionally solo male dance. Acrobatic, competitive, martial-arts-inflected (derived from Abakuá traditions and juego de maíz). Quinto and dancer dialogue directly: the quinto phrases mirror the dancer's footwork.

### 3. Assign drum roles in the percussion framework

Three congas (tumbadoras) + auxiliary percussion form the core:

- **Tumba/Salidor** (lowest conga): plays the harmonic/melodic foundation — a repeating ostinato of open tones and slaps that outline the fundamental pitch contour. In yambú/guaguancó, the tumba plays the downbeat-heavy "marcha" pattern. In columbia, it plays a pattern embellishing six cross-beats.
- **Tres dos / Segundo** (middle conga): bridges tumba and quinto. Its part interlocks with the tumba — fills gaps in tumba's pattern, often playing the "tres-golpe" (three-stroke) figure.
- **Quinto** (highest conga): the lead/improvisatory voice. The quinto player does NOT play a fixed pattern — instead, he or she improvises phrases that respond to the dancer, call section changes, accent the vacunao (in guaguancó), and initiate dialogues with other drummers. The quinto must know the vocabulary of tone-slap-tone and slap-tone-slap "locks" (the quinto clave lock) and be able to switch between them on demand.
- **Claves**: two hardwood sticks playing the invariant five-stroke rumba clave pattern.
- **Catá/Guagua**: hollow bamboo log struck with palitos (two sticks) — plays the guagua pattern (cáscara). In yambú/guaguancó the guagua follows the duple-pulse scheme; in columbia it follows the Abakuá-derived triple-pulse erikundi pattern.
- **Chekeré/Maracas/Güiro**: optional shaker/scraper layer adding continuous subdivisional pulse.

### 4. Compose the vocal architecture

Rumba songs have two main sections, performed by a lead singer (who also plays claves) and a chorus:

1. **Diana**: Unmetered vocal warm-up — the lead singer intones meaningless syllables (the "lloraíto" or cry) to establish pitch center and melodic material. According to Crook, the diana "also contains the first choral refrain" — a short melodic phrase the chorus will later sing. The singer's initial diana is the single most identity-defining moment of the rumba.
2. **Canto/Largo**: The main narrative section. The lead singer *decima* — improvises ten-line décima stanzas (octosyllabic, ABBAACCDDC rhyme) over the unchanging percussion grid. The text often states the reason for the gathering, praises those present, or narrates a story from everyday life. The chorus sings nothing during the canto; they only react with occasional "eh!" encouragement.
3. **Montuno**: The call-and-response climax. The lead singer signals the transition (often with a rising phrase or "ya llegó el montuno!") and the chorus enters with a short fixed refrain. The lead then improvises new lines over each repeating chorus response, escalating in intensity. This section builds through repetition — each call-response exchange is louder, denser, more energetic. The montuno can last many minutes.

### 5. Layer dance-choreography feedback

In all rumba styles, the dancer directly shapes the drumming. Specifically:

- In **guaguancó** and **yambú**: the female dancer's skirt-opening/closing and hip movements dictate quinto accent placement and tempo feel. The vacunao (pelvic thrust attempt) is the peak event — the quinto player must accent it perfectly, usually as the resolution of a phrase spanning more than one clave cycle.
- In **columbia**: the quinto player and dancer are in a direct call-and-response relationship. The pattern of quinto strokes and the dancer's footwork are "at times identical, and at other times, imaginatively matched" (Peñalosa, 2011). The quinto player must switch phrases in real-time to match the dancer's spontaneous choreography. Columbia dancers may incorporate breakdancing, martial arts, flamenco, and hip-hop moves.
- The *vacunao* gesture can also be expressed by a hand or foot gesture rather than the pelvis — the quinto still accents it.

### 6. Structure the performance arc

A rumba performance follows a tension-build-release arc:

1. **Warm-up / clave establish** (drums enter one at a time: clave → guagua → tumba → segundo → quinto)
2. **Diana** (lead singer intones, establishes pitch/melody)
3. **Canto** (extended décima narrative, one or more stanzas)
4. **Montuno entry** (lead signals, chorus enters with fixed refrain, call-response exchanges escalate)
5. **Climax** (maximum density, fastest tempo, quinto virtuosity, vocal intensity, dance peak — vacunao attempts/climactic phrases)
6. **Coda/taper** (lead signals winding down, drums drop out in reverse order, clave may continue briefly and stop)
7. **Final clave stroke** (signals end)

Total duration: anywhere from 10–45 minutes for a single rumba in traditional street performance; shortened to 3–8 minutes in staged/theatrical context post-revolution.

## Practitioner Examples

- **Los Muñequitos de Matanzas** (Conjunto Guaguancó Matancero, est. 1952): the most renowned rumba ensemble. Their 1956 hit "El vive bien" and later albums defined the modern rumba aesthetic. Known for expanding the melodic parameters of the drums and pioneering the modern rumba ensemble sound.
- **Los Papines**: stylized, innovative rumba band known for choreographed performance and virtuosic quinto work. "Unique" and "innovative" approach.
- **AfroCuba de Matanzas** (est. 1950s): created *batá-rumba* fusion; known for combining guaguancó with liturgical batá rhythms.
- **Pancho Quinto & Yoruba Andabo**: leading figures in the *guarapachangueo* style (1970s–present), a highly conversational, rhythmically fragmented rumba variant using both cajones and congas.
- **Gregorio "El Goyo" Hernández**: Cuban rumba master and historian; documented the Abakuá roots of columbia drumming.
- **Chano Pozo** (1915–1948): rumba quinto master and the foundational Afro-Cuban jazz percussionist; made first studio rumba recordings with Carlos Vidal Bolado (New York, 1947).
- **Alberto Zayas & Conjunto Afrocubano Lulú Yonkori**: released the 1956 hit "El vive bien" that started the commercial recording of rumba.
- **Mongo Santamaría** (1917–2003): rumba quinto master who brought rumba vocabulary to Latin jazz and popular music.
- **Pedro Martínez & Román Díaz**: recorded *The Routes of Rumba* (2008), demonstrating guarapachangueo style.
- **Rumberos de la calle** (unnamed street artists): the backbone of the tradition — informal yard rumbas in Havana and Matanzas *solares* where spontaneous creativity and community gathering define the art.

## UnitMatrix Integration

### Voices

| Voice | Role | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Voice 0 | **Clave spine** | Invariant 5-stroke rumba clave pattern (3–2 or 2–3 orientation). This is the unmovable foundation — all onsets are validated against it. Never changes within a rumba. In 2/4 duple: tick positions {0, 4, 6, 10, 12} for 3-side, {0, 2, 6, 8, 12} for 2-side. |
| Voice 1 | **Tumba/Salidor** | Low conga — fixed ostinato. In yambú/guaguancó: downbeat-heavy marcha pattern (open tones on 1 & 3, slaps on 2 & 4 embellishments). In columbia: six cross-beat embellishment pattern. Anchors the harmonic/pitch floor — open tones create melodic foundation. |
| Voice 2 | **Segundo/Tres dos** | Middle conga — interlocking bridge pattern plays the "tres golpe" (three-stroke) phrase in the gaps of Voice 1. Must interlock so that no two open tones of V1/V2 coincide. |
| Voice 3 | **Quinto** | Lead conga — improvised. No fixed pattern. Follows quinto vocabulary (tone-slap-tone locks, vacunao accent phrases, dancer-dialogue calls). Adjacent clave-cycle phrases can alternate between V3a (tone-slap-tone, bars 1–2) and V3b (slap-tone-slap, bars 3–4). |
| Voice 4 | **Catá/Guagua** | Log drum — fixed cáscara pattern. In duple: continuous 16th note/8th note pattern; in columbia: Abakuá erikundi pattern (one hand plays clave rhythm, other plays four main beats). |
| Voice 5 | **Lead Vocal (Diana + Canto)** | Unmetered intro diana + improvised décima verses over the clave grid. Phrasing aligns with clave accents. Not strictly metric — the singer stretches time while the percussion stays locked. |
| Voice 6 | **Chorus (Montuno)** | Fixed short refrain phrase, entered after lead signal. Repeats cyclically. Call-response overlap: the lead sings over the chorus's repeat. |
| Voice 7 | **Shaker/Chekeré** | Continuous subdivisional pulse (16th notes in duple, 8th note triplets in columbia). Fill layer — no rests. |

### Sections

| Section | Bars (approx) | Description |
|---|---|---|
| S0: Drum Entry | 4–8 cycles | Clave starts → guagua → tumba → segundo → quinto enters. Gradual build from sparse to full ensemble. |
| S1: Diana | 2–4 cycles | Lead singer intones unmetered diana (meaningless syllables, establishes pitch center). Percussion is in but at low intensity. |
| S2: Canto | 8–32+ cycles | Lead singer performs décima verses (narrative). Chorus silent. Quinto plays supporting fills. |
| S3: Montuno Entry | 1–2 cycles | Lead signals transition (rising phrase or call). Chorus enters with fixed short refrain. |
| S4: Montuno Build | N cycles (open-ended) | Call-response exchange escalates. Each cycle: lead improvises new line over chorus refrain. Quinto intensifies — more slaps, faster phrases, higher register. Vocal intensity rises. Dance arc rises toward vacunao moments. |
| S5: Climax | 4–8 cycles | Maximum density — all drums at peak volume, fastest tempo, quinto virtuosity peak, dancers at peak (vacunao attempts or columbia acrobatics). |
| S6: Coda/Taper | 4–8 cycles | Lead sings winding-down phrase. Drums drop out in reverse order. Clave may continue alone. Final clave stroke ends. |

### Rules

- **Clave invariance**: Voice 0 never changes. All other voices' onsets must respect clave — "cruzado" (crossed) onsets that contradict clave orientation are rejected.
- **Drum interlock**: No two conga open tones coincide in the same tick subdivision (V1/V2/V3 composite must have at most one open tone per subdivision). Slaps may coincide.
- **Quinto vocabulary mask**: Quinto phrases must draw from the known tone-slap-tone "lock" vocabulary (alternating per clave cycle in Matanzas style). Free invention but within the lock grammar.
- **Vacunao accent**: In guaguancó. The quinto must place a strong accent (typically tone+slap simultaneously, or a phrased resolution) on the tick of the dancer's vacunao attempt. This is the sole event that forces a phrase spanning >1 clave cycle.
- **Canto/montuno phase lock**: Lead singer must continue during the canto; the chorus cannot enter until the lead signals. Once the montuno starts, the chorus's refrain pattern is frozen and repeats every N cycles.
- **Density progression monotonic**: Each section is louder/denser/faster than the previous (S0→S1→S2→S3→S4→S5→S6). Tempo increases subtly through S4 to S5; S6 is the release.
- **Open-ended form**: The traditional rumba has no fixed number of sections — the canto and montuno can continue as long as the participants are engaged. In UnitMatrix terms, these sections have variable cycle count (N parameter).
- **Dance-choreography coupling**: In columbia, the quinto V3 directly mirrors the dancer's step pattern. In guaguancó, the female dancer's skirt-opening/closing tempo cues the overall feel. The dancer is effectively a real-time input shaping V3's improvisation.
