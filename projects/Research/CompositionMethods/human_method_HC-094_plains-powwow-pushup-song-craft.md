# HC-094 — Plains Powwow Songcraft (Push-Up Form, Hard Beats & "Seconding")

**Layer:** human (→concrete) — human-craft knowledge; target layer **concrete** when realized through the UnitMatrix
**Tradition:** North American Plains Indigenous song — Blackfoot (Pikuni/Siksika/Kainai), Kiowa, Ponca, Lakota, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Comanche, Crow; modern pan-tribal powwow (Southern Oklahoma and Northern Plains styles), late 19th c. → present
**Documentation:** Bruno Nettl (*Blackfoot Musical Thought*, 1989; *North American Indian Musical Styles*, 1954), O. Thomas Hatton (*Performance Practices of Northern Plains Pow-Wow Singing Groups*, 1974), Frances Densmore (1907–1930s wax-cylinder collections), David McAllester, William Powers; contemporary practice by host drums (Black Lodge, Cozad, Yellowhammer, Young Grey Horse)

---

## 1. What it is

A Plains powwow song is a **short, monophonic, vocable-set melody carried by a unison male chorus over a single big rawhide drum** — built on a three- or four-note-to-pentatonic field, sung with tense, nasal, pulsating tone, and organized by one of the most distinctive form principles in world music: **incomplete repetition**.

Three interlocking craft ideas define the method:

1. **Terraced descent** — a phrase enters at its highest pitch ("high-pitched beginnings") and steps downward, terrace by terrace, to its cadence. The melody never climbs back above the terrace it started on inside a phrase; the next phrase re-enters high. The whole song is a staircase seen from the side.
2. **Incomplete repetition** (Nettl) — the song is two parts, and the second part is always repeated before returning to the beginning: **A B B′ | A B B′ …** . The returning A is often *reduced* or truncated, which is why Nettl calls the form binary "in which the section is a variation and/or reduction of the first." What makes it a *craft* rather than a loop is that each return is shortened or re-ornamented, so the listener hears a spiral, not a tape loop.
3. **Head motif + "second"** — the lead singer sings the head motif alone; a second singer enters *before the leader's first line ends*, already varying it; the full chorus then joins for the rest of the push-up. The ideal is a song learnable **in one hearing** — so the head motif must generate everything after it, predictably.

Around this sit the **push-ups** (stanzas): a powwow song is normally sung **four push-ups** (chorus + verse, ×4), each chorus led off by the lead singer and "seconded," each closed by **hard beats** (honor beats) — three downward strokes that mark the chorus→verse seam — then a **tail** (coda): a shortened chorus that lands on the final beat and stops. Songs are *owned*: given in vision, inherited, gifted, or sold; two aurally identical melodies with different origins are different songs.

## 2. The craft procedure (how the human does it)

1. **Get the song.** In the traditional frame the song arrives whole — in a vision, a dream, from a relative, or purchased. The composer's job is therefore *reception + reconstruction*, not free invention: sing it back until it is exact, then fix it by repeated performance. (Modern composers do compose, but the ontology — song as bounded object — still shapes the craft.)
2. **Fix the dance function first.** Genre decides tempo, drum pattern, form length and vocal register before a note is chosen: flag song, veterans/honor song, grand-entry, intertribal (vocables only, so no nation's language is favored), contest categories (men's fancy/grass/traditional, women's jingle/cloth), sneak-up, round dance, gourd dance, hand-game/gambling songs (which are litany-like, one or two phrases, isometric).
3. **Choose the pitch field.** Three to five tones, pentatonic in the pan-tribal norm, with the **perfect fourth** as the preferred structural leap and a total range of about a tenth. Semitones appear mainly as a lower-neighbor inflection at cadences, not as a scale member.
4. **Compose the head motif.** A 2–4 beat vocal gesture starting high (often falsetto), on vocables. It must be memorable in one pass and must contain the seed of the descent. Text, if any, is short and non-repetitive; otherwise vocables — consonants **h, y, w** plus vowels; **i/e sit slightly higher, a/o/u lower** — so vowel choice is itself a pitch-colour device.
5. **Build phrase A by terraced descent.** Step down from the head pitch through the field to a cadential tone; cadence formulas are stereotyped per genre and per drum group.
6. **Build phrase B as contrast, then repeat it.** B usually sits lower, or shifts the accent, or answers the descent with a level recitation. Then **B returns once** — that repeat is the "incomplete repetition" hinge and the point where dancers read the structure.
7. **Return to A reduced.** Third time through, A comes back shortened (the last repetition is *incomplete* by design), often a fourth or an octave lower; transposition of a repetition down by octave, P4 or P5 is standard.
8. **Assign the "second."** The second singer's part is not a harmony line but a **simultaneous variant**: same contour, entering early (before the leader's line ends), slightly ahead or behind, adding ornaments and pushes. Because vocal blending is *not* required, the texture is deliberately unblended unison — several individual readings of one melody (a heterophonic practice inside a monophonic ideal).
9. **Place the hard beats.** The traditional method: a pronounced strike by all singers **every other beat**, with a **cluster of three consecutive hard beats** at the end of a hard-beat series — typically closing the chorus and opening the verse. Dancers respond physically (hopping low). The "Hot Five" variant puts the first hard beat four beats before the second, then alternates.
10. **Set the drum under, not on, the voice.** The drum is rhythmically independent of the singing, "in perfect unison, slightly off the beat," often related to the vocal pulse by roughly **2:3**. Playing starts soft on the **rim** and moves toward the **center**, tempo rising with it; drumming may drop out for a phrase in the last stanza and return loudly.
11. **Run four push-ups, then close.** In the **final** push-up, tempo and volume rise over the last five beats; dancing stops on the final beat; the **tail** (shortened chorus) finishes. A dancer may whistle or fan the drum to demand four extra push-ups — the form is extensible on request.
12. **Register by region.** Northern style: high falsetto, more vocable density, wider ornament, tense timbre, songs start in falsetto and settle into head voice. Southern style: lower range, rounder, more relaxed, heavier drum.

## 3. Practitioner examples

- **Black Lodge Singers** (Blackfeet, Kenny Scabby Robe's family drum) — the most-recorded Northern host drum; large-family chorus, dense vocable settings, hard-beat placement used as a signature.
- **Cozad Singers** (Kiowa, Southern) — Oklahoma-style drum: lower tessitura, three-post suspended drum, three-stroke honor-beat clusters.
- **Yellowhammer** (Ponca) — Southern host drum, straight-dance and gourd repertoire.
- **Young Grey Horse Society / Heart Butte Singers / Two Medicine Lake Singers** — Blackfoot contest and ceremonial drums documented in Nettl's fieldwork.
- **"AIM Song"** — intertribal vocable song of the American Indian Movement; the canonical example of a song built purely of vocables so that no nation's language owns it.
- **Frances Densmore's consultants** (1907 onward, >1000 songs) and **George Bird Grinnell / Clark Wissler** (1897, 1903–04 cylinders) — the earliest recorded corpus; the source for how head-motif economy actually sounds.
- **Jack Gladstone** (Blackfeet singer-songwriter) — the modern crossover case where the traditional song object meets authored composition.

## 4. UnitMatrix integration (Musicom engine)

### Voices (rows)
- **Voice 0 — Lead singer.** Head motif, falsetto entry, chorus lead line; the pitch spine of each push-up.
- **Voice 1 — Second singer.** Same contour as Voice 0, entered 1–2 beats early, ornamented: encodes as a *perturbed copy* of Voice 0's unit (micro-timing offset, +1 neighbor tone, short grace figures). Never a chord tone — a variant.
- **Voice 2 — Chorus (drum group).** Full unison entry after the second's line; exactly Voice 0's pitches at unison (zero-interval) or octave, with independent phrase-phrase overlap at entries.
- **Voice 3 — Bass drum.** Structural percussion: hard-beat pattern (every other beat + 3-stroke cluster at section seams), rim→center velocity ramp, 2:3 offset against the vocal pulse, dropout in the last stanza.
- **Voice 4 (optional) — Women's backing.** Octave-above doubling of the chorus, added by rule; skipped where the model must stay strictly male-unison.

### Sections (columns) — the push-up cycle
- **S0 Head** — lead alone, falsetto entry, 2–4 beats (the "learn it in one hearing" hook).
- **S1 Chorus (A)** — head motif + terraced descent; chorus joins at the second's cue; ends with 3 hard beats.
- **S2 Verse (B)** — lower register / level recitation, contrast; drum may thin.
- **S3 Verse repeat (B′)** — incomplete-repetition hinge.
- S1–S3 cloned into **P2, P3, P4** push-ups with a transposition rule per repetition (0 / −P4 / −octave / +0).
- **S_last tail** — shortened chorus (A truncated), accelerando + crescendo over the final five beats, terminal landmark on the final beat (this is the zero-drift pad point).
- Length is *nominal 4* but the encoder takes `pushups: int` (4 default, 6 or 8 on whistle/dancer request).

### Rules (encoder sketch)
- **Pitch-field lock**: 3–5 tone set (tetratonic/pentatonic); perfect fourth as the allowed structural leap; total range ≤ a tenth; semitones only as cadential lower neighbors.
- **Terrace rule**: within a phrase, no pitch above the phrase's entry terrace; entry terrace per phrase drawn from a descending ladder (so the song as a whole is a staircase).
- **Incomplete-repetition form**: `A B B′` mandatory, and the final A must be a *reduction* (shorter by ≥1 beat or stripped of ornament) — not a literal copy.
- **Head-motif economy**: every subsequent cell must be derivable from the head motif by transposition/truncation/ornament; forbid new intervallic material after S0 (this is what makes the song learnable in one hearing).
- **Seconding rule**: Voice 1 onset = Voice 0 onset − (1..2 beats), contour identical, ≤2 added ornaments per phrase, no vertical consonance requirement (unblended unison allowed; "harmony" limited to octave/P5 doubling).
- **Hard-beat rule**: accent every second beat; cluster of exactly 3 consecutive hard beats at the chorus→verse seam; velocity ≥ 1.35× the running mean on hard beats.
- **Drum/vocal ratio**: drum onset offset by ≈1/3 of a vocal beat (2:3 relation); drum velocity ramps rim→center (soft→hard) across the song; drum tacet allowed only in the last stanza.
- **Push-up ramp**: in the final push-up, tempo scales by +6–12% and velocity by +15% over the last five beats; the tail lands exactly on the section terminal tick.
- **Vocable-pitch coupling**: vowel class sets the pitch band — i/e → upper terrace, a/o/u → lower terrace. This is a real, encodable prosody rule, and it is the one place where "text" drives pitch in the method.
- **Monophony invariant**: octave/P5 unison only; no triads, no functional harmony. HARMONY is emergent from the field, not composed.
- **Zero-drift**: every voice padded to each section's terminal landmark; the tail is the terminal landmark of the song.

### Musical Elements Framework mapping
- **PITCH**: terraced descent, 3–5 tone field, P4 leap preference, range ≤ tenth, vowel→register coupling; register split (falsetto → head voice) as a pitch-layer event.
- **RHYTHM**: vocal pulse with pulsation on long tones; drum independent at ≈2:3; hard beats every other beat + 3-cluster; accelerando only at the final push-up; phrase lengths irregular (phrase, not meter, is the unit).
- **HARMONY**: none composed — unblended unison/octaves; the only vertical events are octave and P5 doublings.
- **STRUCTURE**: push-up cycle (head–chorus–verse–verse′), incomplete repetition, transposed restatement, tail coda, extensible push-up count.
- **TEXTURE**: solo head → solo+second → full unison chorus → drum dropout; heterophonic divergence *within* a nominally monophonic texture — the method's signature.

## 5. Quirks, pitfalls, and ethics

- **Not "primitive" pentatonicism.** The four-note field is a deliberate economy serving one-hearing learnability and vocable prosody, not a lack of scale. Encoders that "fill in" the missing tones destroy the method.
- **Hard beats are structural, not expressive.** They are the dancers' cue sheet. Randomizing or accenting them musically (e.g. on the beat the encoder finds "strong") breaks the form.
- **The second is not a harmony voice.** Treating Voice 1 as a third/sixth above Voice 0 produces a European choral sound that is categorically wrong; it must stay a contour-following variant.
- **Incomplete repetition must be incomplete.** A literal AABA loop with identical A's reads as machine repetition; the reduction on return is the human signature.
- **Song ownership.** In this tradition songs are property, given or sold, and some are restricted (ceremonial, medicine-bundle, deathbed songs). Any generative use should model the *craft grammar* abstractly and avoid producing pastiche of specific owned songs; attribute drum-group styles rather than copying songs.
- **Terminology:** "push-up" (stanza), "hard/honor beats," "seconding," "tail," "head," drum group = "drum." Avoid "tribe" as an aesthetic label; name the nation.
- **Region split must be a parameter** (North falsetto/high vs South low/relaxed); defaulting to one silently colonizes the other.
