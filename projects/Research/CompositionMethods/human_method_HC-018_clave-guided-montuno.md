# HC-018 — Clave-Guided Montuno Construction (Cuban Son Layering)

**Method ID:** HC-018
**Tradition / Culture:** Afro-Cuban — Cuban *son* (son cubano), eastern Cuba (Oriente), late 19th c. → Havana 1910s–1950s; sexteto / septeto / conjunto ensemble formats; direct ancestor of salsa, songo, timba. UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage (inscribed 2025).
**Primary Elements:** RHYTHM, HARMONY, STRUCTURE, PITCH, TEXTURE
**Status:** ✅ Documented

---

## 1. What the method is

Clave-guided montuno construction is the craft by which a Cuban *sonero* (singer) and *tresero* (tres guitarist) build a song not from a written melody-and-harmony "top down," but from the **clave outward** — one invariant rhythmic key-pattern (the *clave*) is fixed first, and every other layer (bass *tumbao*, tres *guajeo*, vocal *canto*, chorus/solo *montuno*) is hung on that skeleton so that all five parts interlock. It is a **layered, ostinato-based, call-and-response** method: the verse tells the story, the montuno *repeats it into a groove*, and the whole is held together by a two-cell rhythmic period that no single player ever states in full.

The clave is a *guide pattern* — a 5-stroke, two-cell rhythmic period (antecedent "three-side" of 3 strokes + consequent "two-side" of 2 strokes) that functions as the temporal reference all other parts align against. It is usually only *felt* (or lightly played on claves), never as a melody. Everything the composer/arranger writes either lands on a clave stroke or in the spaces between them.

## 2. Craft procedure — how a human actually does it (step by step)

1. **Fix the clave.** Choose son clave or rumba clave, and choose its orientation:
   - **Son clave (3–2):** three-side = beats **1, 2&, 4**; two-side = beats **2, 3** (in 4/4, 5 strokes total).
   - **Rumba clave (3–2):** three-side = **1, 2a (the last 16th of beat 2), 4**; two-side = **2, 3**. The displaced third stroke is what gives rumba its darker, "deeper" feel.
   - "3–2" means the harmony/phrase begins on the three-side; "2–3" means it begins on the two-side. A skilled arranger decides this *before* writing a note, because it flips where every syncopation falls.

2. **Lay the rhythm-section spine (marcha).** Claves play the key pattern; maracas play a continuous even pulse; bongó plays the *martillo* (hammer) pattern; marímbula/botija (or later double bass) gives the low foundation. This layer never stops and never varies.

3. **Write the bass *tumbao*.** A syncopated one-bar (or two-bar) ostinato on root and fifth that **anticipates** the downbeat — the strong chord tone lands on the "and" of 2 and the "and" of 4, sounding *before* beat 1 rather than on it. This "anticipated bass" is the defining Afro-Cuban harmonic-rhythmic signature.

4. **Write the tres/guitar *guajeo*.** An arpeggiated, offbeat ostinato figure built from the chord tones of the current harmony, repeating every 1–2 bars. The arranger picks one of two stock alignments to the clave:
   - **Clave motif:** the guajeo decorates the three-side of the clave.
   - **Offbeat/onbeat motif:** the guajeo stresses offbeats at the end of the two-side as pick-ups into the next measure.
   The guajeo re-voices (shifts chord tones) when the harmony changes, but keeps its rhythmic cell.

5. **Write the *canto* (verse / cuerpo).** The composed song melody, sung by the lead *sonero*, often as a **duet in parallel thirds** (inherited from the Cuban contradanza/danzón). Lyrics follow Spanish lyric metre; the verse is strophic and relatively short — it is the "information" section, not the climax.

6. **Open with a *diana* (optional).** A melismatic, free or half-time vocal flourish (often on "ay/lalala") that announces the tune and warms the clave feel before the body.

7. **Build the *montuno*.** After one or two verses, the music shifts to the montuno — the collective, African-rooted section that is the actual heart of the son. Here:
   - The **coro** (chorus) sings a short fixed repeated phrase.
   - The **sonero improvises *soneos*** (pregón-style calls) in the gaps between coro phrases — a strict call-and-response.
   - The guajeo + tumbao + clave loop continues unchanged underneath.
   The montuno repeats and **escalates**: more and longer soneos, added percussion, instrumental solos, brass riffs (in septeto/conjunto), rising density — everything still locked to the same clave cycle.

8. **Use clave orientation as a structural device.** A section can "cross the clave" (3–2 → 2–3) by beginning the phrase on the opposite side — this is felt as a major shift of groove without changing harmony or tempo. Harmony is mostly European functional (I–IV–V, i–iv–V; often two chords per clave cycle), but its *placement* is Afro-Cuban: chords land on clave-aligned positions, and the bass anticipates.

9. **Check the interlock.** The arranger listens for the composite: does the guajeo fill the tumbao's gaps? Does the soneo land where the coro leaves space? Is any voice "playing against" (cruzado) the clave? If a voice fights the clave, it is re-voiced — the clave is never altered to fit a voice.

10. **Close.** Short coda or a final coro repetition; the tune often ends on a held chord or a single clave stroke — the period's two cells resolving back to the three-side.

## 3. Real practitioner examples

| Practitioner | Ensemble / Work | Craft significance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Miguel Matamoros** | Trío Matamoros — *"Son de la Loma"*, *"Mamá, son de la loma"*, *"El que siembra su maíz"* | The trovador-turned-sonero; distributed the son's rhythmic layers across three voices + two guitars + maracas; the trio form that codified son as portable art. |
| **Sindo Garay** | Cuban trova → son (early 1900s) | Trovador who bridged trova/canción into son; exemplar of canto craft and Spanish lyric metre. |
| **Ignacio Piñeiro** | Septeto Nacional — *"Échale Salsita"*, *"Suavecito"*, *"Donde estabas anoche"* | Codified **son montuno** form (verse → montuno with coro + soneos); the standard sexteto/septeto arrangement template. |
| **Arsenio Rodríguez** | Conjunto Arsenio Rodríguez — *"Bruca Maniguá"*, *"Dundunbanza"* | Expanded sexteto → **conjunto** (added congas, piano, multiple trumpets); tres master; deepened the montuno's improvisational/escalating structure. |
| **Beny Moré** ("El Bárbaro del Ritmo") | *"Castellano que bueno baila usted"*, *"Vertiente Camaguey"* | Greatest modern sonero — soneo improvisation and pregón craft at its peak; fused guaracha/bolero/mambo into the son. |
| **Compay Segundo / Eliades Ochoa** | Buena Vista Social Club — *"Chan Chan"* (Compay, 1987/1997) | The son's global revival; *Chan Chan* demonstrates the clave + tumbao + guajeo + canto skeleton in its purest minimal form. |
| **Roberto Faz** | Conjunto Roberto Faz | Conjunto-era sonero; smooth trumpet sonora blending son with mambo-era dance-orchestra texture. |

## 4. Mapping to the Musicom engine (Musical Elements Framework)

### PITCH
- **Guajeo** = arpeggiation of the current chord's tones (root/3rd/5th/7th), re-voiced per chord, offbeat. Encodable as: `for each chord in harmonic loop → arpeggiate chord tones in a fixed 1–2 bar rhythmic cell`.
- **Canto** = conjunct song melody; duplicated a third below (parallel-thirds duet). Encodable as: lead voice + auto-generated lower-third doubling, diatonic.
- **Soneo** = improvised pregón over the montuno loop: scale/pentatonic-based, bounded to clave slots (variation-not-invention — cf. HC-004 raga, HC-017 sanjo).

### RHYTHM
- **Clave** = the invariant guide-pattern spine: 5-stroke, two-cell period (3+2), 3:2 cross-rhythm. This is a **Rules-Based (deterministic)** layer — a fixed boolean stroke mask (son clave: `[1, 2&, 4 | 2, 3]`; rumba clave: `[1, 2a, 4 | 2, 3]`). All other voices must align onsets to strokes or their complement.
- **Tumbao** = anticipated bass: strong chord tones on offbeats ("and" of 2 / "and" of 4), anticipating beat 1.
- **Martillo / maracas** = continuous even-pulse + hammer pattern (fill layer, keeps the texture flowing — see hybridization rule).

### HARMONY
- Functional loop, 2 chords per clave cycle typical (I–IV–V, i–iv–V; dominant–tonic). Chord changes are **clave-aligned** — the progression begins on the three-side (3–2) or two-side (2–3), never mid-cell.
- Anticipated bass makes the harmony "pull" forward rather than sit on downbeats.

### STRUCTURE
- Sections = **Diana/Intro → Cuerpo (verse, strophic, composed) → Montuno (coro + soneo loop, repeated with escalation) → Coda**.
- The montuno is the *long* collective groove section — verse is short, montuno dominates (reverse of Euro-pop verse/chorus weighting).
- Clave orientation (3–2 vs 2–3) = a binary macro-form toggle; "crossing the clave" is a structural event with no harmony/tempo change.

### TEXTURE
- **Stratified interlocking**: clave (guide) → bass tumbao → tres guajeo → coro + sonero → percussion. Each layer occupies complementary rhythmic slots; no two voices compete for the same accent.
- Call-and-response hocket between coro (fixed loop) and sonero (gap-filling soneos).
- Monotonic density escalation across montuno repeats (more soneos → solos → brass → full tutti).

## 5. UnitMatrix fill (Voices & Sections)

**Voices (6-voice matrix, sexteto/septeto template):**

| Voice | Role | Content | Rules |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Voice 0 | **Clave** (guide pattern) | 5-stroke two-cell period, invariant throughout | Never varies; boolean stroke mask; 3:2 cross-rhythm anchor |
| Voice 1 | **Bass (tumbao)** | Root/5th ostinato, anticipated (offbeat) | Chord-following; strong tone on "& of 2"/"& of 4"; never on beat 1 |
| Voice 2 | **Tres/Guitar (guajeo)** | Arpeggiated chord-tone ostinato, offbeat | Re-voice per chord; clave-motif or offbeat/onbeat motif alignment |
| Voice 3 | **Lead voice (sonero)** | Canto melody in verse (parallel 3rds duet); soneo improvisation in montuno | Conjunct melody; parallel-thirds doubling; soneo bounded to scale + clave gaps |
| Voice 4 | **Coro / response** | Fixed repeated chorus phrase in montuno | Call-response: fills the gap after coro; short looped phrase |
| Voice 5 | **Percussion (bongó/maracas/conga) + trumpet** | Martillo + continuous pulse; brass riffs in conjunto | Continuous fill; brass on clave-aligned accents; escalates in montuno |

**Sections (4-section matrix):**

| Section | Bars | Content | Density |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| S0 Diana/Intro | 2–8 | Melismatic vocal flourish + clave + light tumbao | low |
| S1 Cuerpo (verse) | 8–16 (strophic) | Canto melody in 3rds + guajeo + tumbao | medium |
| S2 Montuno | 16–64 (looped, escalating) | Coro loop + soneo call-response + full percussion + brass | rising → high |
| S3 Coda | 4–8 | Final coro repeat / held chord / single clave stroke | resolves |

**Encodable rule set (summary):**
1. `clave_invariant`: Voice 0 stroke mask constant across all sections.
2. `clave_align`: any accented onset in Voices 1–5 ∈ clave strokes ∪ complement slots; a "cruzado" (anti-clave) onset is rejected/re-voiced.
3. `orientation_lock`: harmony/phrase begins on the chosen side (3–2 or 2–3); crossing allowed only at section boundary.
4. `anticipated_bass`: Voice 1 strong harmonic tones placed on offbeats anticipating beat 1.
5. `guajeo_arpeggio`: Voice 2 = chord-tone arpeggiation of current harmony, fixed rhythmic cell, re-voiced per chord.
6. `parallel_thirds`: verse melody doubled a diatonic third below.
7. `montuno_call_response`: Voice 4 (coro) fixed loop; Voice 3 (soneo) occupies the gaps only.
8. `harmonic_loop`: 2 chords per clave cycle, functional I–IV–V / i–iv–V, changes on clave-aligned positions.
9. `density_escalation`: percussion/soneo/brass activity increases monotonically across montuno repeats.
10. `continuous_fill`: maracas/martillo/bongó provide uninterrupted subdivision so sparse clave + tumbao never sound staccato (cf. master-map hybridization rule).

## 6. Quirks / pitfalls

- **Clave is felt, not written.** Trained soneros internalize it; novices almost always write voices that fight it (*cruzado*). The #1 craft failure is getting 3–2 vs 2–3 orientation wrong — every syncopation then lands on the "wrong" side.
- **Anticipated bass sounds "wrong" to classically trained ears.** The tumbao plays *ahead* of beat 1; transcribing it "correctly" onto downbeats destroys the groove.
- **The montuno is the real song.** The verse is short; the montuno (repeated groove) is where the son lives. Composers trained in Euro-pop over-build the verse and under-build the montuno.
- **Rumba clave's displaced third stroke** (2a, the last 16th of beat 2) is frequently mis-notated as a downbeat by outsiders; in practice it floats between duple and triple grids.
- **Sparse-vs-flowing (links to master-map hybridization rule):** the clave, bell, and bongó layers are sparse on their own. The son solves this *inherently* by always layering continuous fill (maracas pulse + martillo + guajeo + anticipated bass), so the sparse guide-pattern + continuous-fill pairing is the genre's default texture — not an add-on.
- **No single voice plays the full clave.** The two-cell period is distributed across the ensemble's accents; any single-voice rendering of it reads as a mis-arrangement.
- **Instrument roles are the score.** In the oral tradition the "arrangement" is encoded in *who plays what layer*, not in a written chart — the UnitMatrix voice-role assignment is exactly the right abstraction.
