# HC-066 — Ground-Bass Variation Set (Chaconne & Passacaglia)

**ID:** HC-066
**Layer:** concrete (target when implemented — human methods realize material through the UnitMatrix)
**Tradition:** Western Classical — continuous-variation craft on a repeating bass/harmonic ground
**Elements:** STRUCTURE, HARMONY, TEXTURE, PITCH, RHYTHM

---

## 1. What the method is

The ground-bass variation set is the Baroque age's most durable composition scaffold: the
composer fixes ONE short pattern — a bass line (ground bass / basso ostinato) or a brief
harmonic progression — and then writes a long chain of continuous variations over it, each
statement of the ground carrying a new surface. Two historical names for the same engine:
**passacaglia** (usually minor, leans toward a strict recurring *melodic* bass line) and
**chaconne** (usually major, leans toward a recurring *harmonic* progression whose bass may
move more freely; also *ciaccona*, *chacony*, *passacaille*). The distinction is real as a
tendency but historically fuzzy — Bukofzer's verdict stands: "modern attempts to arrive at a
clear distinction are arbitrary and historically unfounded," and composers (Frescobaldi,
Couperin) deliberately mixed the two in single pieces.

Why it matters as human craft: the ground is a **memory anchor and an invariance contract**.
The composer's entire job across a passacaglia is to re-hear the same skeleton under a
constantly changing texture — the same discipline as an improviser taking chorus after
chorus on a blues, or a jazz soloist on a modal one-chord groove (Coltrane, Miles, Fela),
which is why analysts keep reaching for jazz analogies when describing Bach's variation
sets.

## 2. Origins in one breath

Spanish street music (*pasacalle* — "pass the street", a strummed interlude between
dances/songs, early 17th c.; the *chacona* came from the Spanish New World as a fast,
mocking dance-song). First written examples in an Italian source of 1606; Girolamo
Frescobaldi (late 1620s) redefined the passacaglia (and separately developed the chaconne)
as a series of continuous variations over the bass — the model every later composer
followed. Standard profile: triple meter (3/4 or 3/2), ground of 4, 6 or 8 bars (rarely 7),
often entering on the second beat, ground typically descending from tonic toward dominant
(the "chromatic fourth" 1–♭7–♭6–5 as the classic lament ground), variations flowing into
each other without breaks.

## 3. The craft procedure — step by step as a human does it

1. **Compose the ground.** 2–8 bars (canonical: 4, 6, 8), triple meter. It must have two
   properties at once: a *distinct melodic profile of its own* (the ear must recognize it
   every time it returns) and an *implied harmonic progression* that starts from an
   unambiguous tonic. Decide its cadence policy: a full authentic close every statement
   (self-contained variations) OR a half-cadence that dovetails into the next statement.
2. **Choose the ground mode.** Passacaglia mode: the bass line repeats literally (BWV 582).
   Chaconne mode: only the harmonic skeleton is locked; the bass itself may be re-voiced,
   ornamented, or moved (Bach's solo-violin Chaconne BWV 1004 implies the bass harmonically
   rather than playing it literally). Decide whether, *when*, and *where* the ground may
   migrate to an inner voice or the top voice (Bach BWV 582 variation 11; Brahms burying the
   theme inside a flute melody in variations 12–15).
3. **Compose the opening statement** — the "theme". Exposed, transparent, no elaborate
   non-harmonic tones: ground alone on the pedals (BWV 582) or the bare 8-bar theme in
   winds/brass (Brahms 4). This is the sound the listener will carry through everything.
4. **Lay the variation chain: ONE texture stance per ground statement.** The core rotation:
   - **Rhythmic subdivision levels:** whole/half notes → quarters → dotted rhythms →
     eighths → triplets → sixteenths (BWV 582 moves from syncopated blocks to a straight
     quarter/eighth stream to sixteenth-note scales; Brahms 4 accelerates note values
     across variations 5, 8, 9).
   - **Texture archetypes:** homophonic chords; two-voice counterpoint; imitative entries
     (canons at fixed beat offsets and intervals — middle of BWV 582); perpetual-motion
     figuration; melody placed in the bass; chorale-like sustained chords (Brahms 4
     variations 14–15, trombone chorale); toccata passagework; ornamented "aria" line.
   - **Register & voicing moves:** melody low vs high, wide vs close spacing, octave
     transfers.
   - **Harmony-side variation:** chromatic inflection, tonicizations, excursions to the
     relative key (Buxtehude's modulating passacaglia; Brahms 4's C-minor/major excursion in
     variations 26–28; BWV 582 visiting F minor, A♭ major, E♭ major).
5. **Build paired variations.** Two successive statements as bookends — one elaborated, one
   plain, one inverted (BWV 582 variations 10–11 in double counterpoint at the 15th; BWV
   1004's matched pairs; Brahms' embellished pairs 4/5 and 8/9).
6. **Architect the large arc.** Group statements into sections; RESET complexity to near
   zero at structural pivots (Bach's Chaconne resets at the D-major section ~variation 34
   and again at the D-minor return ~53); offset climaxes with sudden activity drops and then
   build higher — the classic "waves" shape of variation-form drama (simple → complex →
   sudden drop → greater climax).
7. **Compose the coda / climax: break the loop.** The strict repetition is suspended for
   the ending: Brahms breaks the 8-bar units, generates hemiolas (3/4 regrouped as 3/2),
   writes "pseudo-variations" in the Più Allegro coda, reaches a German-sixth apex on the
   theme's chromatic note and punctuates the final E-minor cadence; Purcell's last "remember
   me" cry leaps in register over the 11th ground statement; BWV 582 closes the passacaglia
   on a cadential pedal before launching the fugue.
8. **Optional framing.** Append an independent movement on the same theme (BWV 582:
   Passacaglia + Fugue) or embed the set as a movement of a larger cycle (Brahms 4 finale
   closing the symphony; Biber's passacaglia closing the Mystery Sonatas).

**Pedagogical doctrine** (Rubin's composition model): start from the unassuming and proceed
to the complex in waves; thin the texture to one/two voices for a "perpetual motion" middle
variation (which licenses more non-harmonic tones and arpeggiation); alternate block-chord
density with figuration; close with a coda drawn from first-variation material; use paired
variations (German model) or ritornello/refrain variations (English model — Purcell's
grounds); transfer the bass to another voice, modulate to the relative key, alter the metre
of the ostinato, and throw imitative structures over the ground to keep the set alive.

## 4. Practitioner examples (real human use)

| Practitioner | Work | What they actually did |
|---|---|---|
| Girolamo Frescobaldi | *Cento partite sopra passacagli* (1637) | First comparative treatment of chaconne/passacaglia; metrical and rhythmic alteration of the ostinato inside a long variation series |
| Henry Purcell | "When I am laid in earth" (*Dido and Aeneas*, 1689) | Chromatic-fourth ground (G–F♯–F–E♭–D…) stated **11 times** in 3/2; the vocal line varies freely above the unvaried bass; word-painting ("remember me") over the invariant ground |
| Purcell | *Chacony in G minor* Z. 730 | English "chacony" for strings — the ritornello-variation model |
| Heinrich Ignaz Franz Biber | Passacaglia closing the *Mystery (Rosary) Sonatas* (~1676) | Solo violin passacaglia over a descending tetrachord; sustains implicit four-part harmony on one instrument |
| Arcangelo Corelli | *La Folia* (Op. 5 No. 12, 1700) | The archetypal harmonic-ground variation set; its written-out ornamented variations codified the improvisation-variation practice of the era (model for 200+ later composers) |
| J.S. Bach | Passacaglia & Fugue in C minor BWV 582 | 8-bar ground (opening half borrowed from André Raison) + 21 variations + fugue; ground migrates to the top (var. 11); canons, invertible pairs (vars. 10–11), pedal entries/exits in waves |
| J.S. Bach | Chaconne BWV 1004 (Partita No. 2 for solo violin) | 256 bars = 64 four-bar phrases over a harmonic ground; each phrase tonic → dominant; D minor → D major → D minor ABA; complexity resets at each pivot; every variation a "kaleidoscope" surface over one skeleton |
| G.F. Handel | Passacaille HWV 432 (Suite No. 7 in G minor, 1720) | Keyboard passacaglia of paired variations over a 4-bar ground |
| Dieterich Buxtehude | Passacaglia in D minor BuxWV 161 | Organ passacaglia with a *modulating* ostinato — proving the ground itself can travel |
| Johannes Brahms | Symphony No. 4, finale (1885) | "Ciaccona": 8-bar rising theme (chromatic ♯5th note), derived from Bach Cantata 150 *Nach dir, Herr, verlanget mich*; **30 continuous variations + coda** in four sections (3/4; 3/2 slow interlude with trombone chorale in E major; developmental; recapitulatory with C-major excursion); coda breaks the loop with hemiolas, pseudo-variations and a German-sixth climax |
| Benjamin Britten | Passacaglia from *Peter Grimes* (Op. 33b) | 20th-c. orchestral ground variations as operatic interlude |
| Dmitri Shostakovich | Violin Concerto No. 1, 3rd movement | Modern passacaglia as the concerto's slow, tragic core |
| Anton Webern / M. Ravel / R. Vaughan Williams | Passacaglia Op. 1 / Passacaille (Piano Trio) / Passacaglia on B–G–C (1933) | 20th-c. revival: serial and modal grounds keep the invariance contract under new pitch languages |

## 5. UnitMatrix integration

The ground-bass set maps naturally onto the matrix: **rows = voices, cols = sections where
each column is exactly one ground statement.**

### Voices
- **Voice 0 — Ground/Ostinato spine.** The invariant anchor. Passacaglia mode: literal
  recurring bass cell (pitch-class skeleton locked). Chaconne mode: locked harmonic-skeleton
  row (chord sequence) with a hinted bass; the bass cells may be re-voiced. Length = one
  ground statement; every section is a multiple of this length (zero-drift invariant).
- **Voice 1 — Cantus/surface melody.** Each section's top line; the primary locus of
  variation (new melodic stance per section).
- **Voice 2 — Harmonic realization.** Inner voices realizing the ground's implied
  progression with smooth voice-leading; in solo-instrument mode this voice is implied
  (arpeggiated), not explicit.
- **Voice 3 — Counterpoint/imitation layer.** Second contrapuntal line; canonic entries at
  fixed offsets/intervals; invertible-counterpoint pairs (BWV 582 vars. 10–11).
- **Voice 4 — Figuration/diminution layer.** Perpetual-motion streams, triplet/16th
  passagework; subdivision profile rotates per section.
- **Voice 5 — Pedal/coda extension.** Sustained pedals, hemiola cross-rhythms, final
  cadential punctuation; active only in coda sections (and optionally under the fugal
  appendage).

### Sections
S0_Ground_Statement (exposed theme) → S1..S30 variation blocks (one texture stance each,
each exactly one ground statement long) → S_Major_Interlude (optional parallel-major block,
chaconne ABA) → S_Break_Coda (loop suspended: climax, hemiola, terminal cadence) →
optional S_Fugue on the ground's head (BWV 582 model).

### Rules the engine would encode
- **Ground invariance:** pitch-class skeleton locked (passacaglia) or harmonic row locked
  (chaconne); only designated migration sections may move the ground to another voice.
- **Zero-drift:** every section length = N × ground statement length; all voices padded to
  the section terminal landmark before export.
- **Texture-rotation lock:** no two adjacent sections share the same texture stance.
- **Density gradient:** subdivision levels progress monotonically inside a group
  (whole→half→…→16ths), with the reset rule at structural pivots.
- **Cadence lock:** each variation ends on the ground's cadence; half-cadence flag permits
  dovetailing into the next section.
- **Seam continuity:** next variation's opening resolves the previous cadence; voice-leading
  carried across the seam.
- **Imitation table:** canonic entries at fixed offsets (1 beat / 1 bar) and fixed intervals
  (4th / 5th / octave).
- **Key-mode guard:** minor until the major block, coda Picardy optional; modulation project
  (relative key) tightly scheduled.
- **Coda break:** in the final section the loop rule is suspended; hemiola and cadential
  punctuation allowed.

## 6. Quirks & pitfalls

- **Chaconne ≠ passacaglia dogmatically:** any "hard" rule separating them contradicts
  actual Baroque usage (Bukofzer). Treat the pair as one engine with two regulation
  strengths: strict-bass vs free-bass/harmonic.
- **The ground must be brief and memorable:** 8 bars is near the practical ceiling (BWV 582,
  Brahms 4); longer grounds blur the invariance perception that IS the form.
- **Variation ≠ unrelated fantasy:** a variation that abandons the ground's phrase rhythm or
  cadence reads as a new piece, not a variation — keep the skeleton audible.
- **Complexity is not monotonic:** the masters reset to near-zero at pivots; a relentless
  build fatigues and destroys the form's two-channel listening (ground + surface).
- **Paired-variation habit:** beginners forget that Baroque practice repeats each variation
  with modification; pair design is a structural feature, not decoration.
- **Solo-instrument chaconnes** (Biber, Bach BWV 1004) imply the ground; the engine's Voice 0
  must still exist as an internal row even when it is not literally struck.
