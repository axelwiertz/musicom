## 1. What the method is

Noh is a chanted drama: a play (yōkyoku, the libretto + its music) is **assembled from named modules** — *shōdan* (小段) — each with a fixed syllabic frame, a characteristic melodic contour, a rhythmic-delivery class, and a hayashi (instrumental) pattern. The composer's job is not to invent melody freely but to:

1. choose a source and a play type,
2. write the libretto in 7–5 meter (shichigo-chō),
3. lay the modules into the canonical five-dan sequence,
4. assign each module its delivery class (*hyōshiai* in-rhythm vs *hyōshiawazu* free),
5. set the rhythmic density of the speech-song (*yakuharai* — the three *nori*: hiranori, ōnori, chūnori),
6. pick the melodic style (*tsuyogin* strong / *yowagin* weak) and register (jō high / ge low),
7. and shape the whole with **jo-ha-kyū** (序破急, "beginning–break–rapid") applied recursively: program, play, act, shōdan, phrase.

The craft is documented in the earliest surviving actor's manual in world theatre — Zeami Motokiyo's treatises (Fūshi Kaden c. 1400–18; Sandō "The Three Paths" c. 1423, the first explicit how-to-write-a-play text) — and in the modern practice of the five shite schools (Kanze, Hōshō, Komparu, Kongō, Kita). Nōgaku was inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2008.

## 2. The craft procedure (as a human composer executes it)

1. **Choose the source (honzetsu) and the play type.** A noh play is a redaction of a known tale (Heike monogatari, Ise monogatari, waka lore, local legends). Zeami's *Sandō* fixes the first course: the material. Decide *mugen nō* (dream/ghost play — shite is a spirit recalling the past to the waki; Zeami's signature form: Takasago, Matsukaze, Izutsu) or *genzai nō* (living-drama: Yuya, Shunkan, Ataka). Category (kami-mono god / shura-mono warrior / kazura-mono woman / zō-mono miscellaneous / kiri-mono finale) also fixes the play's slot in the five-play day program.
2. **Draft the libretto in 7–5 meter.** Text is written first, as poetry: lines of 7 and 5 syllables, dense with *kakekotoba* (pivot words — one phrase means two things), *engo* (associated words), and *honkadori* (allusion to classical poems). The libretto is the score's skeleton: every line is pre-assigned to a shōdan whose syllabic template dictates the future musical setting. (The *waka* shōdan is 5-7-5-7-7.)
3. **Lay the shōdan sequence on the five-dan norm.** Zeami's *Sandō* prescribes five dan: Dan 1 *jo* — slow, auspicious (shite entrance: shidai / issei, then nanori name-saying, then michiyuki travel song); Dan 2 *ha* — waki's dialogue and approach (sashi); Dan 3 — the climax, the *kuri–sashi–kuse* complex (narrative song-dance, the heart of the play, sung mostly by the jiutai chorus with the shite's ageha interjections); Dan 4 *ha* easing — rongi (shite↔jiutai question-and-answer), waka after a dance; Dan 5 *kyū* — rapid kiri close. Two-act plays split at the *nakairi* (shite exits to change mask/costume; a kyōgen actor tells the story in the aikyōgen interlude); the second half (nochiba) re-opens with the nochi-shite's entrance and machi-utai.
4. **Classify each shōdan as hyōshiai or hyōshiawazu.** In-rhythm modules (shidai, sageuta, ageuta, kuse, rongi, kiri) are built on the 8-beat *honji* unit (with okuri/tori/kataji = duple/quadruple/sextuple variants); free modules (kotoba, sashi, issei, waka, kudoki) follow no beat grid, only an appropriate tempo and style. Moving from free to in-rhythm is *noru* ("riding"); dropping out is *nori wo hazusu*.
5. **Set the nori — the rhythmic density of the speech-song (yakuharai).** Three densities, all over the 8-beat cycle:
   - **hiranori** (平ノリ, default): 12 syllables of 7–5 text over 8 beats; the first character enters on a half beat; beats 1, 3, 5 are extended. Two sub-styles: *tsuzuke-utai* (even, lively, drummed steadily) and *mitsuji-utai* (free-flowing, rides the lyric).
   - **ōnori** (大ノリ, "large", majestic): ~8 syllables over 8 beats, one per beat (e.g. "So-no-to-ki-yo-shi-tsu-ne"); the first syllable often starts on beat 2; *hashiri* (quicker) and *hiki* (held) allowed; taiko may join.
   - **chūnori** (中ノリ, "medium", excited): 16 syllables over 8 beats, two per beat; no taiko; used at the endings of shura plays (the dead warrior's torment) and demon displays; also called shura-nori / han-nori.
6. **Choose the melody style and register.** Two melodic manners: *tsuyogin* ("strong song" — powerful, breath-driven, louder, simpler scale) and *yowagin* ("weak song" — ornamented, elaborate, beautiful, melody-led); both move on a five-tone chant core in two registers, jō (high) and ge (low). Each shōdan type carries a characteristic contour and cadence formula. (Malm 1959: yowagin melodies gravitate to a softer, fa-like third; tsuyogin to a stronger mi-like third.)
7. **Set the hayashi.** Four instruments: nōkan (transverse flute, deliberately non-tempered, never "in tune" with the chant), kotsuzumi (shoulder drum), ōtsuzumi (hip drum), taiko (stick drum — only in taiko-mono, kiri-category plays). Per-shōdan patterns: *deha* / *issei* entrance music, *ashirai* (subtle "attending" accompaniment), *kakeri* (anguish dance for crazed warriors/women), *inori* (prayer scene — taiko pattern + rosary beads). The drummers' **kakegoe** calls (ya / ha / yoi) are not decoration: with no conductor on stage, they carry the meter, tempo and strength for everyone — actors, chorus and flute.
8. **Shape jo-ha-kyū recursively.** Slow → breaking → rapid, at four levels at once: the day's five-play program (play 1 = jo, plays 2–4 = ha, play 5 = kyū), each play (dan 1 jo → dan 3 climax → dan 5 kyū), each shōdan (its own entrance–development–close), each phrase. The kyū is not only speed — it ends in the rapid cut and the silence.
9. **Rehearse orally; the score is a skeleton.** The utaibon (chant book) prints the text vertically with *goma-ten* dots beside the syllables indicating melodic direction and length — rhythm, ornament, and kakegoe placement are not fully written and are transmitted ear-to-ear (kuden) within each school; *kogaki* ("small writing") marks school-specific variants. Edo-era printing popularized utaibon among amateurs (su-utai, chant-only performance).
10. **Test in performance; fix.** Historically the playwright was the troupe's actor-manager (Kan'ami, Zeami, Zenchiku, Nobumitsu) and revised his own plays against the stage. Since the Edo period the ~240-play repertoire is largely fixed; new composition is rare and gated by the iemoto (house head) system, with kogaki variants as the living edge of the craft.

## 3. Practitioner examples

| Practitioner | Contribution |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Kan'ami Kiyotsugu** (1333–1384) | Sarugaku reformer; adopted *kusemai* (medieval song-dance) into noh, giving the kuse its narrative engine; *Sotoba Komachi*. |
| **Zeami Motokiyo** (c. 1363–c. 1443) | Completed the form under shōgun Ashikaga Yoshimitsu; established *mugen nō*; author of the craft treatises — *Fūshi Kaden* (Style and the Flower), *Sandō* (The Three Paths — the five-dan play-writing method), *Kakyō*, *Kyūi*; plays: *Takasago*, *Matsukaze*, *Izutsu*, *Atsumori*, *Eguchi*, *Sekidera Komachi*. |
| **Komparu Zenchiku** (1405–1468) | Zeami's son-in-law; brought waka poetics into noh; treatises (*Shidōsho*); *Teika* (a "poet noh" about Fujiwara no Teika). |
| **Kanze Nobumitsu** (1435–1516) | Late-Muromachi spectacle style: *Funa Benkei*, *Ataka* (source of kabuki's Kanjinchō), *Momijigari* — busy entrances, taiko-mono kiri plays. |
| **Modern line** | Kanze Hisao (1925–1980, shite + modern theatre collaborations), Umewaka Manzaburō, Hōshō Kazufusa, **Kanze Kiyokazu** (rare modern *shinsaku* compositions), female troupes post-1948; su-utai amateur chant culture. |
| **Scholarship / documentation** | Zeami's treatises (Rimer & Yamazaki trans., 1984); Quinn, "How to Write a Noh Play — Zeami's Sandō" (MN 1993) and *Developing Zeami* (2005); Komparu Kunio, *The Noh Theater: Principles and Perspectives* (1983); Malm, *Japanese Music and Musical Instruments* (1959); Hare (2008); Tyler trans. *Japanese Nō Dramas* (1992); Keene (1966); Waley (1921); the-Noh.com terminology database (CaliberCast). |

## 4. UnitMatrix integration

### Voices (rows)
- **V0 — Shite** (protagonist). Melodic spine; owns the solo shōdan: issei, kudoki, kakeai, ageha; shares kuse/rongi lines with V1. Invariant identity anchor.
- **V1 — Jiutai** (8-voice unison chorus). kuri / sashi / kuse / rongi / waka / noriji; sings in heterophonic unison with V0 (octave or near-octave with ornamental variance) — never a harmony part.
- **V2 — Waki + tsure** (secondary actors). nanori (kotoba speech), michiyuki, machi-utai, kakeai dialogue; speech-song register, text-forward.
- **V3 — Nōkan** (flute). deha / issei / ashirai / kakeri patterns; non-tempered, deliberately outside the chant's scale; sparse — sounds at module seams and dance moments, not continuously.
- **V4 — Kotsuzumi + Ōtsuzumi** (shoulder + hip drums). The 8-beat hyōshi grid; kakegoe calls (ya / ha / yoi) = the timing authority (no conductor); tsuzuke vs mitsuji patterns.
- **V5 — Taiko** (stick drum). Taiko-mono only: kiri-category plays, inori prayer scenes, kakeri climaxes; silent elsewhere.
- **V6 — Metadata** (not sounded). shōdan type, nori class (hira/ō/chū), hyōshiai vs hyōshiawazu, tsuyogin/yowagin, jō/ge register, kogaki variant, jo-ha-kyū phase.

### Sections (columns) — the shōdan sequence
- **S0 Deha / Shidai** (jo) — entrance instrumental + entrance chant (kotsuzumi/ōtsuzumi + optional flute).
- **S1 Nanori** (jo) — name-saying; kotoba (free).
- **S2 Michiyuki** (jo) — travel song; ageuta form, hiranori.
- **S3 Kuri** (ha) — upbeat introductory vocal (first of the kuri–sashi–kuse complex).
- **S4 Sashi** (ha) — free speech-song bridge into the narrative.
- **S5 Kuse** (ha) — narrative climax; jiutai narrative + shite ageha; mai-guse (danced) / i-guse (seated) variants; nidan-guse = two ageha.
- **S6 Rongi** (ha) — shite ↔ jiutai question-and-answer, in rhythm.
- **S7 Nakairi** (ha) — intermission; aikyōgen storyteller; hayashi tacet.
- **S8 Machi-utai** (ha) — waki waits for the nochi-shite (Atsumori example); hiranori ageuta.
- **S9 Kuse′** (ha) — second-half narrative; often chūnori ramp toward the close.
- **S10 Kakeri / Kiri** (kyū) — rapid climax (chūnori, kakeri anguish dance, taiko if taiko-mono) and the fast final vocal; the kiri ends with the rapid cut, not a cadential resolution.
- Super-phases: **Jo = S0–S2, Ha = S3–S8, Kyū = S9–S10**; every column also has its own internal jo-ha-kyū (entry figure → development → cadence figure).

### Rules to encode
| Rule | Content |
| :--- | :--- |
| **7–5 meter lock** | Text syllables per line = 7+5 (waka shōdan = 5-7-5-7-7); kakekotoba pivot words allowed at seams; no rhyme system. |
| **Shōdan template** | Each shōdan type has a fixed syllabic frame, characteristic melodic contour, and hayashi pattern; the composer selects among templates, not free melody. |
| **Hyōshiai set** | shidai / sageuta / ageuta / kuse / rongi / kiri on the 8-beat honji unit; okuri / tori / kataji = duple / quadruple / sextuple variants for text-length adjustment. |
| **Hyōshiawazu set** | kotoba / sashi / issei / waka / kudoki — free rhythm, no beat grid, appropriate tempo + style only. |
| **Nori grid (yakuharai)** | hiranori: 12 syll / 8 beats, half-beat entry, beats 1-3-5 extended; ōnori: ~8 syll / 8 beats (entry often beat 2, hashiri/hiki allowed); chūnori: 16 syll / 8 beats (2 per beat) — **no taiko**. |
| **Hiranori sub-styles** | tsuzuke-utai (even, steady) vs mitsuji-utai (free, lyric-driven). |
| **Melody styles** | tsuyogin (strong: breath-driven, louder, mi-like third) vs yowagin (weak: ornamented, fa-like third); jō (high) / ge (low) register per passage. |
| **Heterophonic unison** | V1 doubles V0 at the octave with ornamental variance; V3 independent and non-tempered; **no vertical harmony, ever**. |
| **Kakegoe authority** | V4's calls (ya/ha/yoi) carry meter + tempo + strength for all voices; drum grid is the conductor. |
| **Jo-ha-kyū arc** | Monotonic tempo/density rise jo→kyū at 4 levels (program, play, shōdan, phrase); climax in the kuse (dan 3); kyū = fast close + cut. |
| **Ma rule** | Structural rests are the heart of the music; never fill them. |
| **Taiko gate** | V5 sounds only in taiko-mono (kiri category / warrior-beast climax / inori); chūnori sections exclude it. |
| **Dynamics / tone** | Narrow dynamic range; straight tone (uragoe falsetto in yowagin high passages); no vibrato; "breath" (iki) is the expressive unit. |
| **Transmission** | utaibon = text + goma-ten direction dots only; rhythm/ornament/kakegoe per-school kuden; kogaki = variant flags. |

### Musical Elements Framework mapping
- **PITCH** — five-tone chant core (yowagin: do-re-fa-sol-la colour; tsuyogin: do-re-mi-sol-la colour); jō/ge registers; nōkan non-tempered and deliberately "off" the vocal scale; microtonal drift not quantizable to 12-TET.
- **RHYTHM** — 8-beat honji cycle; three nori densities (12/8, 8/8, 16/8 syllables); hashiri/hiki; kakegoe as timing authority; ma as first-class rhythm.
- **HARMONY** — none composed: unison/heterophony only (cleanest harmony-free method alongside HC-095 joik, HC-094 powwow, HC-014 Aka hocket).
- **STRUCTURE** — modular shōdan assembly over a fixed five-dan norm; recursive jo-ha-kyū; maeba/nochiba two-act architecture.
- **TEXTURE** — solo shite ↔ 8-voice jiutai unison ↔ hayashi; ashirai subtle accompaniment; density per shōdan template (sparse in jo, layered in kuse/rongi, maximal in kiri).

## 5. Quirks / pitfalls

- **Do not harmonize.** The moment a realization adds a third or a chord under the chant it becomes "noh-flavoured opera". Unison/octave heterophony and the independent flute are the texture.
- **Do not tune the nōkan to the voices.** Its non-tempered "wrongness" is structural; quantizing it to 12-TET erases the ensemble's identity.
- **Do not treat shōdan as through-composed sections.** They are templates; the composer's freedom is in text, sequence, nori and style assignment — encoding should parameterize, not recompose.
- **Jo-ha-kyū is recursive.** Encode it at the phrase level too, or the piece will have form at the top and flatness inside.
- **Ma must stay empty.** The instinct to keep the audio continuous is the #1 encoder error.
- **Kakegoe are the conductor.** Render them as rhythmic-metrical events on the drum voices, not as stray percussion accents.
- **Taiko absence is normative.** Most of a noh play has no taiko; adding it casually is a category error.
- **Composition is historically closed.** The ~240-play repertoire is fixed and school-gated (iemoto); new plays are rare (Kanze Kiyokazu et al.). Model the craft; cite the tradition; do not imply free rein to invent "new noh" — kogaki variants are the sanctioned mutation channel.
- **Terminology traps.** The *waka* shōdan ≠ waka poetry; *kotoba* = speech-style delivery, not "words"; *nori* = riding-the-beat density, not tempo; *shura-nori* is another name for chūnori; *honji* here = the 8-beat rhythmic unit, not the honji-suijaku theology of the same name.
- **Adjacent traditions.** No shamisen in noh (that is kabuki/jōruri); noh's flute/drum hayashi ≠ gagaku's orchestra; okina/kamiuta is a separate ritual layer.
- **Oral core.** Utaibon dots give pitch direction, not rhythm — a realization must supply rhythm from the nori rules, not from a score that doesn't contain it.
