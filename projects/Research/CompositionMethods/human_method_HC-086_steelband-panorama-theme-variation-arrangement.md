## 1. Method name & tradition

**Steelband Panorama arrangement** — the craft of turning a current-year calypso into a 6–10-minute
theme-and-variation showcase for a 100–120-player steel orchestra, scored by ear and taught by rote in
the panyard, to win a judged competition in Queen's Park Savannah, Port of Spain.

- **Place:** Trinidad & Tobago. Panorama founded 1963 (24 bands in the first edition); finals the
  Saturday before Carnival. Since the 1970s bands perform standing still, which allowed longer, more
  intricate arrangements (Ziegler 2015, ch. 2).
- **Material:** traditionally a calypso already broadcast in Trinidad during the previous year; since
  Ray Holman's 1972 "Pan on the Move" an arranger may also enter an **"own tune"** composed for pan.
- **Forces:** "conventional" Panorama bands up to 120 players (the 1968 Harmonites fielded 210, forcing
  the 100-player cap, later raised to 120). Instruments: tenor (lead), double tenor, double second,
  double guitar, cello, tenor bass, six bass — plus the **engine room** (drum kit, congas, plastic
  bells, scratch, iron/brake-drum), roughly 20 percussionists.
- **Aesthetic ancestry:** the pre-Panorama **Bomb** — an arrangement rehearsed in secret and unveiled
  at J'Ouvert in the early 1950s, usually a European "classic" or foreign pop tune rewritten in calypso
  style — plus calypso dance-band practice and, from 1963, the classical devices Tony Williams used to
  impress classically trained judges: introduction and coda, theme and variations, modulation,
  counterpoint.

This is not "steelpan sound design"; it is a **composition/arrangement procedure**: choosing keys,
planning a large form, writing idiomatically for seven pan voices, re-harmonizing, devising transitions,
and staging crowd/judge "moments" — then transmitting the whole thing orally to the band.

## 3. The craft procedure, step by step (how the human does it)

### 3.1 Absorb the tune by ear

Panorama is an oral tradition. Until the 1990s printed Panorama scores barely existed; most arrangers did
not read music. The tune is learned from radio/mas' camp play, and the arranger internalizes its phrase
map: which strain is the hook (usually the chorus), the verse/pre-chorus, the bass riff, the horn lines,
the "engine" of the original recording. Ray Holman is the documented exception who deliberately learned
notation — from a harmony book Ellie Mannette brought to the panyard ("I saw G Major, and G-B-D … Oh, so
*that's* what major is!"); he then worked out melodies and chord progressions **at home on guitar**, not
by experiment at rehearsal (Ziegler 2015, ch. 5).

### 3.2 Fix key, tempo and modulation plan

- Choose a key that sits well in the pans' circle-of-fifths layout and lets the tenor hook ring.
- Tempo is a compositional decision: winning arrangements 2005–2014 ranged **♩ = 119–136**, most
  **125–135**; fast tempo (♩ ≥ 120) appeared in 28 of 30 analysed top-three arrangements. Earl Rodney's
  1968 "The Wrecker" set the fast-tempo precedent at ♩ = 135.
- Plan 1–2 key changes, because the judges' sheet rewards them and they reset the crowd's ear. The
  idiomatic device is **chromatic ("staircase") modulation**: repeat a one-bar melodic fragment and
  transpose it up a semitone at a time through the intervening keys until the target key arrives
  (Helmlinger 2001). Direct phrase modulation (no pivot) is the second common route, especially
  major→minor.

### 3.3 Lay out the Panorama arc

The form is a template, and arrangers know they deviate at their peril:

```
Introduction → Statement of the calypso (a b c d) → Variation 1 → [Jam 1] →
Variation 2 (often minor) → [Jam 2] → Variation 3 / final variation → Transition → Coda
```

Ziegler's analysis of the top three arrangements 2005–2014 found theme-and-variations in **30/30**;
the first variation always immediately after the original; a jam after variation 1 in **19/30 (63%)**;
three to four variations with one or two jams; the final variation immediately before the coda in
**27/30 (90%)**. Average use of the 13 standard techniques: **9.9/13**.

### 3.4 Write the introduction (judged, 8 of 100 points)

The introduction must establish tempo and command attention: unison hits, a repeating bass line with
accents above, a **forte-piano (or subito piano) followed by crescendo**, and a run into the tune. The
run is usually **chromatic** (21/30 arrangements) or diatonic, climbing to the first note of the calypso.
The score sheet's words: "The count should be clear, demanding attention and the right tempo."

### 3.5 State the tune plainly

- Tenor carries the melody; **double tenors double it a third below** (Helmlinger).
- Double seconds and cello supply chordal strum/comping patterns; double guitar fills the middle.
- Six bass plays roots; **tenor bass doubles the bass line an octave up** (Bradley's standard, now
  universal).
- Keep the hook literal and audible. Panorama audiences know the song by heart; the pleasure is hearing
  a familiar tune orchestrated at massed volume.

### 3.6 Generate variations with a fixed operator set

Each variation re-presents the same strain structure with one or two transformed parameters:

1. **Ornamentation** — embellish the melody (turns, trills, fills), keeping the hook's contour.
2. **Rhythmic change** — same pitches, new rhythm (e.g. straight to double-time, or pushed syncopation).
3. **Register transfer** — put the melody in the **low pans** while the upper voices play new material
   above it (Holman's 1966 innovation, used in 28/30 arrangements).
4. **Distribution / trading** — split the melody between high and low sections; each section gets a
   short "solo" passage that shows off its virtuosity.
5. **Re-harmonization** — rescore the tune's chords with suspensions, extended chords, secondary
   dominants, modal interchange (12 of 100 judging points; Ray Holman's trademark).
6. **Texture change** — drop the bass, drop the percussion except the brake drum, thin to unison, then
   slam the full band back in (Bobby Mohammed's device; bass dropped in 23/30).
7. **Counterpoint** — a countermelody or imitative run in another section against the tune (Jit
   Samaroo's Bach-derived practice; Tony Williams' arpeggios and countermelodies in 1963).

### 3.7 Insert the "jam"

A jam is **not** improvisation. It is a written cyclical section: a two- or four-chord vamp, a repeating
bass ostinato, and a cross-rhythmic riff in the front line — Clive Bradley's four-chord vamp with a riff
in cross rhythm against a repeating bass is the model. It functions as an African-aesthetic groove
interruption of the European variation form: repetition and rhythmic interaction instead of thematic
development. The engine room comes forward; the band may chant or dance; a break lets percussion alone
carry the pulse. Jams appear in 27/30.

### 3.8 Build transitions and the coda

- Chromatic run or staircase transposition into the next key; long notes from the full band; a percussion
  break; sudden silence then full-band downbeat.
- Coda: reprise hook fragments, a classical **V–I cadence** ("Beethovenesque", in the words of one
  description), unison hits, a final forte-piano crescendo to the last chord.
- The arranger's stated aim is physical: Jit Samaroo — *"You must get high!"* The chromatic climb exists
  to create tension before the tune and before the final explosion (Helmlinger 2001).

### 3.9 Write idiomatically for the pans

The arranger is a pannist and composes at the instrument. Tenor layout (circle of fifths / spiderweb)
makes some lines fall under the sticks and others not; fast 16th-note "runs" at ♩ = 130–150 are built
from gestures that keep left/right sticking regular and memorizable. Samaroo admitted abandoning ideas
that were too hard for his specific players: *"I know my musicians, I know what they can play."*

### 3.10 Teach it by rote in the panyard

No score is handed out. The arranger walks to a section, plays the new phrase on a pan next to the
fastest memorizer (usually the section leader), and loops it; neighbours watch his hands and copy; the
knowledge spreads outward by visual imitation plus auditory checking. If he cannot play a particular
instrument he names the notes and taps the rhythm on the pan rim. Rehearsals run daily for about six
weeks; most players play only this season. The composition is therefore **fixed by rehearsal**, not by
notation — the recording is the score.

### 3.11 Iterate against the two audiences

Judges score craft (arrangement 40, general performance 40, tone 10, rhythm 10); the crowd scores
excitement. Arrangers cut passages that do not "hit" in the yard, and add breaks, dynamic drops and
unison shouts where the crowd roars. The formula is a known risk: too little and the arrangement is
judged uncompetitive; too much and it is called formulaic.

## 5. What the human is actually deciding (craft cognition)

1. **Hook first, development second.** The audience must recognize the calypso at every return; variations
   decorate a fixed identity rather than replace it.
2. **Register is the main orchestral variable.** With 100+ players on seven timbral layers, " texture"
   means *who has the melody and which layers are present*, not subtle doublings.
3. **Energy is managed in terraces.** fp→crescendo, bass drop, percussion-only break, unison hits,
   chromatic climb: discrete events that reset attention.
4. **Modulation is theatrical, not syntactic.** Staircase transposition makes the key change audible as
   effort; a smooth pivot would waste the effect.
5. **Write for hands and memory.** Idiomatic sticking and phrase symmetry matter more than abstract
   counterpoint because the band learns by watching.
6. **Compose for the judge and the crowd at once.** The published rubric makes craft explicit; the
   panyard makes crowd response explicit.

## 6. UnitMatrix mapping (Musical Elements Framework)

### 6.1 Elements

- **STRUCTURE:** fixed arc — Intro / Statement / Var 1 / Jam 1 / Var 2 / Var 3 / Transition / Coda; phrase
  lengths are multiples of the calypso's 8- or 16-bar strains.
- **TEXTURE:** layered mass orchestration; melody ownership rotates by section; terraced entries/exits;
  jam = cyclical vamp with percussion forward.
- **HARMONY:** tonal calypso changes, re-harmonized per variation; minor-mode variation; chromatic
  staircase or direct modulation.
- **RHYTHM:** brisk 4/4 (♩ = 119–136), calypso/soca feel, cross-rhythm riffs in jams, engine-room
  ostinato (iron), strict ensemble precision.
- **PITCH:** diatonic hook + chromatic runs; pan ranges from soprano tenor to six bass; idiomatic
  circle-of-fifths patterns.

### 6.2 Voices (rows) — one row per orchestral section, not per player

| V0 | Tenor (lead) | 114 Steel Drums | Hook, ornaments, 16th-note runs; primary melody owner. |

| V2 | Double Second | 114 | Chordal strum/comping, countermelody; may double tenor at −12 (Bradley). |
| V3 | Cello / Double Guitar | 114 | Mid-register harmony, bass-line fill, counterline. |

A 120-player band is represented by **seven rows with doubled velocities**, not 120 rows. Zero-drift is
per section: every row's cell must end on the section's terminal landmark.

### 6.4 Rules to encode

1. **Form-order rule:** statement before any variation; first variation adjacent to statement; final
   variation immediately before coda; 1–2 jams, typically after variation 1 (weights, not hard law).
2. **Section-length rule:** every column a multiple of 4 bars; all seven voice cells pad to the exact
   terminal tick (`validate()` is the compositional gate).
3. **Melody-ownership map:** exactly one primary melody voice per section, chosen from
   {V0, V1, V5, split V0/V5}; doublings allowed at −3 (V1), −12 (V2), +12 (V4).
4. **Hook-preservation rule:** structural/hook pitches preserved in every variation; operators may change
   rhythm, register, harmony, texture, or distribution — not the tune's identity.
5. **Variation-operator ladder:** ornament → rhythmic change → register transfer → split/trade →
   re-harmonize; rhythmic density monotone non-decreasing across S1→S5.
6. **Re-harmonization rule:** one chord per bar (or two per bar in jams); allow secondary dominants,
   suspensions, extended chords, modal interchange; bass root motion retained; at least one minor-mode
   variation.
7. **Chromatic-staircase modulation:** at S6, transpose a one-bar cell up one semitone per repetition for
   4–8 bars, landing on the target tonic on the downbeat; alternative direct phrase modulation at a
   section seam.
8. **Jam rule:** 2- or 4-chord loop over 2 bars; riff in 3+3+2 cross-rhythm; bass ostinato; drop bass or
   pans for 2–4 bars then re-enter; engine room continuous.
9. **Dynamic-terrace rule:** fp (velocity ~45) on the downbeat then linear crescendo to ~110 across 4 bars
   at intro/coda/transition; sudden unison hits at ~120; terraced texture drops (bass out, percussion
   alone).
10. **Doubling/envelope rule:** V4 = V5 + 12; V1 = V0 − 3 diatonic; V2 may double V0 − 12 while V1 strums;
    keep each voice inside its pan range (overall A1–G6).
11. **Engine-room rule:** iron/brake-drum on the pulse with accents, congas/kit supporting; percussion
    continues through pan rests and breaks; call-and-response between V0 and V5 at least once.
12. **Performance-style rule:** tempo 125–135, **tight, not rubato** — humanize with small timing sigma
    (≈3–5 ms), zero layback, accents via velocity; grid looseness would read as a bad band, not expression.

## 9. Quirks & pitfalls

- **"Bomb" is not a Panorama section.** Historically it is the secret J'Ouvert classical arrangement of
  the 1950s. The Panorama developmental climax is the **jam**; do not label a section "bomb".
- **Rote, not score.** The arrangement exists as rehearsed performance; any notation is a transcription.
  The arranger teaches by playing, and players copy visually — model the output, not a score pipeline.
- **Do not model 120 players.** Seven section rows with doubled velocities/octaves; per-player rows would
  explode the matrix and add no compositional information.
- **Formula is a scorecard, not a straightjacket.** Technique counts correlate only weakly with placement
  (winners used 7–13 of 13); encode weights and allow deliberate deviation.
- **Tempo is fast and strict.** 16th-note runs at ♩ = 130 are ~8.7 notes/s; do not humanize with rubato or
  large sigma — ensemble precision is the aesthetic.
- **Pan idiom:** circle-of-fifths layout favours certain lines; piano-like figuration and wide chromatic
  leaps are awkward. Runs should be scale-wise or symmetrically transposed.
- **Own tune vs arrangement:** both legal; an own tune must still behave like a calypso (verse/chorus,
  hook, 4/4 soca feel) or judges/crowd reject it.
- **Re-harmonization carries 12 points** — the single largest arrangement sub-score; a plain diatonic
  setting is not a neutral choice, it is a losing one.
- **Engine room is acoustic ensemble**, not a drum kit: iron (truck brake drum struck with nails),
  scratch (kitchen grater + iron comb), congas played with pan sticks. GM percussion only approximates.
- **Length:** modern finals arrangements run ~8+ minutes; a 184-bar sketch at 130 is ~5:40, so extend
  variations/jams rather than speeding up.
- **Judging bias:** arrangement 40 + general performance 40 dominate; tone and rhythm are only 10 each.
  Balance and audibility of every section are explicitly scored — dense mud loses.
