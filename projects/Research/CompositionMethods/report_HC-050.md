## 1. What Is It?

The dùndún is an hourglass-shaped tension drum — the "talking drum" of the Yoruba people. Unlike purely musical instruments, the dùndún can function as a **speech surrogate**: by squeezing leather tension cords with one arm while striking the membrane with a curved stick, the master drummer reproduces the three-tone system of the Yoruba language (Low / Mid / High → three relative drum pitches). The composition craft of the Àyàn hereditary guild is this: **select or compose a Yoruba text → map its linguistic tones to drum pitches → assign supporting drum parts within an invariant timeline → render in speech mode, dance mode, or musical mode**.

The text is always the foundation of the composition. The drum does not "improvise" pitches freely — every pitch is locked to a syllable's tone class.

## 2. Tradition & Culture

- **Origin:** The Àyàn tradition traces its ancestry to **Àyàngalu**, the legendary first drummer and patron spirit of all Àyàn drummers. The guild is hereditary — the name prefix "Àyàn-" (e.g. Ayanlere, Ayanbisi, Ayanfemi) marks a lineage member.
- **Social function:** Dùndún ensembles perform at court ceremonies (kings/chiefs), religious rituals (orisa festivals), naming ceremonies, funerals, weddings, and community celebrations. The composed texts are oríkì (praise poetry for deities, ancestors, towns, families), òwe (proverbs), and historical narratives.
- **Oral transmission:** No notation. Apprentices live with the master drummer for years, absorbing repertoire and technique orally. The master teaches by chanting the text, then demonstrating the drum stroke that maps to each syllable.
- **Recent scholarship:** In 2020–2021, linguistic researchers (González & Oludare, Durojaye et al.) published the first detailed acoustic and phonological analyses of dùndún speech surrogacy, confirming the systematic mapping between the three Yoruba speech tones and the drum's three pitch levels.

## 3. The Craft Procedure (Step by Step)

### Phase 1: Learning the Craft (Apprenticeship)
1. Enter the Àyàn lineage (by birth or initiation under a master)
2. Live with the master: attend every ceremony, watch every performance
3. Memorize the large oral corpus: oríkì for all major orisa (Sango, Ogun, Oya, Ifa, Osun, Yemoja…), oríkì for the town's ruler, proverbs, historical chants
4. Learn to distinguish and reproduce the three Yoruba speech tones by ear — Low (ohùn ìsàlè, drum cords loose), Mid (àárín, neutral squeeze), High (òkè, maximum squeeze)
5. Master the physical technique: left forearm/hand squeezes the tension cords while right hand strikes with a curved stick; the squeeze can be held or pulsed to create glides between tones

### Phase 2: Composing (Selecting/Creating the Text)
1. Determine the occasion (which orisa, which chief, which event)
2. Select the appropriate oríkì from the lineage repertoire, OR compose a new text using formulaic praise-phrase building blocks
3. Internalize the full text's tone contour: read/chant the Yoruba silently noting every syllable's tone class (marked on each vowel: à = low, a = mid, á = high in standard orthography)
4. The tone sequence IS the composition's fundamental melodic material — pitch bends (glides) map to Yoruba diphthongs and tone sandhi

### Phase 3: Mapping Text to Drum
1. **Low tone** (à, ì, ù, ẹ̀, ọ̀) → cords fully loose → lowest possible pitch on that drum body
2. **Mid tone** (a, i, u, ẹ, ọ) → cords at neutral tension → middle pitch (the drum's natural resonance)
3. **High tone** (á, í, ú, ẹ́, ọ́) → cords tightly squeezed → highest pitch (at or near maximum tension)
4. **Glides** (rising: à→a or à→á; falling: á→a or á→à) → executed by continuous squeeze/release during the stroke's sustain
5. Repeated identical tones in sequence → same pitch, differentiated by stroke placement (on-pulse / off-pulse) or by slight dynamics

### Phase 4: Structuring the Ensemble
The dùndún ensemble has a fixed hierarchy:

| Instrument | Role | Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| **Ìyáàlù dùndún** (mother drum, largest) | LEAD — carries the text. The "speaker." | Three-pitch speech tones. Free rhythm in speech mode, grid-aligned in dance mode. |
| **Gángan / Àdàmò** (half-size) | High support. Ostinato or heterophonic echo. | No speech tones. Short repeating rhythmic cell (1–2 bars). Sometimes doubles lead at higher register for emphasis. |
| **Keríkerì / Ìsáájú / Ìkehìn** (medium) | Middle support. Fixed ostinato. | Fixed-pitch (no tension cords). Repeating rhythmic phrase provides the groove. |
| **Gúdúgúdú** (kettle drum) | Timeline anchor. Low-pitched invariant pulse. | 12-pulse or 16-pulse pattern. Never varies within a piece. The temporal foundation. |
| **Èjìn** (2nd ìyáàlù, optional) | Bass melo-rhythmic shadow. | Two-tone ostinato (root/dominant of the tonal center). Invariant. |

### Phase 5: Three Performance Modes (the same composition, three renderings)

**Speech Mode (free rhythm):**
- Ìyáàlù alone (or + gúdúgúdú minimal support)
- No fixed meter — the drummer paces each syllable by felt prosody, pausing between phrases to breathe/rest
- Listeners hear the Yoruba text clearly in the drum's pitches
- Purpose: direct communication — announcements, praise, prayer, proverb

**Dance Mode (timeline-aligned):**
- Full ensemble. Gúdúgúdú timeline established first → support drums enter → lead joins
- The same text is compressed: syllable durations must now land on timeline grid subdivisions
- Some syllables are shortened, elided, or re-stroked to fit the grid — this is the composer's skill (knowing which syllables can be compressed without losing intelligibility)
- Call-response structure: lead plays one phrase → support drums play fixed chorus response (never overlapping lead) → lead next phrase

**Musical Mode (abstracted):**
- The text's rhythm and pitch contour are played as pure music
- Words are no longer recognizable — the drum is "singing" rather than "speaking"
- Faster tempo, denser ornamentation, no attempt at speech intelligibility
- Used in interlude sections or when the ensemble is purely musical entertainment

### Phase 6: Performance Arc (Dance Mode Example)
1. **Intro (Timeline):** Gúdúgúdú plays the invariant pulse cycle alone for 1–2 cycles. Sets the temporal universe.
2. **Support Entry:** Gángan and keríkerì enter one by one with their fixed ostinati.
3. **Lead Entry:** Ìyáàlù begins the oríkì text in call-response. Fixed chorus response plays between lead phrases.
4. **Intensification:** Each subsequent cycle is higher in register, denser in strokes, faster in tempo (subtle acceleration). More material from the oríkì is introduced.
5. **Climax:** Highest register, maximum density, all voices active. Lead may switch to musical mode (text abstracted).
6. **Close (Cadence):** Lead repeats the final line of the oríkì. The last tone is repeated three times (șákàrà) then held in decay, OR rapidly triple-stroked. Gúdúgúdú plays final stroke alone → silence.

### Rules (encoded as composer constraints)

| # | Rule Name | Description |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | **Text-to-tone lock** | V0's pitch sequence is constrained by the Yoruba tone map of the composed text. L→low pitch, M→mid pitch, H→high pitch. Pitch deviations limited to ±1 interval/tone for expressive glide. |
| R2 | **Non-text voices** | V1–V4 never carry speech tones. They provide ostinato accompaniment only. |
| R3 | **Timeline invariance** | V3's pulse grid (12 or 16 pulses) is constant per piece. Never changes. |
| R4 | **Support ostinato constraint** | V1, V2, V4 play fixed repeating cells (1–4 bars). No variation except dynamics. |
| R5 | **Density progression** | Active voices count and note density increase monotonically S1→S4. S5 collapses. |
| R6 | **Three-mode gate** | Same text/pitch material has three distinct renderings (speech/dance/musical). Mode determines V0's rhythm: free vs. grid-aligned vs. abstracted. |
| R7 | **Call-response alternation** | V0 phrases alternate with fixed V1/V2 chorus response. V0 never overlaps response. |
| R8 | **Cadence formula** | S5: last tone repeated → held decay or șákàrà triple-stroke. V3 converges to final beat. |
| R9 | **Zero-drift** | Section boundaries on pulse 1 of timeline cycle. No phrase overhang. |

## 8. Quirks & Pitfalls

1. **Pitch is relative, not absolute.** The three dùndún tones (L/M/H) are relative to each individual drum's tension range. Different drums of different sizes will have different absolute pitches for L/M/H. The mapping is proportional, not frequency-fixed.
2. **Tone sandhi.** Yoruba has phonological tone sandhi (adjacent syllables affect each other's tones). The drummer navigates this by experience — it cannot be reduced to a simple L/M/H lookup per syllable.
3. **Intelligibility vs. rhythm tradeoff.** In dance mode, compressing the text to the timeline grid may reduce speech intelligibility. The master knows which syllables can be shortened/elided without losing the text's recognizability. This is compositional skill, not a formula.
4. **Lineage variation.** The same oríkì will sound different when performed by an Oyo lineage vs. Lagos vs. Ibadan tradition — different support patterns, different timeline choices, different preferred tempi. The text is invariant; the arrangement is lineage-specific.
5. **Speech mode lacks meter.** Representing free-rhythm speech mode in a tick-based MIDI grid requires using very sparse ticks with large gaps — and those gaps are MEANINGFUL (they correspond to prosodic breathing pauses). Do not pad them with filler.
6. **Three pitch levels, not scale degrees.** The dùndún does not produce discrete scale pitches like a piano. L/M/H are continuous bands on a tension continuum. For UnitMatrix representation, treat L=48, M=60, H=72 (approximate mapping at 12-semitone spacing) — but know this is a gross simplification. The drum glides between them.
7. **One drum per role.** The ensemble has exactly one instrument per role. Gúdúgúdú is one drum playing one pattern, not a multi-percussion workstation. Do not over-instrumentalize in MIDI.
8. **Craft vs. science.** The Àyàn tradition views drumming not as a technical skill but as a spiritual calling — the oríkì text conveys àṣẹ (sacred life-force). The composition method described here is the *human craft* side; the ritual side (sacred protocol, which oríkì may be performed when, by whom) is outside scope but contextually essential.
