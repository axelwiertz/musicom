# HC-070 — Carnatic Kriti Composition: Sangati-Built Sahitya Architecture

**Method ID:** HC-070
**Layer:** `concrete (target when implemented)` — a human craft procedure that resolves straight into UnitMatrix cells; no abstract/theory intermediate layer is required.
**Tradition:** South Indian (Tamil Nadu / Andhra) Carnatic classical music. Purandara Dasa (1484–1564) → the Trinity: Tyagaraja (1767–1847), Muthuswami Dikshitar (1776–1835), Syama Sastri (1762–1827); also Oothukkadu Venkata Kavi (c. 1700–1765), Swati Tirunal, and living composers (Balamuralikrishna, Lalgudi Jayaraman).
**Primary elements:** STRUCTURE, PITCH, RHYTHM, TEXTURE (harmony is drone/implicit, not functional).
**Related but distinct methods:** HC-004 (raga-tala *improvisation* architecture — alapana/niraval/kalpana swara) and HC-015 (shape-note dispersed harmony). HC-070 is the **composed** counterpart: the kriti is a fixed, taught artifact whose internal growth principle is *sangati*.

---

## 1. What the method is

A Carnatic **kriti** (also kīrtana/kṛti) is the central composed form of South Indian classical music — the "mental backbone" of a concert. It is a devotional song in a named **raga** (melodic grammar) set to a named **tala** (cyclical metre), in three functional units:

| Unit | Function | Typical length | Register |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pallavi** | Refrain / thesis. The line the listener waits for; returns after every other section. | 1–2 lines (1–2 āvartanas) | middle |
| **Anupallavi** | Intensification / expansion. Often "answers" the pallavi at a higher tessitura. | 2 lines | upper |
| **Charanam** | Narrative / culmination, longer, may be plural; its final line carries the composer's **mudra** (signature). | 2–4 lines | middle→low, then close |

Optional structural enrichments:

- **Chittaswara** — a textless solfa (svara) passage appended after the anupallavi and/or charanam; a fixed, composed flourish in the same raga and tala, normally in second speed.
- **Madhyamakala sahitya** — extra lines of text at the end of the charanam sung at **double speed inside the same tala** (not a metre change).
- **Samashti charanam** — a charanam that absorbs the anupallavi's function (Dikshitar and Venkata Kavi use this), giving a two-part form.
- **Multiple charanams** — a chain of verses, each returning to the pallavi.

The defining compositional engine is **sangati** (also sangathi): the same lyric line is set several times, each pass a deliberate melodic/rhythmic **variant of the same phrase**, progressively wider, denser, or more ornamented, while keeping the same words and the same cadential landing. Tyagaraja is credited with making sangati a structural principle of the kriti rather than incidental decoration; Robert Morris's analysis treats sangatis as genuine motivic development and process, not ornament.

This is human craft in the strict sense: a composer works **text-first, phrase-first, cycle-first**, testing singability aloud, and the variants are judged by taste, raga grammar, and the body's memory of the tala — not generated by a rule system.

---

## 2. Why this is not an algorithm

- The raga is a **phrase grammar**, not a scale: allowed ascents/descents differ, zig-zag (*vakra*) paths are licensed, and certain notes are *jeeva svaras* (life-notes) that must be touched and ornamented in idiomatic ways. A pitch-class filter cannot produce a legal phrase.
- Sangati variants are chosen for **cumulative rhetorical effect** — the composer decides how many passes a line can bear before the listener tires. That judgement is aesthetic, not combinatorial.
- The tala is a **cycle with a hierarchy of beats** (laghu/drutam/anu-drutam), not a bar grid; where a phrase *lands* (arudi) and where it *starts* (eduppu) are independent compositional decisions.
- The words constrain everything: syllable count, vowel colour, caesura (*yati*), alliteration (*prasa*, *anuprasa*). Text and tune are co-composed.

## 3. The craft procedure, step by step

### Step 1 — Fix the frame before writing a note
Choose, in this order:

1. **Raga** — by the emotional devotional stance (*bhava*) and by what the composer wants to display. The composer must already command the raga's *sanchāras* (characteristic phrases), its ascent/descent, its jeeva svaras, and its gamaka habits.
2. **Tala** — Adi (8 aksharas: 4+2+2), Rupaka (6: 2+4), Misra Chapu (7: 3+4), Khanda Chapu (5: 2+3), Ata (14), or a rare tala for display.
3. **Eduppu** — where the first syllable begins relative to the cycle:
   - **Samam** — on the first beat (e.g. *Vatapi Ganapatim*, Rupaka).
   - **Atita** (ateetha) — *before* the cycle's first beat, i.e. the piece starts in the last aksharas of the previous cycle (e.g. *Sivakama Sundari*, Mukhari, commonly cited as starting one beat early).
   - **Anagata** — *after* the first beat, a deliberate delayed entry.
4. **Tempo** — *chowka* (very slow), *vilamba*, *madhya*. Dikshitar's kritis are characteristically chowka kala with a veena-derived gamaka profile.
5. **Language and text source** — Telugu (Tyagaraja, Syama Sastri), Sanskrit (Dikshitar), Tamil, Kannada, Manipravalam.
6. **Mudra** — the signature to be embedded in the final charanam line (Tyagaraja: "Tyagaraja"; Dikshitar: "Guruguha"; Syama Sastri: "Syama Krishna").

### Step 2 — Write the sahitya as verse, not as lyric
The poet-composer drafts the words to fit the tala's syllable budget:

- **Pallavi**: one or two lines, complete in itself, vocative or declarative ("O Lord of…"). It must be worth hearing a dozen times.
- **Anupallavi**: two lines that extend or contrast — often a question, a heightening, a shift of address.
- **Charanam**: longer, narrative or accumulative; last line carries the mudra.
- Prosodic checks: **prasa** (second-syllable rhyme across lines), **anuprasa** (alliteration), **yati** (caesura placed where the tala's internal divisions fall). A line whose caesura fights the tala is rewritten, not patched.

### Step 3 — Find the pallavi's melodic core
Sing the first line repeatedly until a **core phrase** appears that:

- begins on a note that exposes the raga's identity (often a jeeva svara, not necessarily Sa);
- arcs to a **landing note on a structurally strong akshara** — this landing is the **arudi**;
- fits the line's syllables without padding;
- leaves room above and below for later sangatis.

This core phrase is the "theme". Everything else in the pallavi is a transformation of it.

### Step 4 — Build the sangati chain (the heart of the method)
Sing the *same words* again. Change exactly **one dimension per pass**, and keep two things fixed: the **sahitya** and the **arudi** (same landing svara, same place in the cycle). The usable transformations:

| Operator | What the composer does | Perceived effect |
|---|---|---|
| **RANGE↑ / RANGE↓** | Push the phrase's apex one svara higher (or dip lower) before the landing | widening |
| **DENSITY** | Fit twice as many svaras into the same syllables (first speed → second speed), or add turns (*kampita*, *jaaru* slides) | quickening |
| **PATH (vakra)** | Replace the straight ascent/descent with a licensed zig-zag or a different sanchara to the same landing | fresh colour, same destination |
| **START-SHIFT** | Begin the phrase on a different akshara (e.g. anagata by one akshara) while the arudi stays put | rhythmic tension |
| **EMPHASIS** | Move the gamaka weight onto a different jeeva svara; lengthen one syllable | expressive re-weighting |
| **REGISTER** | Take the whole variant an octave up (usually reserved for the anupallavi) | intensification |

Practical discipline:

- 2–6 sangatis per line is normal; exceptional lines are taught with up to 10–11.
- Order them so the sequence is **cumulative** — each pass must sound like a consequence of the previous one, not a random alternative.
- The final sangati of a section is the most elaborate and must deliver the arudi cleanly, because the pallavi return depends on that landing being unambiguous.
- Test by singing with the tala clapped: if the variant's syllables no longer sit on the cycle, it is wrong regardless of how pretty it is.

**Hard invariants of a sangati:** identical lyric; identical arudi (svara + metric position); identical total span in aksharas; same raga; same section function. Break the arudi and it stops being a sangati — it becomes *niraval* (improvised variation) or a new phrase.

### Step 5 — Compose the anupallavi
Same procedure, moved up: higher tessitura, often fewer syllables per akshara (broader phrasing), new sancharas of the same raga, and an ending that opens outward — a half-cadential "question" that makes the return to the pallavi feel necessary. In two-part kritis (samashti charanam) this step is skipped and its function is folded into the charanam.

### Step 6 — Compose the charanam
- Longer lines; register settles to the middle/low, giving relief after the anupallavi.
- Often starts with a rhythmic "reset" (fresh eduppu feel, sometimes anagata) so the ear re-orients.
- Builds through 2–4 lines to the **mudra** line, which is set memorably — it is the composer's signature and the piece's seal.
- Optionally append **madhyamakala sahitya**: the same tala, but the syllables are now delivered at double speed, producing drive without changing the cycle.

### Step 7 — Add chittaswara (optional)
Compose a textless svara passage — pure solfa — in the same raga and tala, normally in second speed, built from the raga's signature sancharas. It functions as a fixed cadential flourish: sung after the anupallavi or charanam, then the pallavi resumes. Because it has no words, its rhythm can be denser than the sahitya allows.

### Step 8 — Whole-form audit
Before the piece is taught, the composer checks:

1. **Raga fidelity** — every phrase is a legal sanchara; jeeva svaras are touched and ornamented idiomatically; no accidental scale-run that flattens the raga's character.
2. **Tala arithmetic** — each section occupies whole āvartanas (or a declared eduppu offset); arudi lands where intended; madhyamakala does not disturb the cycle.
3. **Text underlay** — every syllable singable at tempo, caesura aligned to the tala's internal beats, prasa intact.
4. **Register arch** — pallavi (mid) → anupallavi (high) → charanam (mid/low) → mudra close; no section stranded in the same tessitura as its neighbour.
5. **Return path** — the last note of each section must lead gracefully back into the pallavi's first note.
6. **Scale of invention** — enough sangatis to teach the raga, not so many that the line becomes a etude.

### Step 9 — Notate and transmit
The composition is fixed in **svara notation with the sahitya written under the svaras**, plus lineage-specific gamaka shorthand (wavy/oscillation marks, slide marks). The notation is deliberately incomplete: gamaka, phrasing and tempo nuance pass through the teacher's voice. The composed skeleton then becomes the frame for performance-time **niraval** (improvising on a chosen line) and **kalpana svara** (improvised solfa), which is why a good kriti is judged by how much it *invites* without collapsing.

## 4. Practitioner examples

**Tyagaraja (1767–1847)** — the sangati master. He is credited with introducing sangati into the sahitya of the kriti as a structural device, and his lines are taught as graded chains: simple first pass, then wider arc, then denser svara motion, always to the same landing. *Endaro Mahanubhavulu* (Sri ragam, Adi tala) is a canonical teaching piece precisely because its pallavi and charanam lines carry layered sangatis; the Pancharatna kritis (*Jagadanandakaraka*, Nata; *Dudukugala*, Gowla; *Saadhinchene*, Arabhi; *Kana Kana Ruchira*, Varali; *Endaro*) are the corpus through which the technique is transmitted. His own text in *Nadhasudha* ("sarasa sangati sandarbhamu…") treats sangati as apt, occasioned modulation of speech — i.e. rhetorical, not mechanical.

**Muthuswami Dikshitar (1776–1835)** — the architect. Chowka kala, veena-style gamaka, Sanskrit text, and a characteristic charanam that closes with **madhyamakala sahitya** at double speed. *Vatapi Ganapatim* (Hamsadhwani, Rupaka tala) begins on **samam** and is the standard first-lesson kriti for form; his *Kamalamba Navavarna* cycle shows how a single architectural plan (temple/avarana imagery) can govern nine kritis. He often omits the anupallavi in favour of a samashti charanam, proving the form is a grammar, not a template.

**Syama Sastri (1762–1827)** — the rhythmic thinker: slower, denser, with tala choices and svara passages that foreground arithmetic; his swarajatis are studied for exactly the phrase-to-cycle discipline that sangati requires.

**Oothukkadu Venkata Kavi (c. 1700–1765)** — the formal experimentalist: multiple charanams, contrasts of speed and *gati*, sahitya-sangatis (variation carried by the words as well as the tune), and sectional partitioning. Useful proof that the kriti's units are functions, not fixed slots.

**Purandara Dasa (1484–1564)** — the pedagogue: graded compositions (svaravali, alankara, geete, then kritis) that encode raga and tala knowledge in singable order. The kriti as a teaching vehicle is his inheritance.

**Modern** — M. Balamuralikrishna composed kritis in ragas of his own creation and used the form to demonstrate new sancharas; Lalgudi Jayaraman's varnams (a sibling form with pallavi–anupallavi–muktayi svara–charanam–chittaswara) are studied for eduppu placement and second-speed architecture.

## 5. Mapping to the Musicom engine (UnitMatrix)

### Target layer
`concrete` — the method produces concrete note events per voice per section. Human input supplies raga grammar, tala, text, and variant choices; the engine supplies zero-drift cell arithmetic, validation, and export.

### Voices (rows)

| Row | Voice | MIDI role | Content rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Sahitya** (lead vocal / venu / veena) | melodic program (e.g. flute/voice) | all composed melodic material: pallavi, anupallavi, charanam, chittaswara |
| 1 | **Anusvarana** (violin/sarangi) | same program family, softer | shadows row 0 at the unison/octave, onset delayed slightly (humanized), gamaka imitated — *not* a harmony part |
| 2 | **Tambura** | plucked drone | Sa + Pa (or Sa + Ma) repeated per āvartana; invariant across all columns |
| 3 | **Mridangam** | percussion channel 9 | tala theka per section; pallavi/anupallavi = standard cycle, charanam may switch to sarvalaghu/denser pattern |
| 4 | **Chittaswara** (optional) | same as row 0 | textless solfa cell, second speed, only in designated columns |

No functional harmony row: vertical sonorities are drone + unison shadow. If a Western-style pad is ever layered for production, it must not imply chord progressions — it stays on the drone.

### Sections (columns)

Columns are **form units**, each a whole number of āvartanas:

```
P0 | P1 | P2 | P3 | AP0 | AP1 | C1 | C2 | C3 | C4(mudra) | CS | RET
```

- `P0…P3` — pallavi core + its sangati chain (same lyric id, same arudi).
- `AP0…AP1` — anupallavi lines/sangatis, register +1 octave region.
- `C1…C4` — charanam lines, the last containing the mudra; `C4` may carry madhyamakala (double event density, same span).
- `CS` — chittaswara (optional).
- `RET` — literal reprise of `P0` (the return), possibly with a final cadential elongation.

Each column length = `k × avartana_ticks`; `validate()` requires the terminal landmark at `section_len` in **every** row, including the drone (which must be padded/sustained, not left short).

### Encoding the rules

```text
RAGA      : allowed_pc_set, ascent_paths[], descent_paths[], vakra_paths[],
            jeeva_svaras[], nyasa_svaras[]  (phrase transitions, not a scale filter)
ARUDI     : (svara_degree, tick_offset mod avartana) — every cell's final event
EDUPPU    : e ∈ {0, +k, −k}  → cell_start_tick = section_start + e (mod avartana)
TALA      : avartana_ticks (Adi 8 aksharas, Rupaka 6, Misra Chapu 7), beat hierarchy
SANGATI   : op ∈ {RANGE(±1), DENSITY(×2), PATH(vakra_id), START_SHIFT(±1 akshara),
                  EMPHASIS(svara), REGISTER(±12)} applied to a base phrase object
INVARIANTS: lyric_id unchanged; (arudi_svara, arudi_offset) unchanged;
            cell_span unchanged; raga legal; total ticks == k·avartana
TEXT      : syllables_per_line fixed; ≥1 event per syllable;
            madhyamakala doubles EVENTS, not syllables
REGISTER  : pallavi mid, anupallavi upper, charanam mid/low, mudra close
MUDRA     : required token in final charanam cell metadata
DRONE     : invariant pitch set, spans every section exactly
```

### Base-phrase object (what the composer actually varies)

```python
base = {
  "lyric_id": "pallavi_line_1",
  "degrees":  ["S","R","G","R","S","R","G","P"],   # 8 aksharas, schematic
  "durations":[1,1,1,1,1,1,1,1],
  "arudi":    {"svara":"P", "offset": 7},          # lands on akshara 8
  "span":     8,
}
```

A sangati operator returns a new phrase that must still validate against `arudi` and `span`. In engine terms this is a pure function over the cell's event list followed by `validate()` — the human's choice of *which* operator and *how many* passes stays outside the rule system.

### Schematic sangati chain (Mohanam / Adi, 8 aksharas, samam eduppu)

Invented illustration only — not a quotation of any composition:

| Pass | Degrees | Operator | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| S0 | S R G R S R G **P** | base | lands P on akshara 8 |
| S1 | S R G P G R G **P** | RANGE↑ / PATH | touches upper P earlier |
| S2 | S R G P D P G **P** | RANGE↑ | reaches D (upper limit of Mohanam) |
| S3 | G R S R G P D **P** | START-SHIFT + vakra | begins on G, zig-zag descent |
| S4 | S R G P D P G R G P D **P** (2nd speed) | DENSITY×2 | same 8 aksharas, double events |

Every pass: same words, same final svara P, same 8-akshara span. That triplet of invariants is the whole method in one line.

### Validation checklist for the implementation

1. `composer.validate()` → True (equal-length rows, terminal landmarks).
2. No pitch outside `allowed_pc_set`; no phrase that violates ascent/descent legality.
3. Every cell's last event == `(arudi_svara, arudi_offset mod avartana)`.
4. Column lengths are exact multiples of `avartana_ticks`; madhyamakala columns keep the same length with doubled event count.
5. Drone row covers every column (no gaps at seams).
6. Export both perfect and humanized MIDI (`export_dual`) — the humanizer's micro-timing is a crude stand-in for gamaka phrasing and must not be mistaken for it.

## 6. Pitfalls and quirks

1. **Sangati ≠ ornament.** Gamaka decorates a single note; sangati restructures a whole phrase across repetitions. If the words change, it is a new line; if the arudi moves, it is niraval.
2. **Arudi vs eduppu confusion is endemic**, including in study guides that define arudi as a *starting* point. Correct usage: **eduppu/graha = start**, **arudi = landing/cadence point**. Encode them as separate fields or the model will drift.
3. **Tala is cyclic, not additive.** An anagata piece begins *inside* the cycle; if the engine's grid origin is the section start, the first cell must be padded with a rest or the cycle origin declared explicitly, otherwise every later arudi shifts.
4. **Raga ≠ scale.** A pitch-class whitelist produces bland, ungrammatical lines. The rule table must carry directed phrase paths and jeeva-svara emphasis.
5. **Gamaka is identity, not decoration.** Kampita, jaaru and oscillation on specific notes are how a raga is recognised. A pure 12-TET grid loses this; at minimum encode grace/oscillation hints as metadata, and do not claim MIDI equality with a Carnatic performance.
6. **Madhyamakala is speed within the tala**, not a tempo or metre change. If implemented as a BPM change, it must revert exactly at the next column boundary and must not alter `avartana_ticks` arithmetic.
7. **Prosody is a hard constraint.** Wrong syllable count or a caesura fighting the tala makes the line unsingable, no matter how good the pitch contour is.
8. **Lineage variance.** Sangati counts and exact variants differ by school and teacher; there is no single authoritative version. Do not "correct" a variant against a database.
9. **Mudra is not a tag you can add later** — it is set musically as the charanam's memorable close; treating it as metadata-only produces a limp ending.
10. **Sections must not all sit in one tessitura**; the register arch is what makes the form audible without harmony.
