# HC-063: Improvisation-to-Notation Composition Workflow — Capture the Gesture, Fix It on Paper

**Method ID:** HC-063
**Layer:** human (→concrete)
**Tradition:** Cross-tradition — Western Classical (fantasia & sketchbook tradition: C.P.E. Bach, Mozart, Beethoven, Chopin, Liszt) + Jazz (bebop heads from improvised lines: Parker, Rollins, Davis) + improvised-music-to-score transmission (Jarrett, transcribed improvisation), 18th c.–present
**Primary Musical Elements:** PITCH, RHYTHM, STRUCTURE, HARMONY, TEXTURE
**Status:** ✅ Documented

---

## Description

Many human composers do not start at the desk. They start at the instrument (or voice), improvise — often in private, often over a borrowed harmonic frame — and then *capture, select, refine, and fix* the best of what came out, turning a fleeting spontaneous gesture into a repeatable, teachable, performable "work." This is the **improvisation-to-notation workflow**: improvisation as the *quarry* (idea generation in real time), notation as the *cutting shed* (selection, regularization, development, and locking).

The method inverts the usual "write-then-play" model: the music is first *played*, then *written*. It is one of the oldest documented composition processes (the Baroque *stylus fantasticus* and C.P.E. Bach's *freye Fantasie*, Beethoven's improvisation-to-sketchbook habit) and simultaneously the core of modern jazz composition (bebop "heads" that are notated improvised lines, modal tunes born in the studio, authorized transcriptions of fully improvised concerts). The composer's craft here has two halves: **the ability to improvise well** (vocabulary, fluency, taste in the moment) and **the discipline to fix well** (selection, regularization, development, orchestration).

The workflow is characteristically *opportunistic*: the boundary between improvisation and composition is deliberately blurred. Parker left no working manuscripts because he composed aloud, on the bandstand and in the studio; Beethoven's sketchbooks begin as dumps of improvised material that he then reworked over years.

## Craft Procedure (Step by Step)

### Phase 0: Prepare the Improvising Frame

1. **Choose (or build) a scaffold to improvise over.** The scaffold is almost always borrowed or minimal:
   - a stock harmonic progression (the contrafact frame: Parker over "Cherokee", "How High the Moon", "I Got Rhythm" changes);
   - a modal cell of one or two chords (Davis's D-7/Eb-7 in "So What", the D Dorian cell of "Milestones");
   - a ground bass or ostinato (Baroque *chaconne* improvisation practice);
   - or a declared *free fantasy* frame (C.P.E. Bach: a key, a tempo, a mood — no fixed form).
2. **Set the instrument, register, and tempo** that the hands/voice know best. Improvisation is physical; the frame must sit under the fingers.

### Phase 1: Improvise (Generate)

3. **Play in real time**, letting learned vocabulary (scales, arpeggios, licks, ornaments, rhythmic patterns) flow through the frame. The improviser's job is *taste under time pressure*: shape phrases, vary the rhythm profile, aim for a memorable contour.
4. **Test gestures by immediate repetition.** A motif that survives being repeated (sequence, sequence-fragment, varied repeat) is a candidate for the composition. Improvisers in every tradition use repetition as an in-the-moment durability test — this is the first filter.

### Phase 2: Capture (Don't Trust Memory)

5. **Write it down or record it at once.** Different practitioners, different capture media:
   - Beethoven: after improvising (e.g., the famous Steibelt duel, where he improvised ~an hour on an upside-down borrowed theme), he jotted the ideas into sketchbooks "as soon as possible" — the *Kafka sketchbook* tradition;
   - Mozart: wrote finished-sounding pieces immediately, often while the improvisation was still hot ("as if improvised" *sprezzatura* was the aesthetic ideal, and whole genres — Fantasias — are notation of improvisation-like flow);
   - Parker: scribbled 8-bar melody parts on manuscript paper in the studio minutes before recording, transposing them mentally for his alto;
   - Davis: recorded in the studio, humming/playing into the tape; the tape *is* the capture;
   - Jarrett: the concert recording is the capture; notation came 16 years later.
6. **Parallel capture option:** use a trusted copyist or arranger who transcribes as you play (Gil Evans writing down and orchestrating Miles's improvised lines; the classical *amanuensis* tradition).

### Phase 3: Review & Select (The Quarry → Cut Stone)

7. **Listen back / read back.** Separate the keepers from the filler. Selection criteria used by real practitioners:
   - memorable contour (a distinctive leap, a registral high point, a surprising interval);
   - rhythmic identity (a rhythm profile that survives without the harmony);
   - repeatability (can it function as a head, a theme, a motif?).
8. **Discard ruthlessly.** The improvisation is raw material; most of it is discarded. A 60-second solo may yield one 8-bar head.

### Phase 4: Transcribe / Notate (Regularize)

9. **Fix pitches and rhythm to the grid.** Choose meter and barring (Parker's 8-bar fragments were notated in 4/4 with swing understood; Beethoven's sketchbook dumps lack barring and get it later).
10. **Simplify or regularize** what the hands did fluently: fills become eighth-notes, rubato cadenzas become measured or written-out fermatas, ornaments are either notated or marked as conventions.
11. **Standardize the frame**: write the chord changes (or the modal cell) explicitly under the line — this is what makes the piece playable by others.

### Phase 5: Refine & Develop (The Sketchbook Layer)

12. **Edit the captured line**: adjust a note here, re-shape the ending so it cadences, add a bridge/channel (Parker's "Ornithology" revision — he reworked an already-finished contrafact head).
13. **Extend into form**: repeat A sections, add a contrasting B, write an intro/outro. The improvisation usually gave you only the first A.
14. **Fill the texture**: what did the left hand / rhythm section do? Notate bass motion, comping voicings, or leave them to improvised practice (jazz lead sheet) — this is a human judgment call about how much to fix.

### Phase 6: Orchestrate / Arrange (Optional Final Layer)

15. **Assign voices and registers** — the captured line becomes a lead voice; add counter-voices, pads, percussion (Gil Evans orchestrating Davis's improvised lines into *Sketches of Spain*; a bebopper dictating the head to the trumpet while the rhythm section comps the borrowed changes).

### Phase 7: Lock the Work

16. **Freeze the result**: a lead sheet, a score, or an authorized transcription. The piece is now reproducible by others — but the good practitioners keep the *gesture* alive in notation: rubato, ornament, swing, "as-if-improvised" flexibility preserved as performance instruction rather than erased (Jarrett's published *Köln Concert* transcription preserves rhythmic flow and even the vocal groans; the Fantasia genre keeps its improvisatory surface in written form).

## Practitioner Examples

### Western Classical — the fantasia & sketchbook line
- **C.P.E. Bach** — the *stylus fantasticus* / *freye Fantasie*: keyboardists (and composers) improvised free fantasies at the keyboard; the written Fantasia is the notation of that improvisatory process, with unbarred passages, sudden registral shifts, and cadenza-like digressions preserved on the page. His *Versuch* (Essay on the True Art of Playing Keyboard Instruments) explicitly teaches improvisation as the root of composition.
- **Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart** — wrote down music after improvising or after hearing it once; his Fantasias (K. 475, K. 396/385g) and the piano improvisation culture of the time treat notation as the fixing of an improvisatory flow. The social ideal, *sprezzatura* — "done as if improvised" — governed how even fully written music should sound, blurring the composed/improvised line.
- **Ludwig van Beethoven** — the archetypal improvise-then-write composer. His public improvisations were legend (the Steibelt duel of 1800: he took Steibelt's string trio theme, turned it upside down, and improvised on it for over an hour, driving Steibelt from the room). He then "as soon as possible took notes to remember the ideas, themes, and motifs" he had improvised, feeding them into his sketchbooks (the *Kafka* sketchbook, the *Eroica* sketchbook) where they were refined over years into finished works. Many of his greatest themes (the Fifth Symphony opening rhythm, the *Eroica* first theme) have been traced to improvised origins.
- **Frédéric Chopin** — composed at the keyboard, improvising for hours in his salon before writing; his pieces carry the mark of improvised gesture (the *Fantaisie-Impromptu* genre name; the ornamented, rubato-laden surfaces that sound spontaneous while being minutely fixed). Liszt and Hummel belong to the same branch: written pages that preserve the sweep of keyboard improvisation.

### Jazz — the head-from-solo & studio-composition line
- **Charlie Parker** — bebop composition as notated improvisation. *KoKo* (1945): an improvised alto line over *Cherokee* changes *is* the head — the "tune" is a captured solo chorus, notated (by others and himself) and fixed as a work. *Ornithology* and *Anthropology* are contrafacts: improvisations over *How High the Moon* / *I Got Rhythm* changes, later revised (the MTO analysis documents Parker revising "Ornithology" and composing "Red Cross" / "Blues (Fast)" *in the studio*, with revisions audible on the released takes). Bassist Tommy Potter: "He could compose right on the spot... he'd write out eight bars, usually just for the trumpet... The channel of the tune could be ad libbed. The rhythm section was familiar with all the progressions." Parker left no working manuscripts — his compositions were fixed *aloud*, from improvisation to paper in minutes.
- **Miles Davis** — modal composition from studio improvisation. "So What" and "Milestones" (1958) grew from Davis playing/humming at the piano and in the studio over minimal modal cells (D-7 / Eb-7); the melody floats over a bass pedal while Red Garland comps under it. Davis's *Sketches of Spain* and *Miles Ahead* show the other half of the workflow: Gil Evans notated, arranged, and orchestrated Davis's improvised melodic lines into fixed concert works.
- **Keith Jarrett** — the extreme case: The Köln Concert (24 January 1975) was entirely improvised at the piano, recorded live; in 1991 Jarrett authorized Schott Music's *The Köln Concert: Original Transcription* — a published score of an improvisation, remarkably faithful to the live rhythmic flow and harmonies. An improvised performance became a canonized, learnable work.
- **Sonny Rollins** — heads like *Doxy* and *St. Thomas* have the quality of improvised riffs fixed into song form; the calypso/latin frames came straight from his solo practice.
- **Every jazz musician's transcription habit** — jazz pedagogy itself runs on this workflow in reverse and forward: players transcribe improvised solos into notation for study, then compose their own heads from the vocabulary learned.

### Folk / Oral line (brief)
- **Folk tune collection** — the same workflow at culture scale: a singer's/instrumentalist's spontaneous variation is captured by a collector's notation (or by other musicians' ears), fixed, and re-enters tradition as a "new" tune. Improvisation feeds the oral canon; notation (or recording) is the freeze.

## UnitMatrix Integration

The method maps onto the engine as a **two-phase pipeline over one matrix**: a provisional *capture pass* (improvised gesture seeds) followed by a *fixing pass* (regularization + realization) — both inside the zero-drift UnitMatrix discipline.

### Phase A — CAPTURE (provisional matrix)

| Voice | Role in Capture | Contents |
|-------|-----------------|----------|
| Voice 0 | **Gesture/Lead voice** | Loose "improvised seed" cells: motif units with a rhythm profile + contour skeleton (intervals defined, exact rhythm fuzzy until fixation) |
| Voice 1 | **Skeleton harmony** | Chord-symbol cells (e.g., the borrowed progression or modal cell: D-7 → Eb-7) — the frame the "improviser" plays over |
| Voice 2 | **Bass/pedal skeleton** | Root motion or pedal points (So What-style pedal under the melody) |
| Voice 3 | **Riff/comp skeleton** (optional) | Ostinato or comping pattern cell, repeated |

**Sections** = the improvised phrase slots: a form map like `A1 | A2 | B | A3` (or 12-bar blues, or 8+8+8+8). Each section gets a *gesture budget* (number of bars) and a *frame* (which skeleton chords are active). The capture pass runs a **seed-generation rule set** (below) to fill Voice 0 cells with candidate gestures.

### Phase B — FIX (final matrix, zero-drift guaranteed)

1. **Selection rule**: keep only cells passing the memorability filter (contour highlight, registral climax, distinctive rhythm profile); reject the rest (this is the composer's "discard ruthlessly" step).
2. **Quantize/regularize**: snap captured rhythms to the tick grid; choose meter + barring; every cell padded to end exactly at its section length — `validate()` zero-drift gate is the notation-freeze equivalent.
3. **Realize the skeleton**: chord cells → concrete voicings + voice-leading continuity via the pattern→event realization path (`realize_progression(..., smooth=True)`); bass cells → root/pedal lines.
4. **Fill the texture**: add the comp voice (rhythmic chord hits from the realized voicings), the bass voice, and (optional) a percussion/ride pattern voice.
5. **Section reuse**: repeated A sections share the same fixed cells by reference (reuse, not duplication) — this encodes the "repeat the head" structural move.
6. **Bridge/development**: the B section may be a *transformed* A (varied voice motive, new frame) — the refinement layer.

### Voices & Sections (final)

| Voice | Final Role |
|-------|-----------|
| Voice 0 | **Head/Lead** — the selected, fixed melody (the notated improvisation) |
| Voice 1 | **Comping** — realized chord voicings with rhythm pattern (Parker's rhythm section / Garland's comping) |
| Voice 2 | **Bass** — pedal/root motion from the frame skeleton |
| Voice 3 | **Drums/Percussion** (optional) — ride/swing or clave pattern |
| Sections | Form: `A1 A2 B A3` / 12-bar / 24-bar modal — each a fixed-length block with terminal landmark |

### Rules (as an encoded rule set)

1. **Frame rule** — Voice 1/2 cells must contain only pitches of the declared frame (borrowed progression, modal cell, or scale/ostinato pool). Nothing outside the scaffold.
2. **Seed-generation rule** — Voice 0 gesture candidates are generated from the frame's pitch pool with: contour preservation (relative interval skeleton held, exact steps varied), rhythm-profile preservation (a stored rhythm profile is reused across pitch variants), and a 2–4 bar phrase length budget. This encodes "test the gesture by varied repetition."
3. **Memorability filter** — a candidate survives selection only if it has a registral high point, a distinctive interval, or a recurring rhythm profile (or any two).
4. **Quantization/fixation rule** — captured gestures are snapped to the grid, ornament-separated, and every unit padded to its section's terminal landmark before `validate()` (zero-drift gate). No cell may over- or under-fill its section.
5. **Realization rule** — skeleton chord cells are realized to voicings with smooth voice-leading (common-tone retention, minimal motion); bass follows root/fifth or pedal per the frame type.
6. **Reuse rule** — repeated sections reference identical fixed cells (structural repetition by matrix reuse, not re-generation) — the "repeat the head" move.
7. **Style-lee rule (human choice)** — decide how much to fix vs. leave free: lead-sheet minimal (changes + melody, comping improvised by the player) vs. fully-notated score (Jarrett transcription). The matrix supports both: leave comp/bass cells as skeleton, or realize them fully.

### Quirks & Pitfalls

- **Selection is the craft.** The easy part is generating gestures; the human skill is discarding 90% of them. Automating generation without the filter produces noodling, not a head.
- **Over-fixation kills the gesture.** A fully-quantized, ornament-erased transcription loses the "as-if-improvised" quality the method exists to preserve. Real practitioners keep rubato, swing, and ornament conventions in the notation (Jarrett's transcription keeps rhythmic flow; swing is left to convention in Parker's lead sheets).
- **Contrafact copyright/identity trap** — writing over borrowed changes is standard practice, but the *melody* must stand on its own; the workflow's whole point is that the head has independent memorability.
- **The frame is load-bearing.** The scaffold (changes/modal cell) determines the head's character. A weak frame yields a weak head, no matter how good the improvisation.
- **In-studio capture is perishable.** Parker's last-minute 8-bar scribbles worked because the rhythm section knew the progressions by heart; without a playable frame, captured gestures stay orphans.
- **Different capture media, different results.** Memory-notation (Beethoven) drifts toward refinement; tape-capture (Jarrett, Davis) preserves the raw gesture; the composer's choice of capture method is itself a compositional decision.
