## Description

Kotekan is the Balinese gamelan technique of **splitting a fast melodic line between two players (or two groups of players)** so that neither part is musically complete on its own. One performer plays the **polos** ("simple" — on-beat structural tones) and the other plays the **sangsih** ("off" — off-beat elaborating tones). Together they produce a composite melody that moves at twice the speed of either individual part.

This technique sits at the **fastest layer** of gamelan's stratified texture. The gamelan orchestra is built on the concept of *melodic stratification*: a single core melody (the **pokok**) is played in unison by lower instruments at a slow tempo, while progressively higher instruments elaborate it at increasingly faster multiples. Kotekan occupies the topmost stratum — the fastest elaboration — and is the most technically demanding.

The genius of kotekan is **collaborative virtuosity**: what would be physically impossible for one player (sustaining 8–16 notes per second) becomes effortless when divided between two. The two parts interlock like gear teeth — each is incomplete alone, but together they form a continuous, rippling melodic surface.

## Craft Steps — How a Human Executes It

### Step 1: Compose the Pokok (Core Melody)
The composer (or tradition) establishes the **pokok** — the structural core melody. This is typically 2–4 notes per gong cycle, played by lower-pitched instruments (jegogan, calung, jublag). The pokok defines the harmonic/melodic skeleton; everything else elaborates it.

### Step 2: Determine Elaboration Ratio
Decide the speed multiplier for the kotekan layer. Common ratios:
- **4× the pokok** (most common in kebyar) — the composite kotekan moves at 4 notes per pokok note
- **8× the pokok** (faster, more virtuosic passages)

The composer works out the composite melody at this faster speed, ensuring it stays within the pokok's melodic trajectory.

### Step 3: Compose the Composite Kotekan Line
Write out the **full fast melody** — the complete kotekan line as a single continuous stream. This line should:
- Reference pokok tones at structurally important moments (especially on-beat positions)
- Move mostly by step or small leaps (conjunct motion typical of gangsa metallophones)
- Follow the contour of the pokok (ascending/descending shapes mirror the core melody)

### Step 4: Split Into Polos and Sangsih
Divide the composite line into two interlocking strands:
- **Polos**: takes the **even-indexed notes** (positions 0, 2, 4, 6…). These typically land on stronger rhythmic positions and often carry the more structurally important tones.
- **Sangsih**: takes the **odd-indexed notes** (positions 1, 3, 5, 7…). These fill in between polos notes, often with neighboring/passing tones.

The split can follow different patterns:
- **Kotekan nyogcag**: both parts share the same melodic contour, displaced by one note position (parallel hocket)
- **Kotekan empat**: four-note repeating cells split 2+2 between the parts
- **Kotekan telu**: three-note cells split in a specific 1+2 or 2+1 allocation

### Step 5: Assign to Instruments
Polos and sangsih are assigned to separate players or instrument pairs:
- In a Gong Kebyar ensemble: the **gangsa** (high metallophones) typically play kotekan, with four players splitting into two polos + two sangsih
- Lower instruments (ugal, pemade) may play a mid-speed elaboration (norot or following the pokok at 2×)

### Step 6: Practice Interlocking
This is the crucial human-execution step. The two parts are **practiced together** until they lock. Neither player can hear their own part as melody — they must hear the *composite*. Players learn to:
- Feel the gap between their notes as "filled" by the partner
- Lock timing so the composite sounds like a single instrument playing fast
- Breathe/phrase as a unit despite playing alternating notes

### Step 7: Layer Into Full Ensemble Texture
The kotekan is placed atop the full stratified texture:
- **Stratum 1 (slowest)**: Pokok — jegogan, jublag (1× speed)
- **Stratum 2**: Calung, ugal — mid elaboration (2× speed)
- **Stratum 3 (fastest)**: Gangsa polos + sangsih — kotekan (4× or 8× speed)
- **Reongan/kempli**: Steady rhythmic pulse underneath
- **Kendang**: Drum patterns that lead transitions and dynamics

## Kotekan Types (Summary)

| Type | Pattern Length | Split | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nyogcag** | Variable | Parallel contour, offset by 1 | Mirrored interlock — both parts trace same shape |
| **Empat** | 4 notes | 2+2 (polos=sangsih shifted) | Most common kebyar pattern |
| **Telu** | 3 notes | 1+2 or 2+1 | Used over 3-note pokok cells |
| **Norot** | Variable | Continuous neighbor-tone elaboration | Simpler, used as base or in slower sections |

## Practitioner Examples

### Wayan Beratha (1926–2014)
The most influential Balinese composer of the 20th century. Beratha composed numerous kotekan-heavy works for Gong Kebyar, including the seminal *Teruna Jaya* (revised 1960s) and *Wijaya Laksmi*. His innovations pushed kotekan to extreme speeds and created new types of interlocking patterns that became standard repertoire.

### Ketut Marya / Lotring
Early 20th-century Balinese composer associated with the development of Kebyar style. His works featured rapid kotekan exchanges that exploited the full dynamic range of the gamelan.

### Gong Kebyar Repertoire
Standard kebyar works like *Teruna Jaya*, *Gora Sekarining*, and *Tabuh Gari* demonstrate kotekan at its most virtuosic. The competitive *gong kebyar festival* tradition drives constant innovation in kotekan complexity.

### Practical Learning Context
In Bali, kotekan is learned **orally and aurally** — players memorize their part (polos or sangsih) by singing it with syllables (the tradition of *kilitan tangguh* or vocalized rhythm), then practice with a partner until the interlock is solid. Written notation (if used at all) is a reference, not a performance tool.

## Musical Elements Framework Mapping

### PITCH
- The composite kotekan line uses the **pelog** or **slendro** tuning system (non-12-TET — gamelan instruments are tuned to specific ensembles, each with unique scales)
- Polos tends to carry structurally important scale degrees (aligning with pokok tones)
- Sangsih fills with neighboring tones, passing tones, and occasional leaps
- Both parts reference the same pitch set; the interlocking creates melodic continuity, not harmonic disagreement

### RHYTHM
- Each individual part moves at **half the speed** of the composite
- Polos lands on **strong beats** (even subdivisions), sangsih on **weak beats** (odd subdivisions)
- The rhythmic offset is exactly one subdivision — the fundamental mechanic
- Grouped patterns (empat = groups of 4, telu = groups of 3) create metric ambiguity at the surface level
- Underneath: *kempli* (timekeeper) and *kendang* (drums) maintain the pulse

### HARMONY
- Gamelan harmony is **stratified rather than chordal** — vertical "harmony" emerges from the simultaneous layers (pokok + elaboration + kotekan)
- At any given moment, the vertical stack should reinforce the pokok tone or a closely related scale degree
- Dissonance is controlled by keeping elaborating tones within the scale and resolving toward pokok tones at phrase ends

### STRUCTURE
- **Gong cycles** are the primary structural unit — each cycle ends with a gong stroke
- Kotekan patterns change per gong cycle but always relate to the pokok of that cycle
- Larger form (e.g., a kebyar piece) moves through multiple tempos, keys, and kotekan densities
- Transitions between sections (*ngelabang*) often feature kotekan that accelerates or shifts pattern type

### TEXTURE
- **Stratified heterophony**: the same fundamental melody is rendered at multiple speeds simultaneously
- Texture density increases from bottom (sparse pokok) to top (dense kotekan)
- The interlocking mechanism means the top layer *requires* two voices — it is inherently polyphonic at the fastest level
- Dynamic shifts (*kebyar* = "burst/flare") create textural contrast — sudden shifts from full ensemble to solo kotekan or vice versa

## UnitMatrix Integration (Voices & Sections)

### Voice Allocation

| Voice | Instrument Role | Register | Speed | Function |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Voice 0 | Pokok (Jegogan/Jublag) | Low | 1× | Core melody — structural skeleton |
| Voice 1 | Calung/Ugal (mid elaboration) | Mid | 2× | Transitional elaboration between pokok and kotekan |
| Voice 2 | Gangsa Polos | High | 4× or 8× | Even-indexed notes of composite kotekan |
| Voice 3 | Gangsa Sangsih | High | 4× or 8× | Odd-indexed notes of composite kotekan |
| Voice 4 (opt.) | Kempli/Reong | Low-Mid | Pulse | Timekeeper / rhythmic foundation |
| Voice 5 (opt.) | Kendang | Mid | Variable | Drum patterns — transitions, dynamics |

### Section Allocation
- Each **Section** (column) = one gong cycle or phrase block
- Sections are bounded by gong strokes (the deepest structural marker)
- Within a section: pokok is constant or slowly evolving, kotekan pattern may shift

### Cell Contents

Each cell $U_{v,s}$ (voice $v$, section $s$) contains:

- **Voice 0 (Pokok)**: `{PITCH}` = 2–4 slow notes defining the core melody for this gong cycle. `{RHYTHM}` = long durations (one note per several beats). `{STRUCTURE}` = marks harmonic pillars.
- **Voice 1 (Mid elaboration)**: `{PITCH}` = norot-style neighbor tones around pokok. `{RHYTHM}` = 2× pokok speed. `{TEXTURE}` = transitional density.
- **Voice 2 (Polos)**: `{PITCH}` = even-indexed notes of composite kotekan line. `{RHYTHM}` = fast pulses at 4×/8×, on-beat. `{TEXTURE}` = interlocked with Voice 3.
- **Voice 3 (Sangsih)**: `{PITCH}` = odd-indexed notes of composite kotekan line. `{RHYTHM}` = fast pulses at 4×/8×, off-beat offset by 1 subdivision. `{TEXTURE}` = interlocked with Voice 2.

### Rules to Encode

1. **Interlock Rule**: Voices 2 and 3 together must reconstruct a continuous melodic line at 2× either individual speed. Polos note positions = even indices; sangsih = odd indices.
2. **Pokok Alignment Rule**: At strong-beat positions, polos notes must align with or neighbor the active pokok tone (Voice 0).
3. **Scale Constraint**: All voices restricted to the ensemble's pelog or slendro pitch set. If microtonal tuning is available, use it; otherwise map to nearest 12-TET approximation.
4. **Rhythmic Offset Rule**: Polos and sangsih must be offset by exactly one subdivision unit. No simultaneous attacks between them (except at phrase-end unisons).
5. **Gong Cycle Rule**: Section boundaries align with gong strokes. Kotekan pattern may change between sections but must reference the new pokok.
6. **Conjunct Motion Rule**: Composite kotekan line should move primarily by step (conjunct) with occasional small leaps. Avoid large jumps that would break the rippling character.
7. **Contour Following Rule**: Composite kotekan contour should mirror pokok contour (ascending pokok → ascending kotekan, etc.).
8. **Dynamic Burst Rule (Kebyar)**: Sudden dynamic/textural shifts between sections — transitions from full tutti to thin texture or vice versa should be sharp, not gradual.

### Mapping Flow

```
1. Define pokok melody per gong cycle (Voice 0, slow notes)
2. Choose elaboration ratio (4× or 8×)
3. Compose composite kotekan line (full fast melody following pokok contour)
4. Split composite → polos (even) → Voice 2, sangsih (odd) → Voice 3
5. Add mid elaboration (norot) → Voice 1 at 2× speed
6. Set section boundaries at gong strokes
7. Apply dynamic profile (kebyar bursts) across sections
8. Validate: polos + sangsih interlock correctly, scale constraint holds, pokok alignment holds
```

## Why It Matters for Musicom

Kotekan demonstrates a fundamentally different paradigm from Western composition:
- **Collaborative necessity**: the music literally cannot exist without two voices working together — this is the purest expression of the UnitMatrix's multi-voice design
- **Stratified texture**: shows how a single melody can generate an entire ensemble texture through speed multiplication — directly maps to the UnitMatrix's voice-row structure where each row is a different elaboration speed of the same core
- **Non-Western tuning**: forces the engine to handle non-12-TET pitch systems
- **Interlocking as structure**: the rhythmic offset between voices *is* the compositional mechanism — not a byproduct but the core idea
- **Oral/aural tradition**: the method is learned by doing, not by reading scores — reminds us that notation is a reference, not the music itself
