# HC-055: Fugue Composition (Baroque Fugal Craft — Subject/Answer/Exposition/Episodes/Stretto Architecture)

- **Layer:** human (→concrete)
- **Tradition:** Western Classical — Baroque (Sweelinck, Frescobaldi, Froberger, Buxtehude, J.S. Bach, Handel), ~1600–1750, with extensions into Classical (Mozart, Beethoven), Romantic (Mendelssohn, Schumann, Liszt, Reger) and 20th-c. neoclassical (Shostakovich, Hindemith)
- **Primary Elements:** STRUCTURE, PITCH, TEXTURE, RHYTHM
- **Craft Process (how the human does it):** Compose the subject → determine answer type (real vs tonal) → write countersubject if recurring → lay exposition voice order (S–A–S–A alternating) → write codettas bridging entries → plan episode/entrance alternation (episodes = sequence/fragment of subject, modulate) → compose middle entries in related keys → apply stretto (overlapping entries, offset decreasing) → manipulate subject (inversion, augmentation, diminution, retrograde) → arrange final entry (tonic, often stretto maestrale) → close with coda (pedal + cadence)
- **UnitMatrix Mapping:**
  - Voice 0 = Subject spine (invariant identity, piece fingerprint, stated first in all entries)
  - Voice 1 = Answer voice (dominant transposition, real or tonal, alternates with V0 entries)
  - Voice 2 = Countersubject/free counterpoint (invertible material that accompanies S/A; may be recurring or fresh per entry)
  - Voice 3 = Episodic sequence engine (fragments of subject head-motive sequenced through modulations)
  - Voice 4 = Pedal-point anchor (tonic/dominant pedal in coda and at key structural pillars)
  - **Sections:** Exposition → Episode 1 → Middle Entry 1 (related key) → Episode 2 → Middle Entry 2 (further key) → … → Stretto section → Final Entry (tonic) → Coda (pedal + cadence)
  - **Rules:** subject identity anchor, answer-type gated by subject's opening harmony, invertible counterpoint constraint (countersubject must work above AND below subject), voice-entry-order template S–A–S–A (or A–S–A–S), episodic modulation plan (closely-related keys only, circle-of-fifths bias), stretto offset decreasing monotonic, augmentation 2×/diminution ½ fraction lock, pedal-point invariance at coda, strict contrapuntal independence (no parallel 5ths/8ves, no tritone outlines, dissonance suspension resolution), voice drop-out for textural contrast, cadential convergence on tonic, final entry = tonic key
- **Real Practitioner Examples:** J.S. Bach (Well-Tempered Clavier BWV 846–893: 48 fugues; Art of Fugue BWV 1080; Musical Offering BWV 1079; organ fugues G minor BWV 542, D major BWV 532, Toccata & Fugue in D minor BWV 565), Dieterich Buxtehude (Praeludium in D minor BuxWV 140; Praeludium in F sharp minor BuxWV 146), Girolamo Frescobaldi (Fiori Musicali 1635, ricercars), G.F. Handel (Fugues in Keyboard Suites HWV 427–32; Messiah — And with His Great Mercy fugal chorus), W.A. Mozart (Fugue in C minor K. 426 for 2 pianos; Kyrie from Requiem), L. van Beethoven (Grosse Fuge Op. 133 for string quartet; Symphony No. 3 Eroica, Symphony No. 9 finale; Piano Sonata Op. 110), Felix Mendelssohn (6 Organ Sonatas Op. 65; Preludes & Fugues Op. 37), Robert Schumann (6 Fugues on B-A-C-H Op. 60), Franz Liszt (Prelude and Fugue on B-A-C-H for organ), Max Reger (Fünf leicht ausführbare Präludien und Fugen Op. 56), Dmitri Shostakovich (24 Preludes and Fugues Op. 87), Paul Hindemith (Ludus Tonalis 1942, 12 fugues each in a different key)
