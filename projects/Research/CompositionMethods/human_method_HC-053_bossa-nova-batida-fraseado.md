# HC-053: Bossa Nova Guitar Beat & Vocal Fraseado Craft (Batida + Tempo Rubato)

**Layer:** human (→concrete)
**Tradition/Culture:** Brazilian Bossa Nova — Rio de Janeiro (Zona Sul), 1958–present; pioneered by João Gilberto, Antônio Carlos Jobim, Vinicius de Moraes
**Primary Elements:** RHYTHM, HARMONY, PITCH, TEXTURE, STRUCTURE
**Status:** ✅ Documented

---

## Description

Bossa Nova ("new trend") is a Brazilian popular music style that emerged in Rio de Janeiro in the late 1950s. It is defined by a radical musical proposition: a quiet, intimate fusion of samba rhythm with jazz harmony, executed with extreme restraint and precision. The method comprises three interlocking layers: (1) the **batida** — a syncopated right-hand guitar pattern that divides the hand into an invariant thumb bass (surdo) and a syncopated chordal clave (partido alto); (2) the **vocal fraseado** — a free, speech-like melody that floats ahead of or behind the steady guitar beat (tempo rubato in the 17th/18th-century sense: free melody over steady accompaniment); and (3) a **reductionist harmonic palette** of 4-note extended chords (always with 6th or 7th coloration, never plain triads, never added 2nds) voiced in inversions for stepwise bass motion.

João Gilberto, the method's sole inventor, described his process as "refine and refine until I can get back to the simple truth." Every song he interpreted flowed through this ultra-fine stylistic sieve. The result is a polyrhythmic matrix: three independent layers (surdo bass, partido alto clave, rhythmic melody) coexist with a three-part harmonic counterpoint (bass-line, chord, voice).

## Craft Procedure (Step by Step)

### Phase 1: Fix the Batida (Guitar Foundation)
1. **Settle the thumb**: Right-hand thumb plays a bass note on **every beat** (crotchet/quarter-note), never missing a pulse. The thumb is the surdo (samba bass drum) abstraction — invariant, regular, heartbeat-like. It never plays a pickup or upbeat-note before the beat.
2. **Partition the hand**: Classical guitar technique — thumb for bass, index+middle+ring for chord block. Little finger unused.
3. **Learn the Partido Alto clave**: The three fingers play a syncopated pattern derived from the samba tamborim. The clave has two halves: syncopated (beats 1 and 4 feel) and unsyncopated (beats 2 and 3 feel). This looping phrase is the rhythmic identity.
4. **Play block chords only**: The three fingers pluck all chord notes simultaneously (block chord, never strummed). Never arpeggiated. The chord lands in the spaces between thumb beats — the syncopation.
5. **Never mute or strum**: Guitar notes sustain cleanly. No metallic fret sounds. No percussive muting.
6. **Vary pattern flow seamlessly**: Within a song, the three-finger pattern flows between several canonical variations (see Figure 1-3 in Fred Thomas), selected instinctively according to melody and text. Common variants:
   - Basic pattern (partido alto on beats 2&, 3, 4&)
   - Even quaver chords on slow tempos
   - Quaver triplet breaks at very slow tempos
   - Chord change every beat: play bass+chord simultaneously on every beat
7. **Optional chamada opening**: Begin by repeating the opening chord as even quavers (holding pattern) before sliding into the rhythmic pattern — a "waiting for the singer" trick derived from samba tamborim entry.

### Phase 2: Set the Harmonic Language
1. **Choose 4-note chords only**: No perfect major or minor triads. Every chord carries a 6th or 7th coloration. Never play a triad with an added 2nd.
2. **Invert chords for stepwise bass**: Use inversions (3rd, 5th, 7th in the bass) so the bass moves by step rather than by leap. Root notes are implied, not always present. Example: instead of Dm7→G7→Cmaj7 in root position, play Fmaj7/A → Fdim7/Ab → Em7/G (stepwise bass A→Ab→G).
3. **Use functional jazz harmony**: All chords expressible as standard chord symbols (Cm9, Dm7(9), G13, F#m7b5, etc.). Language derived from Broadway standards via jazz.
4. **Map standard Bossa progressions**: 
   - ii–V–I (Dm7–G7–Cmaj7)
   - ii–V–I with tritone substitution (Dm7–Db7–Cmaj7)
   - Chromatic mediant movements
   - Parallel descending chord forms
   - Center on tonic major or minor, with Dorian/Aeolian inflections

### Phase 3: Compose or Arrange the Melody
1. **Write/select the melody first** (Jobim's process: lyrics by de Moraes → melody by Jobim → arrangement for JG).
2. **Never change the composed melody** — João Gilberto never altered a melody note (except tiny improvised exceptions). Melody is sacred.
3. **Fix the song form**: Standard 32-bar AABA (Tin Pan Alley-derived) or 16-bar AB (verse-chorus) structure.
4. **Set the harmonic rhythm independently**: The composer may double or halve the speed of chord changes against the original. João would often rupture the formal structure by adding a beat to a given bar (momentary 2/4→3/4) to accommodate an elongated phrase.
5. **Set key**: Typically major (bright/nostalgic) or minor (melancholic). João did not transpose within a performance.

### Phase 4: Execute Vocal Fraseado (The Core Art)
1. **Remove all ornamentation**: No vibrato, no portamento (slides), no melismata (multiple notes per syllable), no dynamic swells, no register changes in vocal colour. Voice is soft, spoken quality.
2. **Use exactly the composed pitches**: Never change melodic notes. All freedom is in **timing**.
3. **Float the melody**: Use tempo rubato in the older sense (Mozart/Chopin: free melody above steady accompaniment). The voice is consistently **out of sync** with the guitar — ahead or behind by variable amounts.
4. **Create phrase displacement**: At faster speeds, shift entire melodic phrases forward or backward by a half-beat or whole beat. Example: a phrase naturally landing on beat 1 can be displaced to beat 4 or beat 2.
5. **Vary placement on repetitions**: When a song repeats (João often repeated a song multiple times per performance), each round is phrased uniquely. "Fazendo sempre a mesma coisa que nunca é a mesma" — always doing the same thing that is never the same.
6. **Alternate "in-the-pocket" vs "floating"**: Some phrases sit in the rhythmic sweet spot; others float freely. Often a verse is consistently "out" and the chorus arrives "on the money" — the resolution builds tension-release.
7. **Maintain Brazilian speech-rhythm inflection**: The melody follows the natural syncopation and cadence of Brazilian Portuguese. Pronunciation is eccentric, exaggerated, and treated as micro-sound design (nasal vowels, fuzzy consonants, elision).
8. **Sing at consistent low volume**: Voice and guitar must be in perfect proportional balance. No changes in volume.

### Phase 5: Fill the Percussion Space (Optional to Sparse)
1. **Add minimal percussion**: If percussion is used, only the hi-hat (Sonny Carr's narcotic hi-hats on João Gilberto's "white album" 1973). No drum kit, no tamborim, no surdo.
2. **Keep everything at low dynamic**: The seductive quality is in the quietness. The arrangement is built by what is **not** played.

### Phase 6: Shape the Macro-Form
1. **Play AABA or AB structure** with repeats.
2. **Speed up slow songs, slow down fast ones** — João never chose extreme tempos.
3. **Repeat as needed**: In performance, a single song might last 6+ minutes with multiple unique rounds of phrasing. Each repetition is a new improvisation of timing.
4. **Close with held final chord** (with optional 4-note extension).

## Practitioner Examples

- **João Gilberto** (inventor): *Chega de Saudade* (1958 debut single), *Desafinado*, *O Amor, O Sorriso e a Flor* (1960), *João Voz e Violão* (2000 solo album — quintessential documentation of the mature method), *Live in Montreux*, *In Tokyo* (2003). His 1958 recording of *Chega de Saudade* is the birth document of the entire method.
- **Antônio Carlos Jobim** (composer of the repertoire): *Garota de Ipanema (The Girl from Ipanema)*, *Wave*, *Dindi*, *Samba de Uma Nota Só*, *Águas de Março (Waters of March)* — each composed specifically for João's method of interpretation.
- **Stan Getz** (saxophonist, not a composer using this method but the most famous collaborator): *Getz/Gilberto* (1963 Verve album — international breakthrough, one of best-selling jazz records ever).
- **Astrud Gilberto** (vocalist on *Getz/Gilberto*): Demonstrated the vocal fraseado on "The Girl from Ipanema."
- **Newton Mendonça** (lyricist/co-composer with Jobim): *Desafinado*, *Samba de Uma Nota Só*.
- **Vinicius de Moraes** (poet/lyricist for almost all canonical bossa nova songs): *Chega de Saudade*, *Garota de Ipanema*.

## UnitMatrix Integration (Musicom Engine)

### Voices

| Voice | Name | Role | Invariant? |
|-------|------|------|------------|
| Voice 0 | **Surdo Thumb Bass** | Steady crotchet bass-note pulse; invariant spine of the piece | Yes — invariant |
| Voice 1 | **Partido Alto Clave (Chord Block)** | Syncopated 4-note chord block from the three fingers; clave pattern fills the offbeats | Fixed pattern set per song but varies between variants |
| Voice 2 | **Vocal Melody (Fraseado)** | Composed melody performed with floating tempo rubato; variable onset displacement | Pitches invariant; onsets variable (free) |
| Voice 3 | **Optional Hi-Hat Pulse** | Minimal open hi-hat on offbeats (space holder) | Optional, invariant if present |
| Voice 4 | **Harmonic Grid / Chord Progression** | Underlying chord sequence (changes per section, but frozen per song) | Frozen per composition |

### Sections

Standard 32-bar AABA form (8+8+8+8) or 16-bar AB (8+8):

| Section | Bars | Function | Harmonic Role |
|---------|------|----------|---------------|
| S0 Intro | 0–2 bars (optional) | Chamada: repeated chord quavers establishing pulse | Establishes key center |
| S1 A (Stanza A) | 8 bars | Main melody statement, tonic key | Presentation |
| S2 A (Stanza A repeat) | 8 bars | Same melody, re-phrased | Repeat (new fraseado) |
| S3 B (Bridge/Release) | 8 bars | Contrasting harmony (often subdominant or relative minor) | Harmonic contrast |
| S4 A (Return) | 8 bars | Return to main melody, tonic | Resolution |
| S5 Coda (optional) | 2–8 bars | Final held chord with 4-note extension | Closure |

### Rules Encoded

1. **Beat-invariant**: Voice 0 must fire an event on every beat (crotchet), never resting.
2. **Hand-partition constraint**: Voice 0 and Voice 1 never sound at the same rhythmic subdivision — they interlock (bass on beat, chord in offbeat space).
3. **Chord-voicing rule**: Voice 1 contains exactly 4 pitch classes per event. No perfect triads allowed. Must include 6th or 7th. No added 2nd.
4. **Inversion filter**: Bass note (Voice 0) must move stepwise where possible — reject root-position jumps > P5 unless the progression is structural.
5. **Phrase-displacement**: Voice 2 onsets may be shifted ±1–2 subdivisions from the grid reference while Voice 0 remains locked. This is the core artistic freedom.
6. **No-ornament rule**: Voice 2 pitch events are exactly the composed melody notes. No grace notes, vibrato, slides, or melismata.
7. **Melody-invariant**: Voice 2 pitches never change from the composition. Only timing varies.
8. **Volume invariance**: All voices maintain consistent dynamic level with no swells (piano only).
9. **Section form lock**: Sections follow AABA/AB with repeat structure.
10. **Zero-drift**: All voices terminate simultaneously at section boundaries. The vocal phrase may end early or late on the barline but must complete before the next section.

### Quirks & Pitfalls

- The batida is extremely difficult to quantize because the clave syncopation is micro-timed. A swung 16th feel (not triplet-based) is essential.
- Voice 0 invariancy is absolute: if the bass stops even for one beat, the groove collapses. The thumb never rests.
- The inversion system means Voice 0's pitch content may imply a different root than the chord symbol would suggest. The voice-leading is what matters, not the note names.
- Vocal fraseado cannot be notated precisely. In the UnitMatrix, it should be represented as **grid-relative offsets** (early/late markers) rather than fixed tick positions.
- The 4-note chord rule is a ceiling and a floor: fewer notes is a plain triad (forbidden), more notes exceeds the classical guitar's three-finger limit.
- Percussion is nearly absent in the pure method. Adding percussion is a departure from the strict craft (though Sonny Carr's hi-hat on the 1973 album was the masterful exception).
- João Gilberto's method is **singer-guitarist integrated** — the UnitMatrix would need to track that Voice 0 and Voice 1 are performed by a single human (right hand) and Voice 2 by the same human (voice). In a separated multi-performer realization, this interlocking is simulated rather than felt.
