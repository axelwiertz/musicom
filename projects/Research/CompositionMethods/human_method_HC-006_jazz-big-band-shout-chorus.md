## Description

The **shout chorus** (also called "the shout" or "the press") is the climactic ensemble passage in a jazz big band arrangement — the moment of maximum energy where the full band plays written material together after the solo section. It is the composer/arranger's densest craft challenge: every section (saxes, trumpets, trombones, rhythm) must contribute simultaneously without muddying. The arranger distributes a riff-based or motivic idea across 15–17 instruments, using call-and-response between sections, layered voicings, rhythmic punches, and a dynamic arc that builds from the last solo chorus to a peak before transitioning back to the final head (recapitulation).

This is a human craft rooted in **oral tradition + written notation**: early arrangers (Henderson, Ellington) built shout choruses from "head arrangements" — riffs improvised on the bandstand and memorized — then codified them into written parts. The procedure is top-down: the arranger hears the total sound mentally, sketches the lead line, then assigns and voices each section downward.

## Craft Steps — How a Human Arranger Does It

### Step 1: Establish the Riff / Motivic Seed
The arranger composes or selects a short riff (2–4 bars, often 2-beat cells) derived from the tune's melody or blues scale. This riff becomes the germinating cell for the entire shout chorus. It must be singable, rhythmically punchy, and harmonically clear.

### Step 2: Plan the Architecture (8–16 bars)
Map the shout's formal shape: typically 8 bars (blues) or 16–32 bars (AABA or through-composed). Plan where call-and-response exchanges happen, where the climax peak sits (usually 75% through), and how the shout resolves back into the head or a final cadence.

### Step 3: Write the Lead Line (Lead Trumpet)
The arranger writes the top-line melody (lead trumpet) first. This line carries the primary riff, is rhythmically propulsive (syncopation, accents on weak beats), and is registraly bright (trumpet in upper-mid to high register). The lead must "sing" — if you can't sing it, it won't work.

### Step 4: Assign Call-and-Response Blocks
Divide the shout into antecedent-consequent pairs. The arranger assigns the "call" (antecedent phrase) to one section (e.g., brass) and the "response" (consequent) to another (e.g., saxes). This creates spatial and timbral contrast within the tutti. Classic pattern: trumpets shout → saxes answer → trombones punch → full tutti.

### Step 5: Voice the Saxophone Section (Sax Soli)
If saxes play independently (soli or counterline), voice them in 5-part close harmony (lead alto on top, baritone at bottom) or spread voicing. Include inner-line motion (guide tones, 3rds and 7ths) to maintain harmonic clarity. Doubled lead (alto 1 + tenor 1 in unison) is common for projection.

### Step 6: Voice the Brass Sections
Trumpets (4–5 parts) and trombones (3–4 parts) are voiced to support the lead. Techniques include:
- **Close voicing**: tight intervals, maximum density
- **Drop-2 / Drop-4**: spread one inner voice down an octave for fuller sound
- **Stops / Kicks**: rhythm section stops while horns punch short accentuated chords on off-beats
- **Plunger mutes**: "doo-wah" effects on trumpets/trombones for tonal color
- **Bass trombone**: pedal tones as harmonic anchor (low Bb, low C)

### Step 7: Layer the Rhythm Section
The rhythm section (piano/guitar comp, bass walking 4, drums riding/comping) provides the harmonic and rhythmic foundation. During the shout, the arranger may:
- Write specific hits for the rhythm section (ensemble punches)
- Continue walking bass + ride cymbal for swing momentum
- Use "feathering" (bassist lightening touch) to let horns dominate

### Step 8: Build the Dynamic Arc
The shout must build. The arranger controls dynamics (mp → mf → ff), registral expansion (lines climb higher), textural thickening (more instruments enter), and rhythmic intensification (shorter note values, more syncopation) toward the climax. The final bar(s) of the shout resolve — either a downbeat tutti chord or a transitional figure leading back to the head.

### Step 9: Check for Clashes / Voice-Leading
The arranger plays through the full score (at piano or mentally), checking:
- No parallel fifths/octaves between adjacent brass voices (a carry-over from classical voice-leading, relaxed but still heeded)
- Lead tones (3rds, 7ths) present in each chord
- No muddy intervals in the lower-middle register (avoid 2nds and tritones in close position below middle C)
- Bass trombone not conflicting with string bass range

### Step 10: Mark Dynamics, Articulations, and Release
Final pass: mark accents (>) on syncopated hits, marcato vs. legato, crescendos, dynamics per section, and any "shout" markings (ff, "really wail"). The arranger also marks where the rhythm section drops out for a capella horn passages ("band alone").

## Practitioner Examples

### Duke Ellington — "Ko-Ko" (1940)
A prime example of Ellington's shout chorus craft. The shout features call-and-response between brass and reeds over a minor blues, with plunger-muted trumpets answering open trombone stabs. Ellington's voicing is famously unconventional — he often voiced for specific players' timbres rather than textbook harmony.

### Fletcher Henderson / Benny Goodman — "King Porter Stomp" (1935)
Henderson's arrangement built the shout chorus from simple riffs layered in call-and-response. Trumpets state a 2-bar riff → saxes answer → full band tutti. This became the template for the Swing Era shout.

### Count Basie / Sammy Nestico — "Li'l Darlin'" (1957)
Nestico's shout (though famously slow, not a "shout" in tempo) demonstrates the craft at minimal tempo: every note matters, voicings are pristine, dynamics are exactly controlled. Basie's band played shout-style ensemble passages with legendary precision.

### Thad Jones — "Mean What You Say" (1966)
Thad Jones' shout choruses are known for complex sax soli writing (fast, close-voiced unison-to-harmony passages) answered by brass punches. His voicings use wide intervals and upper-structure triads for a modern, transparent sound.

### Maria Schneider — "The Pretty Road" (2000s)
Schneider extends the shout chorus concept into through-composed orchestral jazz: sections blend seamlessly, voicings use spectral-like timbral blending, and call-and-response operates at the section-group level rather than rigid antecedent-consequent pairs.

## UnitMatrix Integration

### Voices (Rows)

| Voice | Role | Description |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Voice 0** | Rhythm Section | Bass (walking 4 / pedal), comping chords (piano/guitar), ride cymbal swing pattern. Harmonic foundation, invariant. |
| **Voice 1** | Saxophone Section | 5-part sax soli (lead alto + 2 tenors + baritone). Riffs, counter-melodies, call phrases. Close or drop-2 voicing. |
| **Voice 2** | Trumpet Section | Lead trumpet melody + 3–4 harmony parts. Bright, syncopated riffs, plunger "doo-wah" effects, top-line shout material. |
| **Voice 3** | Trombone Section | 3–4 part trombone harmony. Countermelody, low-brass punches, bass trombone pedal tones. Answers brass calls. |

### Sections (Columns)

| Section | Formal Function | Description |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **S1: Intro** | Setup | Rhythm section + sparse saxophone or trumpet lead-in |
| **S2: Head (A)** | Theme presentation | Full band states the melody |
| **S3: Solos** | Improvisation | Rhythm section + soloist; horn backgrounds (optional written pads/riffs) |
| **S4: Shout Chorus** | Climax | Peak ensemble density — call-and-response, tutti peaks, dynamic arc to ff |
| **S5: Recap (Head)** | Return | Final statement of the melody, possibly in a different key or with final tag |
| **S6: Coda / Tag** | Closure | Final cadence, ritard, vamp-and-stop or classic II–V–I tag |

### Rules Encoding the Method

1. **Riff-seed constraint**: All motivic material in the shout chorus derives from a 2–4 bar riff cell (contour + rhythm preserved through transposition/fragmentation).
2. **Call-and-response alternation**: At least one antecedent-consequent pair per shout section (Voice 2 calls → Voice 1 or 3 responds), with at least 1 bar of rest between call and response for timbral contrast.
3. **Voicing density rule**: Brass sections use close voicing at climactic peaks (maximum density), spread/drop-2 at transitional points (transparent texture).
4. **Registral stratification**: Trumpets above saxes above trombones above bass at all times. No voice crosses another's register band without deliberate effect.
5. **Rhythm section invariant**: Voice 0 maintains walking bass + ride swing pattern continuously; deviation only for ensemble hits (written punches) or stop-time passages.
6. **Dynamic arc**: Monotonic intensity increase across the shout section — dynamics progress mp → mf → f → ff, note values shorten, syncopation density increases, registral ceiling rises.
7. **Lead-tone presence**: Every chord must contain 3rd and 7th (guide tones) somewhere across the voicing stack. Absence = rule violation.
8. **Bass trombone anchor**: Voice 3's lowest note must not conflict with Voice 0's bass register (keep > P5 separation or use octave doubling).
9. **Climax placement**: Peak density and peak dynamic occur at ~75% of the shout chorus length, followed by a resolution figure (descending scale, ii–V–I, or punch-chord on downbeat).
10. **Resolution transition**: The final bar(s) of the shout must transition cleanly into the recap section — either a unison rhythmic figure leading to the downbeat of the head, or a held chord with ritard.

## Why It Matters for Musicom

The shout chorus is the **archetypal multi-voice ensemble craft**. It exercises every dimension of the UnitMatrix:

- **TEXTURE**: Layered section writing (saxes vs. brass vs. rhythm) is the core challenge — 4 voices must coexist without masking.
- **RHYTHM**: Syncopated riffs, stop-time hits, and swing-feel quantization are fundamental — not optional embellishments.
- **PITCH**: Voicing rules (close, drop-2, guide-tone presence) are directly encodable as constraints.
- **HARMONY**: The underlying progression (blues, rhythm changes, modal) drives every section simultaneously — harmonic alignment across voices is mandatory.
- **STRUCTURE**: The formal arc (intro → head → solos → shout → recap → coda) maps directly to UnitMatrix sections with clear role transitions.

The call-and-response rule (already present as method 008 in the algorithmic DB) gains a concrete human-craft specification here: how a real arranger decides which section calls, which responds, how long the gap is, and how to layer the response for maximum impact. The shout chorus is the ultimate test of "can multiple voices sound good simultaneously" — exactly what UnitMatrix validates.
