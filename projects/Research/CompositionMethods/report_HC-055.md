## 2. The Craft Procedure (Step by Step)

A human composer constructing a fugue works through the following stages. This is the canonical baroque procedure, codified by Fux (1725), Marpurg (1753), Kirnberger (1777) and practiced most famously by J.S. Bach:

### 2.1 Compose the Subject (2–8 bars)
- The subject is the fingerprint of the fugue: a short melodic-rhythmic cell with a distinctive interval skeleton and rhythm profile.
- It must have a single climax (highest or lowest note) and a clear cadential close (tonic, less commonly dominant).
- Good subjects have **strong opening interval** (unison repeated notes, ascending P4/P5, descending triad arpeggiation) so subsequent entries are audible within a complex texture.
- The subject's harmonic implications determine the answer type (see 2.2).
- Example: Bach C major BWV 846 subject = 8-note ascending triad arpeggiation in quarter + half notes → stepwise returning tail.

### 2.2 Determine Answer Type: Real vs Tonal
- **Real answer**: subject transposed literally to the dominant (exact interval transposition). Used when subject opens on tonic and stays diatonic.
- **Tonal answer**: subject altered to fit the home key when the subject opens on a prominent dominant note or modulates. Typical adjustment: the first note (dominant in subject) becomes tonic in answer; adjustments made to cadential intervals.
- Gate rule: if subject begins on tonic (or tonic arpeggiation), answer can be real. If subject begins on dominant or prominent non-tonic, answer MUST be tonal.

### 2.3 Write the Countersubject (Optional)
- Some fugues have a recurring countersubject — a fixed counter-melody that accompanies every statement of the subject or answer.
- The countersubject must be **invertible**: it must work when placed above the subject AND when placed below it (since voice roles swap).
- Test: write countersubject at the interval of invertibility (usually P8 or P12). If it creates parallel 5ths/8ves or unresolved dissonance in either orientation, revise.
- Many fugues have NO fixed countersubject — each entrance gets fresh accompanying counterpoint.

### 2.4 Lay the Exposition (Voice Entry Order)
- All voices enter one by one, alternating Subject (tonic) and Answer (dominant).
- Typical 4-voice pattern: **S–A–S–A** or **A–S–A–S** (soprano, alto, tenor, bass order varies).
- For 3 voices: **S–A–S** or **A–S–A**.
- The first voice states the subject alone (solo). The second voice enters with the answer after the subject finishes (or on the last note of the subject — overlapping permitted).
- While the answer sounds, the first voice continues with the countersubject or free counterpoint.
- **Codettas**: brief bridge passages between voice entries, built from sequenced fragments of the subject's head motive. Used to modulate back to tonic before the next entry.

### 2.5 Episode–Entrance Alternation (The Body)
After the exposition, the fugue alternates:
- **Episodes**: modulatory passages that sequence fragments of the subject (especially the head motive) through circle-of-fifths progressions. No complete subject statement. May drop voices to 2 for contrast. Often based on a sequence of the subject's head motive.
- **Middle Entries**: complete subject statements (or answer) in related keys. Typical key plan: tonic → dominant → relative major/minor → subdominant → supertonic → tonic.

### 2.6 Apply Manipulations
During middle entries, the subject can be:
- **Inversion**: mirror interval contour (ascending → descending)
- **Augmentation**: note values doubled (2×)
- **Diminution**: note values halved (½)
- **Retrograde**: subject backwards (rare in baroque, more common in 20th c.)
- **Counter-fugue**: the answer is in inversion from the start

### 2.7 Stretto
- The subject overlaps itself across voices: one voice begins the subject before the previous voice has finished.
- Offset decreases progressively (e.g., offset = 4 bars → 2 bars → 1 bar → ½ bar) = density increases.
- **Stretto maestrale** or **grand stretto**: all voices participate, each stating the complete subject (or answer) overlapping.
- Stretto sections typically appear late in the fugue, building toward the climax.

### 2.8 Final Entry + Coda
- The final entry returns to the tonic key.
- Often features stretto maestrale — all voices stating the subject in close canon.
- **Coda**: tonic or dominant pedal point (long held note in bass) + cadential figuration. Convergence to perfect authentic cadence.
- Some fugues end with a **picardy third** (major tonic in minor key).

## 3. Practitioner Examples

### Johann Sebastian Bach (1685–1750) — Apex of the Baroque Fugue
- **Well-Tempered Clavier** (BWV 846–893): 48 prelude-and-fugue pairs, one per major/minor key. Every fugue type represented (2-, 3-, 4-, 5-voice). Book 1 = 1722, Book 2 = 1742.
  - BWV 846 (C major): 4-voice, real answer, no countersubject, 2 episodes, close stretto in final bars. Perfect teaching fugue — shows the entire architecture in 27 bars.
  - BWV 847 (C minor): 3-voice, tonal answer (subject opens on G), chromatic stretto.
  - BWV 861 (G minor): 4-voice, tonal answer, two countersubjects, invertible counterpoint.
  - BWV 865 (A minor): 4-voice, double fugue (two subjects presented simultaneously).
- **Art of Fugue** (BWV 1080, 1742–1749): 14 contrapuncti + 4 canons, a systematic encyclopedia of fugal devices. Contrapunctus I = simple 4-voice fugue; Contrapunctus V–VII = counter-fugues (inversion); Contrapunctus IX–XI = triple fugues (3 subjects).
- **Musical Offering** (BWV 1079, 1747): Ricercar a 6 — monumental 6-voice fugue on Frederick the Great's theme. Canon perpetuus (endless canon) at the conclusion.
- **Organ Fugues**: G minor BWV 542 (5-voice, powerful pedal subject), D major BWV 532 (large-scale prelude + fugue, virtuosic pedal), Toccata & Fugue in D minor BWV 565 (famous opening, though authenticity debated).

### Dieterich Buxtehude (c. 1637–1707)
- North German organ praeludia: free-formed structures with multiple fugal sections (each with its own subject).
- **Praeludium in D minor BuxWV 140**: features 3 related fugal subjects across a single movement, connected by free fantasia sections.
- **Praeludium in F♯ minor BuxWV 146**: chromatic subject, dense stretto.

### Girolamo Frescobaldi (1583–1643)
- **Fiori Musicali** (1635): ricercars and canzonas — direct ancestors of the fugue. Each ricercar is a strict fugue on a single subject, in white-note values (breves/semibreves).

### G.F. Handel (1685–1759)
- Keyboard fugues in the suites (HWV 427–432): hand-friendly, galant-inflected.
- **Messiah**: choral fugues like "And with His Great Mercy" (No. 16) and the "Amen" fugue.

### W.A. Mozart (1756–1791)
- **Fugue in C minor K. 426** (1783): for 2 pianos, strict fugal exposition.
- **Requiem** (KV 626): Kyrie — double fugue (choral subject + orchestral countersubject).

### L. van Beethoven (1770–1827)
- **Grosse Fuge Op. 133** (1826): Große Fuge for string quartet in B♭ major — the most ambitious fugue between Bach and the 20th century.
- **Symphony No. 3 "Eroica"** (1803): fugal development section (subject treated contrapuntally in the development of movement I).
- **Symphony No. 9** (1824): finale — "Ode to Joy" theme treated fugally.

### Felix Mendelssohn (1809–1847)
- **Preludes & Fugues Op. 37** (organ): 3 pairs.
- **Six Organ Sonatas Op. 65** (1845): each contains fugal movements.

### Robert Schumann (1810–1856)
- **6 Fugues on B-A-C-H Op. 60** (1845): organ, chromatic subjects.

### Franz Liszt (1811–1886)
- **Prelude and Fugue on B-A-C-H**: organ, large-scale romantic fugue with chromatic subject.

### Dmitri Shostakovich (1906–1975)
- **24 Preludes and Fugues Op. 87** (1950–51): one prelude-fugue pair in each major/minor key, traversing the cycle of fifths. Neoclassical fugal craft infused with 20th-century harmony (polytonal episodes, extreme chromaticism).

### Paul Hindemith (1895–1963)
- **Ludus Tonalis** (1942): 12 fugues, each in a different key, framed by preludes and interludes. A 20th-century Art of Fugue.

## 4. UnitMatrix Mapping

### Voices

| Voice | Role | Constraint |
|---|---|---|
| **Voice 0 — Subject spine** | Invariant identity anchor. Piece fingerprint. States the subject first in each entry group. Never mutated beyond recognition (pitch skeleton + rhythm profile preserved). | subject identity anchor |
| **Voice 1 — Answer voice** | States the subject transposed to the dominant (or subdominant). May be real (exact transposition) or tonal (adjusted intervals). Alternates with V0 in exposition. | answer-type gate real/tonal |
| **Voice 2 — Countersubject / free counterpoint** | Invertible recurring material (or fresh per entry). Must work above AND below V0. If no fixed CS, this voice provides free contrapuntal filler. | invertible counterpoint constraint |
| **Voice 3 — Episodic sequence engine** | Head-motive fragments sequenced through modulations (circle-of-fifths, secondary dominants). Cadential reaches. Thins to 2 voices for contrast. | modulation plan |
| **Voice 4 — Pedal-point anchor** | Long-held tonic or dominant note in coda and at structural pillars (before final entry). Invariant during pedal. | pedal-point invariance |

### Sections (Formal Architecture)

| Section | Function |
|---|---|
| **S0 — Exposition** | All voices enter S–A–S–A (or variant). No modulation beyond tonic→dominant→tonic. Subject stated once per voice. |
| **S1 — Episode 1** | No complete subject. Sequences head-motive fragments. Modulates to related key (e.g., dominant → relative major). Voices drop to 2–3 for contrast. |
| **S2 — Middle Entry 1** | Subject in a related key (e.g., dominant or relative major). May use inversion/stretto. Full texture returns. |
| **S3 — Episode 2** | Fresh sequence material. Modulates further (e.g., relative → subdominant). May use voice exchange. |
| **S4 — Middle Entry 2** | Subject in another related key (e.g., subdominant or supertonic). Augmentation or diminution possible. |
| **S5 — Episode 3** | Thinnest texture (2 voices). Accelerating harmonic rhythm. Approaches tonic. |
| **S6 — Stretto Section** | Overlapping subject entries, offset decreasing (4 bars → 2 bars → 1 bar). Maximum density. All voices active. |
| **S7 — Final Entry** | Subject returns to tonic. Often stretto maestrale (all voices, complete subject overlapping). |
| **S8 — Coda** | Pedal point (tonic or dominant). Cadential figuration. Perfect cadence convergence. Optional picardy third. |

### Rules Summary

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| Subject identity anchor | Subject never mutated beyond recognition; pitch skeleton + rhythm profile preserved across all transformations |
| Answer-type gate | Real if subject opens on tonic/tonic triad; tonal if subject opens on dominant or prominent non-tonic note |
| Invertible counterpoint constraint | Countersubject valid at P8 or P12 above AND below subject; test both orientations before accepting |
| Voice-entry alternation | S–A–S–A for 4 voices, A–S–A–S also valid; never S–S–A–A (must alternate every entry) |
| Episodic modulation plan | Only closely-related keys (max 1 accidental difference, circle-of-fifths bias); no remote modulation |
| Stretto-offset decreasing monotonic | Offset between overlapping entries (bars) must be ≤ previous offset; never increase |
| Augmentation 2×/diminution ½ | Fraction lock: augmentation = exactly 2× values, diminution = exactly ½ values |
| Pedal-point invariance | Pedal voice plays exactly one pitch (tonic or dominant) with rhythmic zero-activity; cannot move |
| No parallel 5ths/8ves | Strict contrapuntal independence in all voices at all times |
| No tritone melodic outlines | Augmented 4th/diminished 5th cannot be outlined as a melodic leap |
| Dissonance suspension | Dissonance prepared as consonance on weak beat, resolved by step down on weak beat |
| Voice drop-out | Max 2 consecutive voices can drop (for textural contrast in episodes); never drop all 3 of 4 |
| Cadential convergence to tonic | Every section ends on tonic (or dominant preparing tonic); no section ends on non-tonic |
| No functional harmony requirement | Counterpoint determines chord simultaneities; chord syntax is emergent, not pre-composed |

## 7. Quirks & Pitfalls

1. **Real vs Tonal confusion**: Beginners often write tonal answers when real would work, or vice versa. Rule: if subject opens on tonic, answer can be real; if subject opens on non-tonic (especially dominant), answer MUST be tonal. Test by singing the answer back against the original — if it sounds like a different key, adjust.

2. **Countersubject invertibility failure**: A countersubject that inverts into parallel 5ths or unresolved dissonance is invalid. Always test the invertible pair by writing it out in the opposite orientation before committing.

3. **Stretto offset greed**: Students try stretto too early or with too-short offset, creating muddiness. Start stretto with wide offset (≥½ subject length), tighten gradually.

4. **Episode over-modulation**: Baroque fugues modulate only to closely-related keys (max 1 accidental difference). Remote modulations break the style.

5. **Missing cadences**: Every section should end with a recognizable cadence (PAC preferred). Episode endings, especially, need clear cadential arrival before the next entry.

6. **Voice independence degrades**: When writing for keyboard, the two hands can accidentally produce parallel 5ths/8ves between inner voices. Verify pairwise for ≥3 voices.

7. **Subject too long**: A subject longer than 8 bars makes exposition unwieldy and stretto difficult. Bach's subjects average 4–6 bars.

8. **No pedal at coda**: The coda pedal is not optional in Baroque practice — it's the structural glue that confirms the final tonic return. Omit it only in very short fugues.

9. **20th-century exceptions**: Shostakovich and Hindemith freely break all Baroque rules (polytonal episodes, tritone subjects, non-functional harmony). These are valid as neoclassical fugues but must be labelled as such — they do not belong in the Baroque craft category.
