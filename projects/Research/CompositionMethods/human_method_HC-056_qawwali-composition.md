# HC-056: Qawwali Composition — Sufi Devotional Song-Craft (Sama / Qaul / Takrar / Wajd Architecture)

- **Layer:** human (→concrete)
- **Tradition:** South Asian Sufi Devotional — Qawwali of the Chishti Sufi order (Delhi/Ajmer/Pakistan/India/Bangladesh), ~13th c.–present; UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage; roots in Chishti sama gatherings at dargah shrines

## Description

Qawwali is the hereditary devotional song-craft of South Asian Sufi singers (qawwals), transmitted through oral lineages (qawwal bacche) from father to son for seven centuries. A qawwali composition is **text-led**: the poetic text (kalam) determines the raga choice, the rhythmic cycle (tala/theka), the form, and the emotional arc. The qawwal's craft lies not in composing original notes but in **selecting, ordering, and delivering** the text with improvised melodic elaboration, call-and-response intensification (takrar), and gradual rhythmic acceleration that drives both singer and listener toward wajd (spiritual ecstasy).

Unlike Hindustani classical khayal, qawwali is **congregational** — the lead soloist works with a chorus (humnawan or mawwal), harmonium, dholak/tabla, and rhythmic handclaps. The form is a controlled escalation: slow instrumental prelude → refrain statement → verse couplets → repeated refrain escalation → climax → close.

## Craft Procedure (Step-by-Step)

### 1. Fix Performance Context & Text (Kalam Selection)
The qawwal chooses the poetic genre *before* any musical decisions:
- **Hamd** — praise of Allah (opens the mehfil)
- **Naat** — praise of the Prophet Muhammad
- **Manqabat** — praise of Sufi saints (Ali, Nizamuddin Auliya, Moinuddin Chishti)
- **Ghazal** — love/divine longing (sufi subtext, often by Rumi, Hafez, Ghalib, Mir)
- **Kafi** — Punjabi/Seraiki folk-mystic poetry (Bulleh Shah, Shah Hussain, Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai)
- **Qaul** — ritual opening hymn based on sayings of the Prophet (obligatory)
- **Rang** — second ritual hymn celebrating Khusrau's devotion to Nizamuddin Auliya

The text is memorized; qawwals learn hundreds of kalam from their hereditary repertoire.

### 2. Choose Raga (Melodic Mode)
Select a raga that matches the text's emotion (rasa):
- **Bhairavi** (morning, solemn devotion — most common qawwali raga)
- **Kafi** (mellow, romantic longing)
- **Yaman** (evening, majestic praise)
- **Pilu** (light, folk-tinged)
- **Darbari** (deep, serious gravity)
- **Pahadi** (folk mountain flavor)
- **Jhinjhoti** (sweet, romantic)

The raga dictates the allowable notes (swaras), the characteristic phrase (pakad/chalan), and the vadi/samvadi (emphasized notes). The harmonium (or earlier sarangi) provides both melody and drone.

### 3. Fix Tala (Rhythmic Cycle)
Choose the rhythmic cycle that fits the text metre and performance tempo:
- **Keherwa** (8 beats: 4+4, most common qawwali tala)
- **Dadra** (6 beats: 3+3, light/gentle)
- **Rupak** (7 beats: 3+2+2, flowing)
- **Teentaal** (16 beats: 4+4+4+4, for fast climax sections)
- **Jhaptal** (10 beats: 2+3+2+3)

Handclaps (tali) mark the tala cycle — a defining qawwali texture that keeps the entire ensemble locked. The dholak/tabla plays the theka (basic rhythm skeleton).

### 4. Compose the Alap/Naghma (Instrumental Prelude)
The harmonium player (or the qawwal themselves) opens with:
- **Alap** — slow, unmetered introduction of the raga's notes, one swara at a time, establishing the melodic space
- **Naghma** — short composed instrumental tune often based on *Zikr Allahu* (chanting of God's name in three phrases), played by harmonium solo

Duration: ~1–3 minutes, free rhythm.

### 5. State the Mukhda (Refrain / Chorus Hook)
The lead qawwal sings the **mukhda** — the recurring refrain line of the qaul or song:
- Sung in full by the lead
- Immediately echoed by the chorus (humnawan) in unison
- Sets the melodic-rhythmic template for the entire composition
- Usually a short, memorable phrase with a clear cadence on the tonic (sa)

### 6. Alternate Sher (Verses) with Bol (Chorus Response) — Call-and-Response
For each verse couplet (sher):
1. Lead sings the first misra (verse line) — free-rhythm or lightly pulsed, melismatic, ornamented with meend (glides) and murki (grace notes)
2. Lead sings the second misra — may take it higher or add melodic variation
3. **Chorus rejoins** with the bol (the fixed repeated response — often the mukhda or a portion of it)
4. Handclaps and dholak settle into the tala cycle

This is the core qawwali alternation pattern: **sher → bol → sher → bol**.
A girah (knot) may be inserted: a couplet from a different poet that fits the same theme.

### 7. Apply Takrar (Repetitive Intensification)
When a couplet strikes emotional resonance (the audience shows wajd signs):
- The qawwal repeats the couplet multiple times
- Each repetition: faster tempo, higher register, denser harmonium chords, louder handclaps
- Takrar can last 5–15 minutes, building in a controlled crescendo
- The qawwal adds **sargam** (solfege taans at breakneck speed, the Nusrat hallmark)
- Side singers may sing **taans** in parallel (layered)

### 8. Orchestrate the Intensity Arc (Sama Architecture)
The qawwali follows a monotonic energy curve — it never relaxes back down:
| Phase | Texture | Handclaps | Tempo |
|-------|---------|-----------|-------|
| Alap/Naghma | Harmonium solo, free rhythm | None | Static (slow) |
| Mukhda statement | Lead + chorus unison | Sparse | Jor (pulsed) |
| Sher 1–2 | Lead solo + chorus bol | Keherwa, steady | Moderate (70–90 bpm) |
| Sher 3–4 + girah | Increased ornament, sargam | Accented claps | Medium (90–110) |
| Takrar build | Repeated couplet, rising pitch | Faster denser claps | Fast (110–130) |
| Climax/Wajd | Maximum density — lead + chorus + harmonium + all clapping in sync | Loudest, fastest | Peak |
| Close (Qaul/Qaul return) | Return to mukhda, decelerando | Taper | Settle |

### 9. Close
The qawwal signals closure by:
- Returning to the mukhda (home refrain)
- Decelerating the tempo (the dholak/tabla slow to a stop)
- Singing a final couplet that resolves to the tonic
- Optional: ending the session with a **du'a** (prayer) or a **rang** (closing hymn)

## Practitioner Examples

### Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan (1948–1997)
The most recorded qawwal in history. Signature techniques:
- **Sargam taans** at extreme speed — solfege runs over multiple simultaneous rhythmic cycles
- **Takrar mastery** — could repeat a single couplet for 20+ minutes, each cycle more intense
- **Raga breadth** — sang in Bhairavi, Kafi, Yaman, Darbari, Pilu, Jhinjhoti, Pahadi and more
- Key works: "Allah Hoo," "Tum Ek Gorakh Dhanda Ho," "Mast Nazron Se Allah Bachaye," "Dil Haram Ho Gaya," "Mustt Mustt" (collaboration with Michael Brook, Real World 1990)

### Sabri Brothers (Ghulam Farid Sabri, Maqbool Ahmed Sabri, Amjad Sabri)
Known for powerful, raw vocal delivery and long takrar sections. Their "Hamd-o-Naat" repertoire set the standard. Key works: "Saqi Nama," "Tajdar-e-Haram," "Meri Maut Ka Aalam."

### Aziz Mian (1942–1989)
Known for his deep, grainy voice and extraordinarily long qawwalis (over 60 minutes). His style was more contemplative, with extended alap sections. Key works: "Allah Hi Allah," "Hashr Mein," "Jahan Jahan Mera Noor-e-Nabi."

### Amir Khusrau (1253–1325)
The "Father of Qawwali" who synthesized Persian, Arabic, Turkic, and Hindustani elements. Credited with inventing the qaul, rang, and tarana forms. His poetry in Hindavi (early Urdu/Hindi) and Persian remains the core qawwali repertoire.

### Other notable practitioners:
- **Rahat Fateh Ali Khan** (nephew of Nusrat, carries the lineage forward)
- **Rizwan-Muazzam Qawwali** (Real World artists, contemporary presentation)
- **Fareed Ayaz & Abu Muhammad** (known for classical khayal-inflected qawwali)
- **Badar Miandad** (solo qawwal with lighter, pop-influenced style)
- **Qawwal Bahauddin Qutbuddin** (Ajmer Sharif shrine hereditary qawwals)

## UnitMatrix Mapping

### Voices

| Voice | Role | Invariant Property |
|-------|------|-------------------|
| **Voice 0** | **Harmonium drone/pedal** (sa-pa) + **key mukhda melody** — the spine. Never stops sounding; provides the raga's tonal anchor. | Invariant drone (sa or sa-pa) sustain. Note set constrained to raga swaras. |
| **Voice 1** | **Lead qawwal vocal** (alap → mukhda → sher → takrar → wala walo taans). Carries the primary melodic line and text. | Freedom to ornament, improvise, repeat. Pitches ∈ raga set. Rhythmic density increases monotonically from free-rhythm to densest taans. |
| **Voice 2** | **Chorus (humnawan/Mawwal) vocal response** — echoes the mukhda/bol. Never overlaps Voice 1's couplet. Provides the call-response gap-fill. | Fixed-response text invariant. Onsets occur in Voice 1's rest spaces. Uniform rhythm (handclap-locked). |
| **Voice 3** | **Harmonium chord/sargam layer** — the qawwal plays harmonium alongside singing (right-hand melody, left-hand chords). Additional harmonium side-player fills with chords. | Chord types: tonic, dominant, subdominant (bhajani ang). RH melody doubles lead vocal with octave/ornament offset. |
| **Voice 4** | **Dholak/tabla percussion** — plays theka (rhythm skeleton) for the chosen tala. Tempo accelerates monotonically through performance. | Tala pattern invariant across sher/bol alternation. Tempo ramp: starts ~70 bpm → climax ~130+ bpm. |
| **Voice 5** | **Handclap grid (tali)** — all members clap. Marks the tala cycle's accented beats. The loudest, most energetic layer. | Clap pattern follows tala structure (sam and tali beats). Intensity crescendos with takrar. |
| **Voice 6** | **Lead vocal taan/sargam layer** (optional, active only in climax section). Fast solfege runs. High-register, rhythmic density max. | Only active during takrar/climax. No text — only sargam (Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni) syllables. Pitch runs traverse the raga's upper register. |
| **Voice 7** | **Audience/participant response** (optional layer in live performance). Clapping, vocal affirmations ("Subhanallah," "Wah Wah"), wajd gestures. | Sparse, unpredictable. Only in live context. |

### Sections

| Section | Function | Voice Activity Mask | Duration (approx) |
|---------|----------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **S0: Naghma/Alap** | Instrumental prelude. Harmonium solo establishes raga. No text, no percussion, no handclaps. | V0 + V3 only (harmonium). Free rhythm. | 1–3 min |
| **S1: Qaul/Mukhda Statement** | Lead states the opening hymn/refrain. Chorus echoes. Dholak/handclaps enter with tala cycle. | V0,V1,V2,V4,V5 active. V3 fills. | 2–4 min |
| **S2: Sher 1–2 (Verse couplets)** | Lead sings first verse lines (sher alternation). Chorus returns mukhda bol between each misra. | V0,V1,V2,V4,V5. V1 lead solo during couplet; V2 fills gap. | 5–10 min |
| **S3: Sher 3–4 + Girah (insert)** | More verses, optional girah (borrowed couplet from another poet). Increased ornamentation. | Same as S2. V1 density increasing. V3 harmonium chords more active. | 5–10 min |
| **S4: Takrar Build (Intensification)** | Qawwal selects a key couplet and repeats it. Each cycle is faster, louder, higher register. Sargam taans enter. | V0–V6 all active. V6 (taan layer) ACTIVE HERE. V5 handclaps loudest. Tempo accelerates 70→110+ bpm. | 5–15 min |
| **S5: Climax / Wajd** | Maximum density. Lead at highest register, fastest taans, densest harmonium, handclaps at full force. Audience wajd peaks. | ALL voices active at max intensity. | 1–3 min |
| **S6: Settle/Close** | Return to mukhda. Decelerando. Dholak slows. Lead sings final couplet, resolves to tonic. Optional du'a. | V0,V1,V2 tacet except mukhda. V4 decelerates. V5 tapers. | 1–3 min |

### Rules

| # | Rule | Description | Enforces |
|---|------|-------------|---------|
| 1 | **Raga pitch-set constraint** | All melodic pitches ∈ chosen raga's allowable swaras. Harmonium drone signals sa-pa. | Purity of melodic mode. |
| 2 | **Tala cycle invariance** | The chosen tala's theka pattern (e.g., Keherwa's dha-ge-na-ti-ke-dhi-na-na) repeats cyclically. Vocal/couplet phrases resolve on sam (beat 1). | Rhythmic coherence across ensemble. |
| 3 | **Call-response gap-fill (non-overlap)** | The chorus (V2) may ONLY sound in the gaps between Voice 1's couplet lines. No overlap with lead's sung text. | Clear text intelligibility; participatory form. |
| 4 | **Monotonic tempo ramp** | Tempo never decreases from S0→S5. Strict monotonic increase per section boundary. | Sama energy architecture — one-directional intensification. |
| 5 | **Takrar density escalation** | Each takrar cycle must be at least as dense as the previous: more repetitions, higher register, faster subdivisions, louder dynamics. | Controlled wajd trajectory. |
| 6 | **Mukhda identity invariance** | The mukhda (refrain) returns identically after every verse couplet. Its melody and text are frozen for the piece. | Recognizable anchor; chorus can sing without cue. |
| 7 | **Taans only in takrar/climax** | Sargam solfege runs (V6) restricted to S4–S5. Never in earlier sections. | Preserve the gradual intensification arc. |
| 8 | **Text-led priority** | No purely instrumental section longer than 30s during S2–S5. The text is the primary carrier of meaning; harmonium serves the voice. | Qawwali is devotional poetry, not instrumental display. |
| 9 | **Vocal register ascent** | Each successive sher/takrar cycle may ascend: start in mandra (lower octave), move to madhya (middle), climax in tar (upper octave). | Wajd trajectory = upward pull. |
| 10 | **Lead-chorus alternation lock** | Every vocal section alternates strictly: lead solo line → chorus response → lead solo → chorus response. No two consecutive lead lines without a chorus interjection. | Congregational participatory structure. |
| 11 | **Climax convergence** | S5 (Wajd climax) must coincide with the highest register note of the piece, the fastest tempo, and the maximum handclap density. All voices converge on a unified pulse. | Maximum energy at architectural peak. |
| 12 | **Close-deceleration** | S6's tempo decelerates from climax to ~60% of peak over 16–32 tala cycles. Final note is the tonic (sa). | Resolution; return from ecstasy to ordinary consciousness. |
