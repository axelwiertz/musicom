## Description

The Ethiopian Qañat (kiñit/የኢትዮጵያ ቅኝት) modal system is a pentatonic framework of four principal modes — Tizita (ትዝታ), Bati (ባቲ), Ambassel (አምባሳል), and Anchihoye (አንቺሆዬ) — each with distinct interval structure, emotional register, and melodic contour. These modes are the foundation of Ethiopian Highlander secular music, realized predominantly by the **azmari** (አዝማሪ), a poet-musician comparable to the West African griot or European medieval minstrel.

The azmari composes and performs extemporized verses — sung while accompanying themselves on the **masenqo** (one-string bowed lute), **krar** (5–6 string bowl lyre), or **washint** (bamboo flute) — using the **sem-ena-werq** ("wax and gold"/ሰም ና ወርቅ) double-entendre poetic technique. The melody is always in one of the four kiñit modes, with microtonal inflections (neutral 2nds/3rds, quarter-tone bends) characteristic of traditional non-tempered performance. The rhythmic feel is asymmetrical, often in lilting 6/8 or additive meters, reinforced by **kebero** drum and handclaps.

## The Four Principal Kiñit Modes (pitch sets in C relative)

Each mode has a Major and Minor variant (or characteristic tuning), and each carries a distinct emotional/social register:

### Tizita (ትዝታ) — "Nostalgia / Reminiscence"
- **Emotional register**: Melancholic, nostalgic (song of remembrance)
- **Tizita Major** (C–D–E–G–A): W–W–m3–W–m3 — identical to major pentatonic
- **Tizita Minor** (C–D–E♭–G–A♭): W–H–m3–H–M3 — degrees 1, 2, ♭3, 4, ♭5; a distinctive "blue" pentatonic
- **Contour**: Descending tendency, narrow tesitura, phrase endings fall to tonic
- Most common mode for lyrical/ballad expressions (the *tizita* ballad form is itself a named genre)

### Bati (ባቲ) — "Lively / Dance"
- **Emotional register**: Energetic, celebratory, joyful
- **Bati Major** (C–E–F–G–B): M3–H–W–M3–H — degrees 1, 3, 4, 5, 7
- **Bati Minor** (C–E♭–F–G–B♭): W–W–W–H–W — 1, ♭2, ♭3... wait let me re-check
  Actually: Bati Minor (C–E♭–F–G–B♭): m3–W–W–m3–W = 1, ♭3, 4, 5, ♭7 — degrees 1, ♭2, 3, 4, ♭5 if built stepwise: C, D♭, E, F, G♭
- Often used for dance tunes and festive songs; features wide leaps and faster tempo

Let me be precise with the interval structures from the published sources:

**Tizita Major**: C–D–E–G–A (steps: M2–M2–m3–M2–m3) = major pentatonic {0, 2, 4, 7, 9}
**Tizita Minor**: C–D–E♭–G–A♭ (steps: M2–m2–M3–m2–M3) = {0, 2, 3, 7, 8} — degrees 1–2–♭3–4–♭5
**Bati Major**: C–E–F–G–B (steps: M3–m2–M2–M3–m2) = {0, 4, 5, 7, 11} — degrees 1–3–4–5–7
**Bati Minor**: C–E♭–F–G–B♭ (steps: m3–M2–M2–m3–M2) = {0, 3, 5, 7, 10} — degrees 1–♭3–4–5–♭7
**Ambassel**: C–D♭–F–G–A♭ (steps: m2–M3–M2–m2–M3) = {0, 1, 5, 7, 8} — degrees 1–♭2–4–5–♭6
**Ambassel Type 2** (Mixolydian pentatonic): C–D–F–G–A (steps: M2–m3–M2–M2–m3) = {0, 2, 5, 7, 9}
**Anchihoye**: C–D♭–F–G♭–A (steps: m2–M3–m2–M3–m2) = {0, 1, 5, 6, 9} — degrees 1–♭2–4–♭5–6

All four are modes of the pentatonic scale (they are rotations of the C–D–E–G–A major pentatonic set, except Tizita Minor and Anchihoye which introduce ♭5/♭6 modifications).

### Microtonal deviations

When performed on traditional instruments (krar, masenqo, washint), the pitches are NOT tempered:
- Neutral 2nds (~150¢) and neutral 3rds (~350¢) may appear
- The tritone (♭5) in Tizita Minor is often sung as a quarter-tone between ♮4 and ♭5
- The pitch set is flexible by region (Gojjam, Wello, Shewa each shade differently)
- The **ambassel** mode particularly features a "compressed" major 2nd at the top of its tetrachord

## Craft Procedure: How an Azmari Actually Composes and Performs

The azmari tradition is **oral, improvisatory, and social** — there is no notation. The craft is transmitted by apprenticeship (father-to-son, master-to-student) within tejbet (honey-wine houses) and community gatherings.

### Step 1: Internalise the kiñit repertoire by ear
The apprentice azmari absorbs the four principal modes (and their regional variants) through years of listening and imitating elder azmaris. They learn:
- Which kiñit evokes which emotional atmosphere (Tizita = nostalgic/sad, Bati = festive/lively)
- The characteristic melodic motifs (short 2–4 note cells) associated with each mode
- The "correct" microtonal inflections (where neutral 2nds/3rds fall, where the bend should occur)

### Step 2: Tune the instrument to the chosen kiñit
- **Masenqo** (single-string fiddle): The single horsehair string is tuned by turning the peg against the resonator. Relative tuning shifts the available pentatonic pitches. The bow tension and left-hand finger placement (no fret) produce the microtonal shadings.
- **Krar** (5–6 string lyre): Tuned to the pentatonic set of the chosen mode. Open strings provide the drone/pedal. The krar's bowl resonator and goat-skin head produce a buzzing timbre.
- **Washint** (flute): finger holes produce the pentatonic set; the player bends pitches by half-covering or embouchure adjustment.

### Step 3: Choose the emotional/social register for the occasion
The azmari reads the room (tejbet patrons, wedding party, celebration, social event) and selects:
- **Tizita**: for a wistful, nostalgic opener — song of reminiscence, often about lost love, homeland, or the past
- **Bati**: for dance, celebration, wedding festivities — faster tempo, energetic
- **Ambassel**: mixed character, can be gentle or ceremonial depending on tempo
- **Anchihoye**: dramatic, solemn, often used for sharp social commentary or satire

### Step 4: Open with instrumental vamp + melodic cell
The azmari begins by playing the kiñit's characteristic **motif/vamp** — a short 2–4 bar pentatonic riff establishing the mode, the pitch center, and the rhythmic feel. This serves as the **reference cell** (the "wax"/sem of the musical form):
- Masenqo: sustained bowed drone on tonic + short melodic figure
- Krar: plucked ostinato pattern with open-string drone
- The vamp is repeated and varied, giving the azmari time to improvise the lyrics

### Step 5: Compose the first verse extemporaneously
The azmari sings the first **sem-ena-werq** ("wax and gold") line:
- **Sem (wax)**: The surface meaning — a seemingly innocent line about a flower, a bird, a landscape
- **Werq (gold)**: The hidden meaning — a political critique, a personal insult, a coded love confession, social commentary
- The vocal melody follows the pentatonic contour of the mode
- Melismatic ornamentation: slides, trills, breathy attacks, microtonal bends on key syllables
- Phrasing is asymmetrical (follows the Amharic poetic meter, not a fixed bar grid)
- The azmari targets the **key pitches** of the mode (the tonic/tezeta anbessa and the characteristic degree)

### Step 6: Alternate instrumental fill and sung verse
Structure per verse:
1. Sung verse (8–16 syllables, one poetic line, melismatic)
2. Instrumental fill (krar/masenqo responds to the vocal phrase, echoing its contour or playing the vamp riff)
3. Sung verse again (next poetic line)
4. Handclaps + audience response (ululation, cheering, shouted praise)

This call-and-response continues through the song. The azmari varies the melody with each verse (ornamentation increases, register may climb).

### Step 7: Intensify through the performance arc
As the performance progresses:
- Tempo may increase (Bati mode especially drives to faster dance tempos)
- Ornamentation thickens (more slides, faster melodic runs)
- The vocal register pushes higher (tension increase)
- Sem-ena-werq becomes sharper (the hidden meaning more pointed)
- The audience responds more energetically

### Step 8: Close with cadence back to tonic
The azmari ends the song by returning to the opening riff or vamp, settling on the tonic of the mode, often with:
- A held note on the tonic (masenqo bowed drone)
- A krar plucked final chord
- A washint prolonged note fading to silence
- The final line of poetry lands the punchline or resolution of the "gold" layer

### Rules (Zero-Drift Constraints)

1. **Kiñit Pitch Constraint**: All melodic pitches (V1, V2) MUST belong to the pentatonic set of the chosen mode. Any accidental or chromatic note outside the set is forbidden unless it is a microtonal ornament (slide/bend) that resolves to a set member.

2. **Voice 0 Invariance**: The krar/masenqo ostinato cell (Voice 0) is the invariant identity anchor — it MUST repeat without pitch change throughout the piece. Mode identity depends on this cell being frozen.

3. **Call-and-Response Alternation**: V1 (vocal) and V2 (instrumental fill) must NEVER play simultaneously. V2 fills the gap left by V1's resting points. This is the hocket-like interlock of azmari form.

4. **Sem-Ena-Werq Mask**: The vocal melody (V1) must have a clear surface contour (wax) that supports the lyric. No purely virtuosic runs unconnected to the poetic line.

5. **Ornament Gradient**: Ornament density (slides, trills, bends on V1) must increase monotonically across sections: S0=none, S1=light, S2=medium, S3=heavy, S4=return to light.

6. **Microtonal Tolerance**: V1 is permitted microtonal offsets (neutral 2nds/3rds, quarter-tone bends on ♭5) that resolve to the pentatonic set pitches. The masenqo/krar timbre supports these without requiring equal temperament.

7. **Asymmetrical Phrasing**: Phrase lengths follow poetic meter (Amharic qəne), NOT fixed bar lengths. Section lengths are approximate targets, but the zero-drift invariant is maintained by padding or truncating at the section boundary.

8. **Cadence Convergence**: Every section must end with the vocal landing on the tonic of the kiñit mode. The final cadence (S4) must hold the tonic for ≥2 beats.

9. **No Functional Harmony**: No chord progressions. Harmony is emergent from the pentatonic set's vertical sonorities (open 4ths/5ths, clusters of 2nds). The krar drone provides the only harmonic reference (tonic + 5th).

10. **Density Progression**: Voices must enter monotonically (no voice drops out mid-piece except in S4 return). Once a voice enters, it stays until the cadence.

## Quirks & Pitfalls

- **Microtonal mismatch**: The kiñit modes on krar/masenqo use non-tempered tuning. Translating to MIDI (12-TET) loses the characteristic "neutral" intervals of Ambassel and the ♭5 bend of Tizita Minor. Consider using pitch-bend MIDI events or approximating with quarter-tone pitch sets.
- **No notation tradition**: Azmari composition is entirely oral. Any written representation is an approximation. The "real" piece exists only in performance.
- **Kiñit ≠ maqam**: Despite surface similarities to Turkish makam (seyir, microtones), the kiñit system is pentatonic (not heptatonic) and has no modulation convention. Each piece stays in one mode throughout.
- **Sem-ena-werq untranslatability**: The double-entendre poetry is deeply tied to Amharic language, social context, and local politics. In an instrumental realization, the "wax-and-gold" layer is absent — only the melodic contour remains.
- **Regional variation**: Gojjam, Wello, Shewa, and Gondar azmari traditions each shade the kiñit modes differently (pitch bends, preferred ornaments, tempo feel). A "standard" Tizita doesn't exist.
- **Kebero tuning**: Hand drums are tuned by fire-heating the skin before performance. This changes pitch mid-performance. Not reproducible in MIDI.
