# HC-077 — Maguindanao Kulintang (Palabunibunyan) Composition: Rhythmic-Mode Binding & Ornamental Improvisation over an Interlocking Gong-Chime Ensemble

**Method ID:** HC-077
**Layer:** `concrete (target when implemented)` — every element is note/stroke-level: the kulintang gong-row is a monophonic melody row with explicit ornament events (repetition, extension, insertion, suspension, variation, transposition); the babandil/dabakan rows are invariant pulse grids; the agung row is sparse bass punctuation. "Harmony" is absent by design — vertical sonorities are incidental gong decays, never chord syntax. Mode/style/occasion are cell attributes.
**Tradition:** Maguindanao (and Maranao, Tausug, Sama, Yakan, Dusun variants) gong-chime ensemble music, southern Philippines / Maritime Southeast Asia (Mindanao, Sulu, Sabah, Brunei, eastern Indonesia, Timor). Pre-Islamic archaic stratum of the Southeast Asian knobbed-gong culture; UNESCO-adjacent living heritage, actively revived since José Maceda's 1968 University of the Philippines program. Ensemble names: *basalen* / *palabunibunyan* (Maguindanao), *kolintang* (Maranao), *kulintangan* (Sulu/Sabah/Brunei).

## 1. What the method is

Kulintang music is the most developed tradition of the archaic Southeast Asian **gong-chime culture** (alongside Javanese gamelan and Thai piphat). The core instrument is a row of **5–9 graduated bossed gongs** laid horizontally on a frame (*antangan* = "to arrange" in Maguindanao; *pasangan* in Maranao), lowest gong on the player's left, struck on the bosses with two wooden beaters. Around it stands a small ensemble — the Maguindanao *palabunibunyan* — of five instruments:

| Instrument | Description | Ensemble role |
|---|---|---|
| **Kulintang** | 5–9 bossed gongs on a horizontal frame | Primary melody; *the conductor* of the ensemble |
| **Gandingan** | 4 large hanging bossed gongs (thin-rimmed, paired low/high) | Secondary melody; "talking gongs" (*apad* speech imitation); contest virtuosity (*kulndet*) |
| **Agung** | 2 large wide-rimmed suspended gongs | Bass; sparse phrase-end punctuation |
| **Babandil** | 1 narrow-rim gong struck on the rim with a stick | Timekeeper (pulse grid) |
| **Dabakan** | Single-headed goblet/hourglass/tubular drum, rattan sticks | Tempo support; roll/dampen/open strokes; starts pieces in modes where babandil is absent |

**The central insight:** kulintang music has **no fixed compositions** in the Western sense. It is organized by **rhythmic modes** — a musical unit that *binds the whole five-instrument ensemble together*. Each instrument plays its own rhythm for the mode; add the rhythms together and you get the music; change one rhythm and you get different music. The kulintang player takes the mode's theme and **"dresses it up"** with ornamentation — repetitions, extensions, insertions, suspensions, variations, transpositions — at her discretion. She is simultaneously the soloist and the conductor: she decides the length of each rendition, can change the rhythm, speed up or slow down. The other four instruments lock into the mode and follow.

So the human method is: **fix the mode → lay the timekeeping grid → lock the bass punctuation → set the support patterns → improvise the ornamented melody within the mode's parameters → open with old-style pieces, escalate to new-style virtuosity → perform for the occasion** (wedding, feast, ritual, contest, courtship, or long-distance *apad* conversation).

## 2. Why this is not an algorithm

- **The mode is a binding convention, not a generative rule.** Practitioners choose *sinulog* or *tidtu* for the occasion's affect; the choice is craft, and the labels even vary from household to household within one village.
- **Improvisation is mandatory.** The audience expects surprise; a player who merely imitates the previous player is judged *repetitious and mundane*. Ornamentation operates *within* the mode's parameters — freedom bounded by convention, exactly the human condition.
- **Tuning is per-set and non-standardized.** Every kulintang set has a unique pitch level, interval sizes and timbre (approximately pelog/slendro-like large/small intervals, not equal temperament). The composition lives in contour, not in absolute pitch.
- **No notation.** The repertoire is orally transmitted; cipher notation (gong numbers 1–8, lowest = 1) is a recent academic aid, not the tradition.
- **The music is situational.** The same mode serves feasts, trance healing (*tagonggo* + *sagayan* dance), courtship (a girl playing in the *lamin* chamber for suitors to hear), and long-distance village-to-village messages on the gandingan (*apad*). Social function is part of the method.

## 3. Craft procedure, step by step

### Step 0 — Learn the repertoire by ear (rote, no notation)
Apprentices absorb pieces and modes by sitting with masters and playing along; improvisation skill is acquired by imitating *patterns*, then learning to vary them. The gongs are tuned by ear (*tongkol* hammering from inside to raise, outside to lower) until the row "sounds like a melody contour I know."

### Step 1 — Fix the ensemble and the mode
Assemble the *palabunibunyan* (kulintang + gandingan + agung + babandil + dabakan; informal occasions may run reduced — 3 or 4 instruments, or kulintang solo). Choose the **rhythmic mode**:

- **Maguindanao genres:** *duyug*, *sinulog*, *tidtu*, *binalig*, *tagonggo*.
- **Maranao genres:** *kapromayas* (romayas), *kapagonor* (onor), *katitik pandai* (kapaginandang).
- **Sulu-type (Tausug/Samal/Yakan/Bajau/Dusun):** *kuriri*, *sinug*, *lubak-lubak* (Tausug); *tini-id*, *kuriri* (Yakan); *ayas*, *kudidi*, *tidung* (Dusun).

Modes differ by: number of beats in the recurring phrase, melodic/rhythmic grouping within the phrase, rhythmic emphasis, **opening formulas**, and **cadential patterns**. *Tagonggo* is special: ritualistic, used to accompany trance and the *sagayan* dance, unclassifiable as old/new.

### Step 2 — Choose the style register (old vs new)
- **Old style** — *kamamatuan* (Maguindanao) / *andung* (Maranao): slow, dignified, well-pronounced; moderate tempo, balanced, **few improvisations**; played by seniors and **always played first**, giving respect to the older generation.
- **New style** — *kagungudan* (Maguindanao) / *bago* (Maranao): fast, showy, highly rhythmic and pulsating; **highly improvised**, virtuosic, using formulae not found in old patterns; younger players perform these *after all kamamatuan pieces*.

### Step 3 — Lay the timekeeping grid
The **babandil** strikes its rim in a steady pulse that anchors the mode (on most modes — *sinulog*, *duyug* — it enters first). In **tidtu** the babandil is absent and the **dabakan starts the piece** instead. This grid is invariant for the rendition.

### Step 4 — Lock the tempo-support drum
The **dabakan** maintains tempo with quick muted strokes, using *dampening*, *roll*, and *open stroke* techniques (rattan sticks pivoted from the wrist). Its pattern is mode-specific. (Special case: in Maranao *Kasorondayong*, two dabakan players face each other playing an **interlocking rhythm**.)

### Step 5 — Set the bass punctuation
The **agung** (pair of large suspended gongs) plays low, long-decay strokes at structural points — phrase ends and cadential moments — never continuous. It is the ensemble's bass; its sparse placement defines the phrase lengths.

### Step 6 — Set the support/melodic-second layer
The **gandingan** (four hanging gongs whose interval pattern duplicates the four lowest kulintang gongs):
- **Old/formal ensemble role:** a *limited number of repeated patterns* that reinforce the mode's rhythm — a sonorous foundation (traditionally women's playing).
- **Contest role (*kulndet*):** highly dense, complicated rhythmic patterns; two expert players (*pagaganding*) alternate renditions in rotation; assistants (*mulits*) hold the gongs because the fast play would otherwise swing them.
- **Speech role (*apad*):** using only the three highest gongs, mimic the intonation of the Maguindanao language — the "talking gongs" — for long-distance messages, gossip, courtship ("*Singkaden Ka Singkaden*" = "come here"; "*Pagngapan ko seka*" = "I am waiting for you"), and warnings.

### Step 7 — Improvise the lead melody (the signature step)
On the **kulintang**, state the mode's theme, then dress it up at discretion with the ornament toolkit:
- **repetitions** (of segments),
- **extensions** (stretching a segment),
- **insertions** (extra material between segments),
- **suspensions** (holding a tone),
- **variations** (altering the segment),
- **transpositions** (shifting segments to other gongs of the row).

Rules of the game: stay within the mode's parameters (opening formula, phrase structure, cadential patterns); keep the theme recognizable; never merely copy the previous player. The kulintang player also *conducts*: she decides when the rendition ends and may change tempo mid-piece.

### Step 8 — Assemble the rendition/occasion arc
Order the pieces for the occasion: **kamamatuan first** (dignity, respect to elders), **kagungudan after** (youth, virtuosity, contests). Formal occasions follow traditional rules; informal family sessions ignore them. At Maguindanao weddings, solo gong contests (agung, gandingan, kulintang) showcase individual skill — a distinctive Maguindanao practice.

### Step 9 — Respect the social calendar
Kulintang is prohibited inside mosques, during Ramadan daytime (allowed at night after iftar), during mourning for an important person, at funerals, and at the peak of planting/harvest. It accompanies weddings, Hajj returnees, state functions, dignitary visits, healing rituals, and parades. Composition includes *knowing when not to play*.

## 4. Practitioner examples

| Practitioner / work | Role in the craft |
|---|---|
| **Aga Mayo Butocan** (Maguindanao kulintang master) | Worked with José Maceda at UP (1968); devised a cipher notation system for kulintang; published *Palabunibunyan*, a collection of Maguindanao pieces; emphasized the improvisational aspect of kulintang playing; authored the "Maguindanao Kulintang" Tao Music resource. |
| **José Maceda** (1917–2004, ethnomusicologist, UP) | Ushered in modern interest in kulintang (1968); *The Music of Maguindanao in the Philippines* (1963); *Gongs and Bamboo* (1998); formulated the contrast between gamelan's skeletal-tone/time-interval framework and kulintang's flexible, improvisation-rich framework. |
| **Master Danongan "Danny" Kalanduyan** (Maguindanao, U.S.-based) | Diaspora revival through teaching and performance with the Palabuniyan Kulintang Ensemble; documented instruments, repertoire, performance contexts, social functions (*Asian Music* 1996); key source on *apad* and gandingan roles. |
| **Usopay Cadar** (Maranao) | Analyzed Maranao *kolintang* music (instrument organization, ethnomusicology, history, 1971 dissertation; *Asian Music* 1996); with Robert Garfias studied formal variation principles in Maranao kolintang; diaspora teaching. |
| **Robert Garfias** (UC Irvine) | Brought Cadar and Kalanduyan to the U.S.; documented that the same melody heard on different kulintang sets keeps a common contour despite per-set tuning differences. |
| **Kristina Benitez** (University of Michigan, 2005) | *The Maguindanaon Kulintang: Musical Innovation, Transformation and the Concept of Binalig* — the definitive study of how *binalig* functions as both a mode name and a "new style" concept. |
| **Yoshitaka Terada** (1996) | *Variational and Improvisational Techniques of Gandingan Playing* — documents kulndet contests, pagaganding rotation, apad speech imitation. |
| **Steven Otto** (1996) | *Repertorial Nomenclature in Maranao Kolintang Music* — documents the fluidity of composition titles: labels vary household to household; modes/styles are continually revised in transmission. |
| **Philip Dominguez Mercurio** (PnoyAndTheCity / "A center for Kulintang") | Contemporary Filipino-American documentarian of the tradition and of gandingan talking-gong lore (Martial-law warning stories, courtship messages). |
| **Sulu-type ensembles (Tausug, Yakan, Sama, Dusun)** | Distinct craft: short melodic phrases *exchanged* between kulintangan and agung, imitating each other's rhythms rapidly — jousting as a game of skill (Tausug *sinug*, Yakan *tini-id*). |

**Listening:** "Sinulog a Kamamatuan" (Maguindanao, old style); "Tagunggo" (healing/trance mode for the *sagayan* dance); Palabuniyan Kulintang Ensemble recordings; Smithsonian Folkways *Music of Indonesia* series for the wider gong-chime region.

## 5. UnitMatrix integration (Musicom engine)

**Layer:** concrete. All cells are real events (gong strokes, drum strokes); tags ride on cells. No chord row exists — pentatonic melody + gong decays only.

**Voices (rows):**
- **V0 — Babandil (timekeeper).** Invariant pulse grid for the rendition; rim-struck narrow gong, one pitch class, constant subdivision. In *tidtu* mode: empty (dabakan takes the anchor role).
- **V1 — Dabakan (tempo support).** Mode-specific stroke pattern (open stroke / dampened / roll) on a single drum; for *Kasorondayong*, two interlocking dabakan rows.
- **V2 — Agung (bass punctuation).** Sparse low long-decay gong events at phrase ends and cadential points only; the phrase-length definer.
- **V3 — Gandingan (secondary melody / support).** Old-style: limited repeated mode-support patterns. Contest: dense *kulndet* patterns with per-voice event density caps. Speech (*apad*): 3-gong speech-tones row, events follow a Maguindanao intonation contour (metadata: text row).
- **V4 — Kulintang (lead melody + ornament).** Mode theme stated, then ornamented: repetitions / extensions / insertions / suspensions / variations / transpositions; the conductor row (rendition length, tempo control).
- **V5 (optional) — Ritual/vocal row.** *Sagayan* trance dancer (tagonggo): sparse high events or a tagged non-pitched row; *tunong* spirit-call moments.
- **V6 (metadata, non-sounding).** Mode tag, style gate (kamamatuan/kagungudan), occasion tag, tuning table (per-set pitch offsets), apad text when present.

**Sections (columns)** = one mode rendition each, grouped by occasion arc:

| Cell | Mode | Style gate | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| S0 | duyug (or romayas) | kamamatuan | opening; dignified, few ornaments |
| S1 | sinulog | kamamatuan | dance-entertainment; moderate |
| S2 | tidtu | either | babandil absent — dabakan opens |
| S3 | binalig | kagungudan | showpiece; dense ornament, virtuoso |
| S4 | tagonggo | ritual (unclassified) | trance/healing; sagayan; tunong calls |
| S5 | kulndet / apad (gandingan) | kagungudan | contest rotation or speech-conversation |

**Rules to encode:**
1. **Mode lock** — every section declares one rhythmic mode; per-instrument rhythm masks are mode-specific; no instrument may switch masks mid-section.
2. **Pulse invariance** — V0 (or V1 in tidtu) is a constant grid for the whole section; no tempo change without the V4 conductor's tagged decision.
3. **Agung punctuation** — V2 events only at phrase ends / cadences; max density ≈ 1 per phrase; never continuous.
4. **Ornament-within-parameters** — V4 must preserve the mode's opening formula, phrase length, and cadential pattern; allowed operators = {repetition, extension, insertion, suspension, variation, transposition}; identity of the theme must remain recognizable (≥N interval-skeleton features retained or rejected).
5. **Anti-imitation gate** — V4 may not equal the previous rendition's event sequence (audience rule: imitation = mundane).
6. **Style ladder** — kamamatuan sections precede kagungudan sections in an occasion arc; ornament density (operators per phrase) increases monotonically across the ladder.
7. **Tagonggo exception** — ritual mode is exempt from the style ladder and may appear in any position when tagged ritual.
8. **Pentatonic + per-set tuning** — V4/V3/V2 pitches come from the section's pentatonic row; render with per-set interval offsets (approx. pelog/slendro), documented, not forced into 12-TET.
9. **Registration** — V4 highest, V3 secondary, V2 lowest; no crossing of lead and bass registers.
10. **Zero-drift** — all rows end at the section terminal landmark; V0/V1 grids fill exactly; `validate()` must pass.

**Distinct in engine terms:** HC-002 (Balinese kotekan) is a *fixed* composed interlock with polos/sangsih hocket around a pokok; HC-077 has no hocket split and the lead voice *improvises within a mode*. HC-046 (Javanese gending) has a colotomic gong hierarchy and a balungan skeleton; HC-077's agung is punctuation, not a colotomic structure, and there is no skeletal cipher line. HC-005 (Ewe) shares the "invariant timeline + improvising master" idea but is purely rhythmic — HC-077 adds a *melodic* gong-chime lead with pitch ornamentation and a named-mode binding unit.
