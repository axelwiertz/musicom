## Description

Ragtime and stride piano represent the first distinctly African-American composed piano idioms, born from the fusion of European march form (with its multi-strain AABBACCDD structure) with West-African derived syncopation patterns. **Classic ragtime** (Scott Joplin, James Scott, Joseph Lamb, ~1897–1917) is notated, composed-on-paper music: the composer writes out every note. The left hand maintains a strict "oompah" pattern (single bass note/octave on beats 1 and 3, chord on beats 2 and 4) while the right hand places syncopated melodic figures off the beat. **Harlem stride piano** (James P. Johnson, Willie "The Lion" Smith, Fats Waller, Art Tatum, ~1920s–1940s) extends this: faster tempos, wider left-hand leaps (often spanning 10ths), more blues-inflected harmony (♭7, ♭3, ♭5 blue notes), improvisatory right-hand virtuosity, and the integration of swing rhythm.

The craft is profoundly pianistic — designed for the human hand — with physical constraints (hand span, finger independence, the mechanical impossibility of playing certain combinations) shaping every compositional decision.

## Craft Procedure (12-Step Method)

### Phase 1: Conception

1. **Choose key & key scheme** — Most classic rags start in a "bright" key (C, F, B♭, E♭, A♭) and the trio shifts up a 4th (Ⅳ) or 5th (Ⅴ). Scott Joplin's template: key climax pattern = A=tonic → B=dominant → A=tonic → Trio=subdominant → D=subdominant. Stride pianists often favour E♭, A♭, D♭ for richer resonance on acoustic piano.

2. **Fix tempo & meter** — Classic ragtime: 2/4 or 4/4, 80–120 BPM (Joplin: "ragtime should never be played fast"). Stride: up to 160–200 BPM for cutting contests. Marches are always felt in 2.

3. **Generate a motif seed** — A 2–4 bar rhythmic + melodic cell. Examples: Joplin's *The Entertainer* motif = E–C dyad with 3 sixteenth-note ante + held note; *Maple Leaf Rag* motif = arpeggio with rhythmic dotted-eighth-sixteenth pattern. The motif supplies unity across all strains.

### Phase 2: Left-Hand Architecture (The Engine Room)

4. **Compose the left-hand oompah pattern** — The non-negotiable mechanical foundation: **bass note/octave on beats 1 & 3, chord on beats 2 & 4** (in 4/4). The stride pianist's left hand "walks" (strides) an average distance of a 10th–12th between bass and chord. Variations:
   - *Standard oompah*: root octave (1,3) → root-5th chord (2,4)
   - *Walking tenths*: root on 1/3, 10th chord on 2/4 (characteristic of Johnson, Waller, Tatum)
   - *Broken oompah*: root on 1, 10th on 2, root inversions on 3/4
   - *Stride-ragtime hybrid*: single note on 1/3, 3- or 4-note chord on 2/4
   - *Pedal oompah*: repeated low octaves with chromatic approach tones

5. **Establish harmonic grid** — I–IV–V–I and ii–V–I progressions as the harmonic skeleton. Classic rag uses functional harmony with chromatic passing chords. Stride adds: ♭7 dominant extensions, diminished passing chords, blues iii–VI–ii–V turnarounds, and "barbershop" chromatic mediants. The harmonic rhythm is typically 2 beats per chord.

6. **Check left-hand playability** — The human hand can span at most a 10th (rarely an 11th). If the bass-to-chord interval exceeds a 10th, the pattern must be re-voiced (invert chord, use 1-handed arpeggiation, or split between bass and chord). Good ragtime = playable ragtime.

### Phase 3: Right-Hand Melody (The Syncopation)

7. **Write the A-strain melody against the left-hand grid** — Syncopation is achieved by placing melodic attacks on the **off-beats** (the "ands" of beats) and holding them across bar lines. Joplin's *School of Ragtime* (1908) demonstrated that ragtime syncopation = the pattern `♩. ♪ ♩.` or `♪ ♩. ♪` against the steady left hand. Core principle: the right hand must never play solid eighth notes in a march pattern — the "ragged" feel comes from irregular off-beat placement.

8. **Apply the three syncopation density zones**:
   - **Strain A** (opening): moderate syncopation, clearly stated motif, homophonic texture
   - **Strain B** (dominant key): denser syncopation, octaves, broken chords
   - **Trio/D strains** (subdominant): maximum syncopation, wide leaps, virtuosic runs

9. **Check melodic shape** — Ragtime melodies should have a visual/audible rise and fall shape not exceeding a 2-octave span per phrase. Avoid random leaps: melodic skips should outline chord tones or be followed by stepwise resolution in the opposite direction.

### Phase 4: Form & Assembly

10. **Arrange in multi-strain march form** — The classic ragtime blueprint: **Intro (2–4 bars, optional) → A (16 bars) → A (repeat) → B (16 bars, dominant key) → B (repeat) → A (8 or 16 bars, return) → Trio/C (16 bars, subdominant key) → Trio/C (repeat) → D (16 bars, same key or new modulation) → D (repeat)** = **AABBACCDD** pattern. Some rags use Intro–AA–BB–A–CC–DD (Joplin's *Maple Leaf Rag* format). Stride pieces often compress to AABB–CC or AABB–solos.

   16-bar strains divide as **8+8 antecedent/consequent** (period structure). The antecedent opens with the motif and pauses on a half cadence (V). The consequent resolves to tonic (I) with cadential rhyme matching the antecedent's tail.

11. **Engineer the trio transition** — The trio must feel like a fresh start: new key (usually subdominant), new tonal center, new rhythmic idea, but retaining motivic fragments from the A strain. Joplin often re-used the A-strain's last 4 bars as a ritornello between trio sections. In stride, the trio is where the pianist "opens up" with improvisatory invention while the left hand stays in oompah lock.

12. **Add two endings, dynamics, and performance markings** — Ragtime repeats are written-out (1st/2nd endings). Mark dynamics: A = *mf*, B = *f*, Trio = *p* (contrast), D = *ff*. Stride relies more on performance than notation; written dynamics are sparser.

## Practitioner Examples

### Classic Ragtime Composers
- **Scott Joplin (1868–1917)** — "King of Ragtime." *Maple Leaf Rag* (1899): perfect AABBACCDD form, E♭→A♭ scheme, three distinct motifs unified by rhythmic cell. *The Entertainer* (1902): highly symmetrical (every section half = same 4-bar phrase). *School of Ragtime* (1908): published exercise book codifying proper syncopation technique. *Magnetic Rag* (1914): his most harmonically advanced rag, with 14/10 internal strain asymmetry.
- **James Scott (1885–1938)** — *Frog Legs Rag* (1906): paradigmatic classic rag form. *Grace and Beauty* (1909): lyrical melodic writing. *Sunflower Slow Drag* (1901, with Joplin). Known for more chromatic harmony and elaborate bass lines.
- **Joseph Lamb (1887–1959)** — *American Beauty Rag* (1913): three-strain form with exceptional motivic coherence. *Ragtime Nightingale* (1914). Lamb was the only white composer accepted into the Joplin–Scott circle.
- **Artie Matthews** — *Pastime Rags No. 1–5* (1913–1917): advanced syncopation and harmonic complexity.

### Harlem Stride Pianists (transition to jazz)
- **James P. Johnson (1894–1955)** — "Father of Stride Piano." *Carolina Shout* (1921): the first recorded stride piano solo, modeled after the ring shout rhythm. *The Harlem Strut* (1917). *Charleston* (1923, from *Runnin' Wild*). Johnson integrated blues notes, swing eighth feel, and call-and-response between left and right hands.
- **Willie "The Lion" Smith (1897–1973)** — *Finger Buster* (1921): extremely fast stride. *Echoes of Spring* (1936). Known for dynamic contrasts, abrupt stops-and-starts, and "trick" piano effects.
- **Thomas "Fats" Waller (1904–1943)** — *Handful of Keys* (1929): stride tour-de-force. *Honeysuckle Rose* (1929). Bridged stride to swing. Waller's left hand walked 10ths in nearly every phrase; his right hand mixed syncopation with stride "stride-breaks" (two-handed upward runs).
- **Art Tatum (1909–1956)** — *Tiger Rag* (1932), *Body and Soul* (1940): pushed stride to its harmonic and virtuosic extreme. Tatum's left hand used reharmonized stride (altered dominants, tritone substitutions, diminished chains); his right hand played arpeggiated cascades at extreme tempo. He expanded the left-hand span to incorporate 10th–12th reaches with rolled chords, and his stride patterns often displaced the chord by a 16th-note.

## UnitMatrix Integration

### Voices

| Voice | Role | Instrument (MIDI) | Craft Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Voice 0** | Left-hand bass spine (oompah foundation) | Acoustic Grand Piano (ch 0, program 0) | Invariant bass/chord alternation on beats 1–2–3–4. The oompah pattern never stops, never changes its basic shape. It is the mechanical engine. |
| **Voice 1** | Right-hand melody (syncopated lead) | Acoustic Grand Piano (ch 0, program 0) | All melodic material: motif statement, syncopated figures, runs, breaks. Plays exclusively in the right hand's register (C4–C7). |
| **Voice 2** | Right-hand harmony (chordal filler / break layer) | Acoustic Grand Piano (ch 0, program 0) | Second right-hand layer occurring in stride (Waller/Tatum): two-handed chords, arpeggiated break figues, tremolos. Typically an octave below Voice 1, filling the gap between V0's chord and V1's melody. |
| **Voice 3** | Bass extension (left-hand 10th/walking tenth layer) | Acoustic Grand Piano (ch 0, program 0) | Variant left-hand pattern: instead of single notes on 1/3, uses 10th intervals. This is optional — applied in stride but not in classic ragtime. When active, replaces V0's single-note bass with a richer interval. |
| **Voice 4** | Cut-time percussion pulse | Side Stick / Snare (ch 9, percussion) | Optional reinforcing pulse: accents on beats 2 and 4 (the backbeat), matching the stride left-hand chord on those beats. Keeps the groove locked during cutting contests. |

### Sections

| Section | Bars | Key | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Intro** (optional) | 0–4 | Tonic | Establish tempo, set motif, rake chord: V7→I. Rare in classic ragtime, more common in stride (Johnson's *Carolina Shout* opens with a 2-bar raked chord). |
| **A** | 1–16 | Tonic (I) | Main theme, stated twice (repeat). 8+8 antecedent/consequent. Moderate syncopation, clear motif presentation. |
| **B** | 17–32 | Dominant (V) | Contrasting theme, repeated. Denser syncopation, octave melody, upper register climax just before cadence. |
| **A** (reprise) | 33–40 (or 33–48) | Tonic (I) | Brief return of A (8 bars usually, not full 16) leading into the trio. |
| **Trio/C** | 41–56 | Subdominant (IV) | New key, fresh thematic material. Often the "musical heart" of the rag. Retains motivic fragments from A. In stride, the trio is where improvisation flourishes. |
| **D** (optional, often = B material in new key) | 57–72 | Subdominant (IV) | Final strain, repeated. Most virtuosic, highest register, densest syncopation. Often recycles B's melodic shape with IV-key harmony. |
| **Coda** (optional) | 73–80 | Tonic (I) | Closing tag: ii–V–I chain, register rise, final tonic chord with fermata. Common in stride; Joplin's rags typically end on D's repeat. |

### Rules

1. **Oompah invariance**: Voice 0 plays bass note/octave on beats 1 & 3, chord on beats 2 & 4, every bar, never stopping. No rests. No syncopation. (Constraint: `[onset ∈ {0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5} bar] ∧ [pitch ∈ {root,5th} on beats 1,3] ∧ [pitch ∈ {chord tones} on beats 2,4]`)
2. **Hand-span filter**: No bass-to-chord interval > 10th (20 semitones). If the harmonic plan requires a wider reach, voice must invert or roll the chord. (Constraint: `V0.chord_pitches - V0.bass_pitch ≤ 20` for any single-hand span)
3. **Syncopation density gradient per strain**: A (density=0.3, moderate off-beats) → B (density=0.5, more off-beat, more octaves) → Trio (density=0.4, contrasting quieter) → D (density=0.7, max syncopation). Measured as proportion of right-hand notes shifted from beat-grid. (Constraint: `V1.syncopation_ratio ∈ [0.2, 0.7]`; Joplin's *School of Ragtime* rule: `pattern MUST entail at least one tied note across a beat`.)
4. **Strain period division**: Each strain = 16 bars split 8+8 (antecedent→half cadence V, consequent→full cadence I). The two 8-bar halves share the same melodic tail (cadential rhyme). (Constraint: `bars[1..8].last2bars ∈ {V(7), IV(6)} ∧ bars[9..16].last2bars ∈ {I(1), V(5)}`)
5. **Key modulation plan**: A = tonic (I) → B = dominant (V) or relative minor (vi) → A return = tonic → Trio = subdominant (IV) → D = subdominant or V/IV (dominant of subdominant). Modulations are abrupt (no developmental bridge), cued by double-bar.
6. **Motivic coherence**: At least ONE motif from the A strain must reappear transformed in the Trio (inversion, augmentation, rhythmic diminution, or interval expansion). This ensures the rag sounds unified rather than like 4 separate pieces.
7. **No functional parallel**: Every strain must be harmonically self-contained (no modulations inside a strain). The strain must resolve to its local tonic before the repeat.
8. **Playability validation**: Every written span must be playable by human hands. Chords requiring > 10th span across both hands concurrently = illegal. Decompose into rolled chords or re-voice.
9. **Stride variant** (when `is_stride = True`): Replace single-note bass on beats 1/3 with 10th intervals. Add walking 10th bass motion (chromatic/diatonic approach to next root). Right hand may improvise ornamentation on repeats (each pass = different syncopation surface over frozen harmonic grid). Tempo ≥ 140 BPM.

### Quirks & Pitfalls

- **The ragtime paradox**: The left hand MUST be metronomic (stiff, march-like, no rubato) while the right hand plays "ragged" (off-beat, flexible, anticipatory). Beginners often let left hand speed up with right-hand syncopation — this destroys the feel. The left hand is a human drum machine.
- **Readability trap**: Ragtime notation with multiple beam-break syncopation patterns is notoriously hard to read. Joplin notated exact syncopation but later composers (Bolcom, Edwards) recommend writing syncopation with tenuto/tie marks to make sight-reading easier.
- **The "not a rag" risk**: If all 3 strains in the same key = it's a march, not a rag. If syncopation is absent = cakewalk/march. If syncopation is EVERY beat = feels like mechanical stagger, not syncopation. Bill Edwards' rule: ~30–50% of RH events should be off-beat; 0% or >70% both sound wrong.
- **Hand stretch injury**: Stride pianists (Waller, Tatum) frequently destroyed their left hands practicing 10th leaps. The 10th in C major (C–E) is the maximum safe static span; 10th on black keys (B♭–D, A♭–C) is tighter. Compose within safe human limits unless writing for a specific giant.
- **Cutting contest trap**: In stride cutting contests, pianists often sacrificed melodic coherence for speed. Johnson's *Carolina Shout* works because it swings AND has clear motivic logic. Speed alone is empty.
